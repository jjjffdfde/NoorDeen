The History & Theological Structure of the Hijri Calendar

How Caliph Umar (RA) established the Islamic dating epoch and how the lunar cycle governs Muslim worship.

The Establishment of the Calendar by Caliph Umar (RA)

During the reign of the second Rightly Guided Caliph, Umar ibn al-Khattab (RA), around the year 16 or 17 AH, the expanding Islamic state encountered administrative challenges because official letters, fiscal documents, and debt contracts were dated simply by the month without specifying the year. Caliph Umar assembled the senior Sahaba to institute a standardized dating epoch for the Muslim world.

Various proposals were deliberated: dating from the birth of the Prophet ﷺ, dating from the commencement of revelation in the Cave of Hira, or dating from his passing. Ali ibn Abi Talib (RA) proposed dating from the Hijrah—the epic migration from Makkah to Madinah in 622 CE. Ali (RA) reasoned that the Hijrah was the pivotal turning point that separated truth from falsehood, transformed an oppressed community into an independent sovereign civilization, and laid the foundations of Islamic law and social justice. Caliph Umar unanimously endorsed this decision, and Muharram was selected as the first month of the new calendar year.

The Four Sacred Months (Al-Ashhur al-Hurum)

The Holy Quran establishes the sanctity of four specific lunar months in Surah At-Tawbah (9:36):

The Prophet ﷺ clarified in his Farewell Pilgrimage (Sahih al-Bukhari #3197): "Time has completed its cycle and is as it was on the day Allah created the heavens and the earth. The year is twelve months, of which four are sacred: three are consecutive—Dhul-Qa'dah, Dhul-Hijjah, and Muharram—and Rajab of Mudar, which is between Jumada and Sha'ban."

During these four months, committing sins is magnified in gravity, while righteousness, voluntary fasting, and almsgiving receive multiplied heavenly reward.

Moon Sighting (Ru'yah) versus Astronomical Calculation (Hisab)

A central subject of contemporary Islamic jurisprudence is determining the onset of the lunar months of Ramadan and Shawwal (Eid al-Fitr):

  • Physical Sighting (Ru'yah): The classical majority position relies on the prophetic command: "Fast when you see it and break your fast when you see it, and if it is obscured, then complete thirty days of Sha'ban." (Sahih al-Bukhari #1909).
  • Astronomical Calculation (Hisab): Many contemporary councils (such as the Fiqh Council of North America and the European Council for Fatwa and Research) utilize astronomical calculations to determine the exact moment of the lunar conjunction (birth of the new moon) and its visibility coordinates, providing advance certainty for civic planning.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Islamic Calendar

Why did Caliph Umar (RA) choose the Hijrah as the starting year?

Ali ibn Abi Talib (RA) suggested the Hijrah because it marked the profound transition when Muslims established an independent society and separated truth from falsehood, selecting it over the Prophet's birth or death.

Why does an Islamic day begin at sunset (Maghrib)?

Because the Islamic calendar is lunar, each new lunar month is inaugurated by sighting the crescent moon in the western sky immediately after sunset. Consequently, every Islamic day begins at Maghrib.

How many days are in a Hijri year compared to a Gregorian year?

A lunar year consists of 12 lunar months of either 29 or 30 days, totaling approximately 354 or 355 days. This makes the Hijri year approximately 10 to 11 days shorter than the 365-day Gregorian solar year, causing Islamic dates to rotate backward through the seasonal cycle every 33 years.
