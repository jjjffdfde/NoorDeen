Understanding the 99 Names of Allah

NoorDeen Editorial · 11 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The "99 Names of Allah" is one of the most popular topics in Islamic teaching, and one of the most frequently misunderstood. The phrase comes from an authentic hadith, but the famous list of ninety-nine is a scholarly compilation, not a list given in the Qur'an. This article explains what the names are, what the hadith actually says, how the names relate to Islamic theology, and how to study them without falling into either literalism or speculation.

It is written from a mainstream Sunni perspective and notes where scholars differ.

Key takeaways
  • Allah's names are revealed descriptions of His perfection, not labels we invent.
  • The hadith promises a great reward for "memorising" 99 names, but the number is not a limit on Allah's names.
  • The famous enumerated list is compiled from Qur'an and hadith, chiefly through al-Tirmidhi.
  • Names are studied through Tawhid al-Asma' wa al-Sifat: affirming them without likening Allah to creation.
  • True memorisation means understanding, worshipping through the name, and acting on its meaning.

1. What "Names" Means in Islamic Theology

In Arabic, ism (name) is not merely a label. A name discloses something about the named. Allah's names are therefore descriptions of His perfect attributes: He is ar-Rahman (the Most Merciful), al-'Alim (the All-Knowing), al-Qadir (the All-Powerful). They are not separate deities, and they are not metaphors with no content.

“And to Allah belong the best names, so invoke Him by them. And leave those who practice deviation concerning His names — they will be recompensed for what they have been doing.”
Qur'an 7:180

The Qur'an also says:

“He is Allah, the Creator, the Inventor, the Fashioner; to Him belong the best names. Whatever is in the heavens and earth is exalting Him. And He is the Exalted in Might, the Wise.”
Qur'an 59:24

The names are therefore both a matter of knowledge and a matter of worship. To know that Allah is al-Ghafur is to turn to Him for forgiveness; to know that He is al-'Adl is to act justly.

2. The Hadith of the Ninety-Nine Names

The Prophet ﷺ said: “Allah has ninety-nine names, one hundred less one. Whoever memorises them will enter Paradise.” The hadith is narrated in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, though the wording and chains vary.

The hadith does not say that Allah has only ninety-nine names. It says that Allah has ninety-nine names with a specific virtue attached. The distinction is crucial. Other authentic reports indicate that Allah has names He has not revealed to creation.

The famous list of ninety-nine names is most commonly taken from a narration of al-Tirmidhi, who compiled it and graded the hadith hasan. Scholars differ on the authenticity of the full list and on the exact enumeration. Some names in the list are derived from Qur'anic descriptions rather than direct name-forms.

Honest caveat: There is no single, universally agreed, Qur'an-only list of ninety-nine names. Different scholars produce slightly different lists. NoorDeen does not present one list as the only valid one.

3. Are There Only Ninety-Nine Names?

No. The number ninety-nine is not a limit. The Prophet ﷺ taught a supplication in which he says: “I ask You by every name You have named Yourself, or revealed in Your Book, or taught to any of Your creation, or kept hidden in the knowledge of the unseen with You...” (narrated in Musnad Ahmad; graded authentic by scholars).

This indicates that Allah has names beyond what has been revealed. The ninety-nine are a known, accessible set with a promised reward, not an exhaustive inventory.

The practical implication is humility: we worship Allah as He has described Himself, and we do not claim to encompass Him with our categories.

4. Categories of Names: Beauty and Majesty

Scholars often divide the names into two broad families:

  • Names of beauty (jamal): mercy, love, generosity, forgiveness, kindness — such as ar-Rahman, ar-Rahim, al-Wadud, al-Ghafur, al-Karim.
  • Names of majesty (jalal): power, sovereignty, justice, greatness, wrath — such as al-Jabbar, al-Qahhar, al-'Aziz, al-Muntaqim, al-Malik.

A balanced understanding holds both together. A God who is only merciful becomes permissive; a God who is only severe becomes terrifying. The Qur'an consistently pairs mercy with justice, and hope with fear.

“Declare, "O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful."”
Qur'an 39:53

The names are also a remedy for spiritual imbalance. A person consumed by guilt should meditate on al-Ghafur; a person consumed by arrogance should meditate on al-Kabir and al-Jabbar.

5. Tawhid al-Asma' wa al-Sifat: Affirming Without Likening

Sunni theology affirms Allah's names and attributes as He revealed them, without distorting their meaning, denying them, asking how they are, or likening them to creation. The Qur'an states the principle:

“There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing.”
Qur'an 42:11

This verse combines negation and affirmation: Allah is not like anything, and He is Hearing and Seeing. The attributes are real, but their modality is beyond human comprehension.

Two errors are rejected. The first is tashbih — likening Allah to creation, imagining Him with a body or human emotions in the created sense. The second is ta'til — stripping the names of meaning, reducing them to empty metaphors. The middle path affirms the name and denies the likeness.

This is not abstract theology. It shapes how a Muslim reads verses about Allah's "hand", "face" or "throne": with affirmation of what Allah intended, and with the conviction that He is unlike creation.

6. What "Memorising" the Names Actually Means

The hadith's reward is attached to ihsa', often translated as memorising or counting. Scholars explain that it includes several levels:

  • Learning the names and their correct meanings.
  • Understanding them without distortion or likening.
  • Worshipping Allah through them — invoking the appropriate name in supplication.
  • Acting on their implications — being merciful because Allah is Merciful, just because He is Just.
  • Living with awareness of Allah's presence and attributes.

Rote repetition without understanding is a beginning, not the goal. The names are meant to transform the heart, not just fill a checklist.

7. Invoking Allah by His Names

The Qur'an commands us to call upon Allah by His names. This is one of the most intimate forms of supplication:

“And to Allah belong the best names, so invoke Him by them.”
Qur'an 7:180

Examples from the Sunnah include: “Ya Rahman, Ya Rahim” for mercy; “Ya Ghafur, Ya Rahim” for forgiveness; “Ya Hayyu Ya Qayyum” for relief in distress; “Ya Latif” for subtle kindness.

The name should match the need. Asking for forgiveness with al-Ghafur, for provision with ar-Razzaq, for protection with al-Hafiz, for guidance with al-Hadi. This is not manipulation of God; it is approaching Him through the attributes He has revealed.

8. Selected Names Explained

Ar-Rahman and Ar-Rahim

Both come from the root r-h-m, mercy. Ar-Rahman describes Allah's vast, all-encompassing mercy; ar-Rahim describes His special mercy toward believers. The Basmalah opens with both.

Al-Malik

The Sovereign. All dominion belongs to Allah; human kingship is delegated and temporary. This name relativises every earthly power.

As-Salam

The Source of Peace and Perfection, free from every defect. It is also the root of the greeting salam.

Al-Khaliq, Al-Bari', Al-Musawwir

The Creator, the Originator, the Fashioner. These three names appear together in 59:24 and describe the stages of creation: decree, bringing into being, and shaping.

Al-Ghafur and Al-'Afuww

The Forgiving and the Pardoner. Al-'Afuww is the name taught in the Laylat al-Qadr supplication; it suggests erasing sin entirely, not merely covering it.

Al-Wadud

The Loving. Allah's love is real and selective: He loves the believers, the patient, the just, the purified. It is not a vague universal sentiment that ignores conduct.

Al-Hayy Al-Qayyum

The Ever-Living, the Sustainer of all existence. These two names are central in the verse of the Throne (2:255) and in the Prophet's ﷺ supplication for distress.

Al-Ahad and As-Samad

The Unique and the Eternal Refuge. Surah al-Ikhlas is built on these names: Allah is one, indivisible, and everything depends on Him while He depends on nothing.

9. Common Errors to Avoid

  • Inventing names: Allah is named only by what He or His Prophet ﷺ revealed. We do not derive new divine names from personal preference.
  • Claiming the list is exhaustive: the ninety-nine are not the limit of Allah's names.
  • Treating names as magical formulas: reciting a name a set number of times for guaranteed worldly results is not established in the Sunnah.
  • Likening Allah to creation: affirming attributes does not mean imagining a body or human psychology.
  • Denying meaning: reducing the names to empty symbols is also an error.
  • Using the names to justify injustice: "Allah is forgiving" does not cancel the obligation to repay debts or seek justice.

The names should produce humility, not certainty about God's inner nature. The more one knows, the more one realises how little one knows.

10. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Are the 99 names listed in the Qur'an?

No. The Qur'an contains many divine names, but it does not provide a single list of ninety-nine. The famous list is a compilation from Qur'an and hadith, chiefly through al-Tirmidhi, and scholars differ on its exact contents.

2. Does Allah have more than 99 names?

Yes. The hadith indicates that ninety-nine names have a specific virtue, not that Allah's names are limited to that number. Other authentic reports mention names kept hidden from creation.

3. Can we call Allah "God" in English?

Yes, "God" is the English word for the deity, and Arabic-speaking Christians also use "Allah". However, "Allah" is the proper name used in the Qur'an and carries theological precision. In English writing, "Allah" is often preferred to avoid confusion.

4. Is using prayer beads for dhikr an innovation?

Scholars differ. Many permit beads as a permissible means of counting, citing the practice of some companions and the general permissibility of aids. Others prefer counting on the fingers, which is also established. The means is not the core issue; sincerity and consistency are.

5. How should a beginner start learning the names?

Learn ten names with their meanings and one practical implication each. Use them in supplication. Study the verses where they appear. Avoid memorising the full list without understanding, and avoid debates about the exact enumeration until the basics are secure.
