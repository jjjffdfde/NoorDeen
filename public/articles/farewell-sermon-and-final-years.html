The Farewell Sermon and the Final Years

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

In the tenth year after the Hijrah, the Prophet ﷺ performed his only Hajj after the Hijrah and addressed the largest gathering of Muslims he had ever led. The speech we call the farewell sermon is short, but it summarises the moral revolution of his mission: the sanctity of life, property and honour; the abolition of usury; the rights of women; the equality of believers; and the command to hold fast to the Book of Allah.

This article sets the sermon in its context, examines what the sound sources actually report, and follows the Prophet ﷺ through the final months to his death and burial. It also addresses the transmission problem: the “last sermon” that circulates online is often a composite paraphrase, and some of its most quoted lines are not from the most reliable reports. Honesty requires distinguishing the core from the embellishment.

Key takeaways
  • The farewell Hajj took place in the tenth year after the Hijrah, conventionally 632 CE.
  • The sermon was delivered at Arafah, with some reports placing the address at Mina; the core content is well attested.
  • It declared life, property and honour inviolable, abolished usury, and commanded fair treatment of women.
  • The “Book and Sunnah” version is found in some reports; the “Book of Allah” version is the most securely attested.
  • The equality passage (“no superiority of Arab over non-Arab”) is reported in later collections and graded variously.
  • The Prophet ﷺ died in Rabi al-Awwal of the eleventh year, in Aisha’s room, and was buried there.
  • The succession was resolved at the Saqifah of Banu Sa’idah; Sunnis and Shi’a interpret the events differently.

1. The Farewell Pilgrimage

The Prophet ﷺ announced his intention to perform Hajj, and Muslims gathered in Madinah from across Arabia. The number of pilgrims is given in the sources as tens of thousands, though the figures vary. He performed the rites publicly so that the community could learn them from him, and he said, “Take your rites from me, for I do not know whether I will perform Hajj after this year.” The report is in Muslim.

The pilgrimage was therefore both worship and instruction. The Prophet ﷺ demonstrated the stations, the standing at Arafah, the sacrifices and the circumambulation. He also addressed the people at Arafah on the ninth of Dhul-Hijjah, and the sermon is associated with that day. Some reports place a further address at Mina on the tenth or the days after. The sources do not give a single verbatim transcript; they give several reports with overlapping content.

The Qur’an is reported to have been revealed at this time with the verse, “This day I have perfected for you your religion and completed My favour upon you and have approved for you Islam as religion” (5:3). Umar is reported to have wept when he heard it, understanding that the completion of the religion signalled the approaching end of the Prophet’s ﷺ life. The report is in Bukhari and Muslim.

2. The Sermon: Setting and Transmission

The farewell sermon is preserved in several reports, the fullest being the hadith of Jabir ibn Abdullah in Sahih Muslim describing the Hajj. Other versions appear in Bukhari, Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa’i, Ibn Majah and the Musnad of Ahmad. They agree on the main themes but differ in wording and order, which is normal for a sermon transmitted by multiple listeners.

The most widely circulated modern text — beginning “O People, lend me an attentive ear, for I know not whether after this year I shall ever be amongst you again” — is a composite. It blends lines from different reports and adds material that is not in the earliest collections. That does not make the whole text false, but it means that a quotation should be checked against a specific report rather than repeated as if it were a single authenticated transcript.

This article therefore quotes only lines that are securely attested in the major collections, and it labels the rest. The core of the sermon is not in doubt: the Prophet ﷺ declared the sanctity of life, property and honour, abolished usury, commanded justice towards women, affirmed the equality of believers and asked the people to bear witness that he had conveyed the message.

Transmission caution: the popular “Last Sermon” text is a harmonised composite. Its themes are authentic, but it is not a verbatim transcript from a single sound report. Quotations should be attributed to a specific collection where possible.

3. The Sanctity of Life, Property and Honour

The sermon begins with a declaration that echoes the sanctity of the sacred month and the sacred city. The Prophet ﷺ said that the lives, property and honour of the believers are sacred to one another, and that they will meet their Lord and be questioned about their deeds. The report is in Muslim and Bukhari in slightly different wordings.

“Your lives, your property and your honour are sacred to one another, like the sanctity of this day of yours, in this month of yours, in this city of yours.”
Sahih Muslim, farewell sermon report of Jabir

The comparison is deliberate. The Arabs revered the sacred month and the sacred territory; the sermon extends that reverence to the person, property and reputation of every believer. It is a radical move: the ordinary Muslim’s life and dignity are placed on the same level as the sanctuary itself.

The Prophet ﷺ then said that he had conveyed the message and asked the people to witness it. The sermon ends with a repeated invocation, “O Allah, bear witness.” The structure is that of a covenant: the community is entrusted with what it has heard, and it is made responsible for transmitting it. The sermon is therefore not only a farewell but a commissioning.

4. Usury and Economic Justice

The sermon declares that all riba is abolished, and that the first riba to be abolished is that of Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib, the Prophet’s uncle. The report is in Muslim. This was a concrete act, not a general principle: a specific debt owed to a specific man was cancelled as a public example.

The Qur’an had already condemned riba in the strongest terms, warning of war from Allah and His Messenger against those who persist in it (2:279). The Madinan market had been reformed by the prohibition of fraudulent weighing, hoarding and unjust exchange. The farewell sermon sealed the reform by making the abolition of usury part of the final testament.

The economic teaching of the sermon is not limited to riba. It also assumes the sanctity of property: no one may take another’s wealth without lawful consent. The Prophet ﷺ had said in the same period that a Muslim’s property is not lawful for another except with his consent. The combination is important: the sermon protects property and simultaneously abolishes a form of profit that the Qur’an regarded as exploitative.

5. The Rights and Dignity of Women

The sermon contains a well-known passage on women: “Fear Allah concerning women, for you have taken them by the trust of Allah and made their private parts lawful by the word of Allah.” The Prophet ﷺ explained that husbands have rights over wives and wives have rights over husbands, and he commanded kindness and provision. The report is in Muslim.

The passage is often quoted in isolation, but its context matters. It comes within a sermon about the sanctity of life and property, and it treats the marital relationship as a trust (amanah) rather than a possession. The Qur’an elsewhere commands men to live with women in kindness (4:19), grants women inheritance, property and the right to consent to marriage, and condemns the harsh treatment of wives (4:19–21; 2:228–233).

The sermon did not create a fully egalitarian society, and it would be dishonest to claim that it did. It set a standard of reciprocal rights and dignity that the community was to live up to, and the gap between the standard and the practice has varied across Muslim societies. The honest reading holds both: the sermon is a strong statement of women’s rights, and history shows that those rights were not always honoured.

6. Equality and the End of Tribal Superiority

The most quoted equality passage is not in the earliest collections in a single sound report. It is transmitted in the Musnad of Ahmad and in the collections of al-Bayhaqi and others, with chains that scholars have graded variously, often as hasan or acceptable in meaning. Its content is consistent with the Qur’an:

“O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you.”
Qur’an 49:13

The reported sermon line — “O people, your Lord is one and your father is one; there is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a white over a black, nor of a black over a white, except by piety” — expresses the same principle. Even where the chain is debated, the meaning is firmly established by the Qur’an and by the Prophet’s ﷺ practice.

The sermon did not abolish slavery in a single sentence, and it did not create a classless society. It did something more specific: it removed lineage and race as grounds of superiority and made piety the criterion. That principle would be invoked repeatedly in later Islamic history, and it would be violated repeatedly as well. The sermon remains a standard by which those violations are judged.

7. “Have I Conveyed the Message?”

The sermon closes with a question. The Prophet ﷺ asked the people whether he had conveyed the message, and they replied that he had. He then raised his finger to the sky and said, “O Allah, bear witness,” three times. The report is in Muslim.

The gesture is a legal and spiritual act. The Prophet ﷺ is not asking for applause; he is calling Allah to witness that the trust has been delivered. The community is now the bearer of the message, and the sermon is the moment at which responsibility passes to it. The Qur’an describes the Prophet ﷺ as a warner and a bringer of good tidings, and it insists that his duty is to convey, not to compel (2:256; 88:21–22).

The question also has a backward look. The Prophet ﷺ had begun his mission with a small circle in Makkah and had been rejected, tortured and expelled. At Arafah he stood before tens of thousands who had accepted the message. The sermon is the public completion of that arc, and the answer of the people is the community’s acceptance of the trust.

8. The Final Months: Tabuk, Delegations and Illness

The farewell Hajj took place in the tenth year. In the ninth year the Prophet ﷺ had led the expedition to Tabuk, the farthest northern campaign, which ended without a battle but consolidated the northern frontier and tested the community’s resolve in a year of drought and hardship. The Qur’an addresses the reluctance of some believers to march (9:38–57) and praises those who gave generously.

The ninth year is also called the Year of Delegations, because tribes from across Arabia sent representatives to Madinah to accept Islam or to conclude treaties. The community had become the dominant power in the peninsula, and the Prophet ﷺ spent much of his time teaching, receiving delegations and appointing governors and teachers.

In the eleventh year he fell ill. The illness began with a headache and fever, and it worsened over several days. He continued to lead the prayer as long as he could, then asked Abu Bakr to lead it in his place. The report is in Bukhari and Muslim and is one of the indications that the community understood about the succession. He also ordered the expedition of Usama ibn Zayd to be prepared, and he urged the people to treat the Ansar well.

9. The Death of the Prophet ﷺ

The Prophet ﷺ died on a Monday in Rabi al-Awwal of the eleventh year after the Hijrah, conventionally 632 CE. The exact day is given as the twelfth in the most common reports, with some saying the first or the second. He died in the room of Aisha, his wife, with his head resting against her. She reported that he was in pain and that he asked for water, and that his final words included the command to guard the prayer and to treat those whom their right hands possessed with kindness.

The report of the final words is in Abu Dawud and Ibn Majah, and its chain has been discussed by scholars; it is not at the highest level of authenticity. What is certain is that the Prophet ﷺ remained concerned with prayer and with the vulnerable until the end. He also said, according to Bukhari, “Allah curses the Jews and the Christians who took the graves of their prophets as places of worship,” warning against the divinisation of graves.

When he died, the community was stunned. Umar denied it. Abu Bakr entered, uncovered the face of the Prophet ﷺ, kissed his forehead and said, “You are more precious than my father and mother.” He then went out and addressed the people: “Whoever worshipped Muhammad, Muhammad has died. Whoever worshipped Allah, Allah is alive and does not die.” He recited, “Muhammad is not but a messenger; messengers have passed before him” (3:144). The report is in Bukhari.

The Prophet ﷺ was washed, shrouded and buried in Aisha’s room, where he died, according to the report that prophets are buried where they die. The room later became part of the mosque of Madinah. The burial was simple, without a monumental tomb, in accordance with his teaching.

10. The Succession and the Community After Him

The Prophet ﷺ did not leave a written designation of a successor that was accepted by all. After his death, the companions gathered at the Saqifah of Banu Sa’idah, where the Ansar and the Muhajirun debated the leadership. Abu Bakr was chosen as the first caliph, and the majority of the community gave him allegiance. The decision was urgent because the community faced the threat of fragmentation and apostasy.

The question of succession is one of the great divisions of Islamic history. Sunnis hold that the Prophet ﷺ did not explicitly appoint a successor and that the community’s choice of Abu Bakr was legitimate. Shi’a hold that Ali ibn Abi Talib was the divinely appointed imam and that the leadership belonged to the Prophet’s family. This article does not adjudicate the theological dispute; it records that the disagreement is old, serious and rooted in different readings of the sources.

On the return journey from the farewell Hajj, at a place called Ghadir Khumm, the Prophet ﷺ addressed the people and spoke about the Ahl al-Bayt, his family. The report is found in several collections and its interpretation is disputed. Some see it as a designation of Ali; others see it as a general exhortation to honour the Prophet’s family. The difference is part of the same larger question.

What is not disputed is the achievement. Within a generation, the community had moved from a persecuted minority in Makkah to a polity that would carry the message across three continents. The farewell sermon is the moral charter of that transition, and the death of the Prophet ﷺ is the moment at which the community had to learn to live without his physical presence.

11. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for the farewell sermon and the final years are:

  • The Qur’an — especially 3:144, 5:3, 9:38–57, 49:13 and 88:21–22.
  • Sahih Muslim — the fullest report of the farewell Hajj and the sermon of Jabir.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari — the farewell pilgrimage, the death of the Prophet ﷺ, Abu Bakr’s address and the burial.
  • Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa’i and Ibn Majah — additional reports on the sermon and the final words, with varying grades.
  • The Musnad of Ahmad and the collections of al-Bayhaqi — the equality passage and other sermon variants.
  • Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah — the chronology of the final years, the illness and the death.

The sermon is transmitted in several versions, and the popular composite text should not be treated as a single authenticated transcript. The exact date of the death, the details of the final illness and the words of the final moments are reported with variation. The article follows the most widely accepted chronology and marks the differences where they matter.

Editorial note: quotations are attributed to the collection in which they are reported. Where a report is graded weak or hasan, the article says so. The succession dispute is described, not adjudicated.

12. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. When was the farewell sermon delivered?

It was delivered during the farewell Hajj in the tenth year after the Hijrah, conventionally 632 CE, at Arafah on the ninth of Dhul-Hijjah. Some reports place a further address at Mina. The sources give several versions rather than one verbatim transcript.

2. Is the popular “Last Sermon” text authentic?

The text that circulates online is a composite. Its main themes are authentic and found in the major collections, but the wording is harmonised from different reports and includes later material. It should not be quoted as a single sound hadith.

3. Did the Prophet ﷺ say “I leave behind the Book and the Sunnah”?

The “Book of Allah” version is the most securely attested, in Sahih Muslim. The “Book and Sunnah” version appears in other reports, including a mursal report in Malik’s Muwatta, and scholars have discussed its chain. The meaning of both is that the community is to hold fast to revelation.

4. How did the Prophet ﷺ die?

He died of an illness in Rabi al-Awwal of the eleventh year after the Hijrah, in Aisha’s room, at the age of about sixty-three. He was buried where he died. The final words are reported with variation, and the most certain theme is his concern for prayer and for the vulnerable.

5. Who succeeded the Prophet ﷺ?

Abu Bakr was chosen as the first caliph at the Saqifah of Banu Sa’idah. Sunnis regard the choice as legitimate; Shi’a hold that Ali was the divinely appointed successor. The disagreement is old and rooted in different readings of the sources.
