Bilal ibn Rabah: The Voice of the First Adhan

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Bilal ibn Rabah is the companion whose voice became the sound of the new religion. He was an enslaved man in Makkah, tortured for saying that Allah is one, bought and freed by Abu Bakr, and then chosen by the Prophet ﷺ to call the believers to prayer. His life is a compressed history of what Islam did to the social order of the peninsula: it took a man whom the powerful regarded as property and made him the first voice of the community’s public worship.

His story is also a story about race. Bilal was of Abyssinian descent, and the sources remember the insults that were thrown at him. The Qur’an answered those insults with a verse that made piety, not lineage or colour, the measure of nobility (49:13). Bilal’s place in the tradition is therefore not a decorative detail. He is evidence that the early community’s claim of equality was tested on the bodies of the weak.

This article follows the established sources and marks the line between what the hadith and early biography report and what later storytelling added. It does not claim to know Bilal’s inner life, the exact price of his freedom or the precise date of his death. It does claim that his story is one of the most important in the first generation, and that it should be told without either sentimental exaggeration or embarrassed silence about slavery.

Key takeaways
  • Bilal was an enslaved man of Abyssinian descent in Makkah, owned by Umayyah ibn Khalaf al-Jumahi.
  • He was among the earliest Muslims and was tortured for his faith; the report that he repeated “Ahad, Ahad” is preserved in early biography.
  • Abu Bakr bought and freed him, and the sources differ on the price.
  • After the hijrah he became the muezzin of the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah, and the adhan was instituted in that period.
  • He fought at Badr and was present through the main campaigns of the Madinan period.
  • On the day of the conquest of Makkah the sources report that he gave the adhan from the Ka‘bah.
  • He left Madinah after the Prophet’s death and spent his later years in Syria, where he died around 20 AH according to the most common report.
  • The stories of his dream-visit to the grave and of his refusal ever to give the adhan again are later traditions and are not uniformly attested.

1. Why Bilal Matters

Bilal matters because he is the first test of the community’s claim that faith abolishes rank. The Quraysh measured a man by his tribe, his wealth and his birth. Bilal had none of those things in the eyes of Makkah. He was enslaved, dark-skinned and without protection. Yet the Prophet ﷺ chose him to stand above the congregation and call it to prayer, and the community accepted his voice as the signal of worship.

He also matters because his story preserves the memory of the persecuted. The Qur’an speaks repeatedly of those who were weak and oppressed in the land, and Bilal is one of the few whose torture is described in detail. His suffering is not an embarrassment to the tradition; it is part of its moral record. The same society that produced the persecution produced the man who ended it for Bilal.

Finally, Bilal matters as a reminder that the sources are not a single voice. The hadith collections preserve his virtues, the biographical works preserve the narrative of his torture and freedom, and the later preaching tradition added stories that are edifying but not always sound. An honest account holds those layers apart.

2. Slavery and Origins in Makkah

The sources describe Bilal as a man of Abyssinian descent, and his name is often given as Bilal ibn Rabah al-Habashi. His mother is named as Hamamah, and his father as Rabah. The biographical works do not agree on every detail of his parentage or on whether he was born in Makkah or brought there as a child. What is clear is that he was born into slavery and that his owner was Umayyah ibn Khalaf, a leading man of the Banu Jumah.

Slavery in Makkah was not a marginal institution. Enslaved people worked in households, herds and caravans, and they had no legal standing of their own. A slave could be bought, sold, inherited and punished without a hearing. Bilal’s position was therefore not merely poor; it was legally helpless. The sources say that he was known for his honesty and his strength, but those qualities did not protect him.

Source caution. The details of Bilal’s parentage, birthplace and early years vary across the biographical works. The article follows the common outline without claiming that every name and date is settled.

3. The First Believers and the Torture

Bilal was among the earliest converts to Islam. The sources place him in the first group of believers, alongside Abu Bakr, Zayd ibn Harithah, Ammar ibn Yasir and others. He did not come to the Prophet ﷺ as a man of influence seeking an alliance. He came as a slave who heard the message and accepted it, and his acceptance cost him more than it cost almost anyone else.

When Umayyah learned of his conversion, he subjected Bilal to punishment. The reports describe him being dragged over the hot sand of Makkah, a heavy stone placed on his chest, and his repeated refusal to renounce his faith. In the best-known version, Bilal could only say “Ahad, Ahad” — “One, One.” The torture did not break him, and the sources present his endurance as one of the earliest acts of heroic faith.

The Qur’an honours those who were persecuted for their belief. It speaks of the weak and oppressed who were wronged and who cried out to God (4:75), and it promises that those who emigrated after being tried will be rewarded (16:110). Bilal’s story is not the only one behind those verses, but it is one of the clearest illustrations of what they meant in practice.

Source caution. The “Ahad, Ahad” detail and the scene of the stone on the chest are preserved in early biography, especially Ibn Ishaq, rather than in a sound hadith with a detailed chain. The torture itself is widely reported; the exact staging is not.

4. Abu Bakr and the Price of Freedom

Abu Bakr al-Siddiq bought Bilal from Umayyah and freed him. The sources describe the transaction as a public act: Umayyah mocked Abu Bakr for paying a high price for a slave, and Abu Bakr replied that he would have paid more if necessary. The exact sum is reported differently in different works, and the article does not pretend that one figure is certain.

The purchase was not a casual charity. It was a deliberate use of wealth to remove a believer from the reach of his persecutor, and it became a model for the early community’s practice of manumission. The Qur’an lists the freeing of slaves among the acts of the steep path (90:11–13) and among the marks of righteousness (2:177). Abu Bakr’s money turned a verse into a lived action.

Source caution. The reports on the price of Bilal’s manumission differ widely. The article treats the purchase and release as established and the amount as uncertain.

5. The Hijrah and the First Adhan

Bilal migrated to Madinah with the Muslims. In the new city the community needed a public signal for the prayer, and the sources describe the institution of the adhan in that period. The best-known account involves a dream seen by Abdullah ibn Zayd, which the Prophet ﷺ recognised as a true dream and used as the basis for the call. Bilal was chosen to give it because of his powerful voice.

The choice was symbolic as well as practical. The man who had been told that his voice did not matter was now the man whose voice gathered the community five times a day. The adhan was not a private devotion; it was a public declaration of belief, heard by Muslims, Jews, Christians and polytheists alike. Bilal’s voice carried the shahadah across the rooftops of Madinah.

The sources also report that the Prophet ﷺ said, “O Bilal, comfort us with the prayer.” The phrase treats the call as a source of rest rather than a burden. Bilal’s task was to summon others to that rest, and the tradition remembers him as the muezzin of the Prophet ﷺ par excellence.

Source caution. The dream of Abdullah ibn Zayd is reported in the hadith literature, but the details of the adhan’s institution vary. The “comfort us with the prayer” report is preserved in Abu Dawud and has been discussed by scholars of hadith.

6. Badr, Uhud and the Battles

Bilal fought at Badr, and the sources preserve a report about his encounter with Umayyah on the battlefield. When he saw his former owner among the prisoners or the fallen, he is said to have declared that he would not be safe while Umayyah was safe. The report is dramatic, and it belongs to the early biographical narrative rather than to a calm legal text.

He was present at Uhud, the Trench, Hudaybiyyah and the other campaigns of the Madinan period. The muezzin did not stay behind the lines. He carried a weapon, stood in the ranks and shared the dangers of the community he called to prayer. The sources do not make him a general, but they do place him among the fighters.

Source caution. The account of Umayyah’s death at Badr is preserved in Ibn Ishaq and later histories. The hadith collections mention the event, but the dialogue and the exact sequence are not uniformly reported.

7. Bilal in the Prophet’s Household

Bilal was close to the Prophet ﷺ in a way that the sources remember with affection. The most famous report is the hadith of the footsteps: the Prophet ﷺ asked Bilal what deed he hoped would be most rewarded, and Bilal replied that he never made ablution without praying two units of prayer. The Prophet ﷺ said that he had heard the sound of Bilal’s footsteps in Paradise.

The report is in the sound collections, and it is one of the clearest statements of Bilal’s rank. It does not make him a scholar or a ruler; it makes him a man of consistent, hidden devotion. The Prophet ﷺ saw in his private habit a sign of nearness to God, and the community preserved the observation.

Bilal also served the Prophet ﷺ in practical ways. He kept the time of prayer, carried messages, managed the household’s needs and accompanied the Prophet ﷺ on journeys. The sources present him as trustworthy, generous and quick to defend the Prophet’s honour. His closeness was not the closeness of a courtier; it was the closeness of a servant who had been freed and who chose to stay.

Source caution. The hadith of the footsteps is in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. The details of Bilal’s daily service are drawn from biography and should not be read as a complete job description.

8. The Conquest of Makkah and the Adhan on the Ka‘bah

At the conquest of Makkah in 8 AH, the Prophet ﷺ entered the city with the Muslim army and granted a general amnesty to those who did not resist. The sources report that Bilal was with him, and that he gave the adhan from the Ka‘bah or from its roof. The act reversed the order of the city: the man who had been tortured in Makkah now called the city to the worship of the One he had refused to deny.

The report is preserved in the biographical and hadith literature, though the details differ. Some versions place Bilal on the roof of the Ka‘bah, others on a nearby height. The symbolic force of the scene is unmistakable, and it explains why the story became one of the most memorable images of the conquest.

The adhan on that day was not a triumph over a defeated people. It was a summons to a new order in which the Ka‘bah belonged to the worship of Allah alone. Bilal’s voice announced that the old hierarchy of clan and wealth had been judged, and that the community’s public worship would no longer be led by the sons of the powerful.

Source caution. The report that Bilal gave the adhan on the day of the conquest is widely preserved, but the exact location and wording vary. The article does not claim that every detail of the scene is established.

9. The Death of the Prophet ﷺ

The death of the Prophet ﷺ in 11 AH broke Bilal’s world. The sources describe his grief and his difficulty in continuing to give the adhan in the city where the Prophet ﷺ had lived. The call to prayer had been the sound of the Prophet’s presence; after his death, it became a reminder of absence.

The later reports say that Bilal asked Abu Bakr for permission to leave Madinah, and that Abu Bakr granted it. Other reports say that he stopped giving the adhan and that the community asked him to return. The stories differ, but they agree on the emotional core: Bilal could not separate the adhan from the man who had chosen him to give it.

Source caution. The reports about Bilal leaving Madinah and refusing to give the adhan are later biographical traditions. The fact of his grief is not in doubt; the exact sequence of events is.

10. Syria and the Later Years

Bilal spent his later years in Syria, where the Muslim armies had established a presence during the caliphate of Abu Bakr and Umar. The sources place him in Damascus and report that he took part in the campaigns. He is not remembered as a commander, but as a companion who carried the community’s worship into a new land.

One of the best-known later reports describes Umar’s visit to Syria. When Umar arrived, the people asked Bilal to give the adhan, and when he did, the companions wept. The sound reminded them of the Prophet ﷺ and of Madinah. The report is not a legal text; it is a memory of loss, and it shows how deeply the adhan was associated with the first generation.

Source caution. The report of Umar’s visit and the weeping of the companions is preserved in later biographical works. It is edifying and widely quoted, but it is not a sound hadith with a detailed chain.

11. Death, Burial and the Reports

The sources differ on the date and place of Bilal’s death. The most common report places his death in Damascus around 20 AH, during the caliphate of Umar, and his burial at the Bab al-Saghir cemetery. Other reports say that he died in Madinah, and some give a later date. The disagreement is not surprising for a companion who lived through the first conquests and whose family settled in Syria.

His age at death is also reported differently, with figures in the sixties. The numbers are later estimates, not contemporary records. What is consistent is that he died after the Prophet ﷺ, after the conquest of Makkah and after the community had expanded beyond Arabia. He did not live to see the great fitna, and the sources do not place him in the political conflicts of the later caliphate.

Source caution. The date and place of Bilal’s death are not settled. The article follows the most common report, Damascus around 20 AH, without claiming certainty.

12. The Later Legends and Their Limits

Bilal’s story attracted many later additions. One of the most popular is the account of his dream in which the Prophet ﷺ told him to visit his grave, and of Bilal travelling to Madinah to place his face on the tomb. Another is the claim that he never gave the adhan again after the Prophet’s death, or that he gave it only once, in Syria, and that the whole city wept.

These stories are beautiful, and they express a real grief. But they are not all equally attested. The dream-visit narrative is not found in the earliest collections, and the reports about his silence after the Prophet’s death vary. The reader should enjoy the stories as later expressions of love without treating them as historical records of equal weight to the hadith.

Source caution. The dream-visit story and the “he never called the adhan again” narrative are later traditions. They are not in the sound collections and should not be presented as established history.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

Bilal’s life teaches four things. The first is the dignity of the person: a man whom the world called property was chosen to lead the community’s worship. The second is the cost of faith: his conversion was not a change of opinion but a decision that led to physical torture. The third is the power of a voice: the adhan he called became the sound of Islam across the world. The fourth is the honesty of grief: he loved the Prophet ﷺ and did not hide the loss.

The limits of the sources are also a lesson. We do not know Bilal’s inner thoughts, the exact price of his freedom or the precise date of his death. The later stories fill the gaps with emotion, and some of them are edifying. But the historian’s task is to distinguish the established from the beautiful, and the believer’s task is to honour the man without inventing his biography.

The best way to remember Bilal is to listen to the adhan. Every call to prayer in every mosque repeats the words he was chosen to carry. The voice that Makkah tried to silence is now heard five times a day from every direction. That is a more eloquent memorial than any legend.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith, the early biographical reports and later narrative. It does not claim that every detail about Bilal is historically established, and it does not use his story to minimise the reality of slavery in early Islam.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Who was Bilal ibn Rabah?

Bilal ibn Rabah was an enslaved man of Abyssinian descent in Makkah, owned by Umayyah ibn Khalaf. He was among the earliest Muslims, was tortured for his faith, was bought and freed by Abu Bakr, and became the muezzin of the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah.

2. Was Bilal the first muezzin?

Yes, in the standard account. After the hijrah, when the community needed a public call to prayer, the Prophet ﷺ chose Bilal because of his strong voice. He is remembered as the first muezzin of Islam.

3. What did Bilal say under torture?

The early biographical reports say that he repeated “Ahad, Ahad” — “One, One” — refusing to renounce the oneness of Allah. The torture itself is widely reported; the exact staging of the scene is from early biography rather than a detailed sound hadith.

4. Did Bilal give the adhan at the conquest of Makkah?

The sources report that he did, from the Ka‘bah or its roof, on the day the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah in 8 AH. The details vary, but the report is widely preserved and the scene became one of the most memorable images of the conquest.

5. When and where did Bilal die?

The most common report places his death in Damascus around 20 AH, during the caliphate of Umar, and his burial at Bab al-Saghir. Other reports say he died in Madinah. The sources do not agree, and the article presents the disagreement rather than a certainty.

15. Sources & Further Reading

This article cites the Qur'an by surah and verse, and identifies hadith by collection and, where verified, book or number. Where scholars differ, the difference is stated rather than resolved by assertion.

  • The Qur’an — especially 49:13 on piety and nobility, 4:75 on the oppressed, 16:110 on those tried for their faith, and 90:11–13 on freeing a slave. Translations adapted from Saheeh International unless stated.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari — the hadith of Bilal’s footsteps in Paradise, the institution of the adhan and the reports on his virtues.
  • Sahih Muslim — the reports on Bilal and the muezzin’s rank.
  • Abu Dawud, Sunan — the report “O Bilal, comfort us with the prayer” and the adhan traditions.
  • Ibn Ishaq, Sirat Rasul Allah, as transmitted by Ibn Hisham — the torture, the manumission and the Badr narrative.
  • Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk — the conquest of Makkah and the later campaigns.
  • Ibn Sa‘d, Kitab al-Tabaqat al-Kabir — the biographical entry on Bilal and his family.
  • Ibn Kathir, al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah and al-Dhahabi, Siyar A‘lam al-Nubala — later synthesis with grading.
  • Modern academic studies of slavery in late antique Arabia and of race and conversion in early Islam.
