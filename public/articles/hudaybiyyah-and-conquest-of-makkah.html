Hudaybiyyah and the Conquest of Makkah

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Hudaybiyyah is the hinge of the Madinan Seerah. A pilgrimage that was blocked became a treaty; a treaty that looked like a humiliation became, in the Qur’an’s language, a clear victory. Two years later the same community entered Makkah almost without bloodshed and granted a general amnesty to the city that had tortured and expelled it.

This article follows that arc: the journey, the negotiations, the pledge under the tree, the terms, the Qur’anic interpretation, the breach, the march and the conquest. It also examines the moral character of the victory. The Prophet’s ﷺ restraint at the moment of maximum power is as important as the military achievement, and it is the part most often reduced to a slogan.

Key takeaways
  • Hudaybiyyah (6 AH) was an umrah journey that ended in a ten-year truce, not a battle.
  • The pledge of Ridwan took place under a tree after a rumour that a Quraysh envoy had been killed.
  • The terms seemed one-sided: the Muslims would not perform umrah that year and would return converts without guardians.
  • The Qur’an called the treaty a clear victory (48:1), and the years that followed saw a major expansion of Islam.
  • Quraysh’s allies breached the truce by attacking the allies of the Muslims, leading to the march on Makkah in 8 AH.
  • The conquest was marked by a general amnesty, the protection of Abu Sufyan’s house and the cleansing of the Kaaba.
  • The “go, you are free” speech is reported by the historians rather than as an authentic hadith.

1. A Turning Point in the Seerah

By the sixth year after the Hijrah, the Muslims had survived Badr, Uhud and the Trench. They were no longer a besieged minority, but they were still excluded from the sanctuary of Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ saw in a dream that he and his companions would enter the Sacred House in safety, with heads shaved or shortened, and he announced his intention to perform umrah.

The decision was religious, not military. The Muslims set out in the sacred month of Dhul-Qa’dah, wearing the pilgrim dress and driving sacrificial animals, so that their intention would be unmistakable. They were about fourteen hundred or fifteen hundred strong, with no intention of fighting. The Quraysh, however, saw the pilgrimage as a political challenge and sent forces to block the road.

The standoff at Hudaybiyyah, on the edge of the sacred territory, forced both sides to negotiate. The episode is a study in how a position of weakness can be converted into a strategic gain through patience, and how a Prophet ﷺ could accept terms that his companions found humiliating because he understood what they could not yet see.

2. The Journey and the Blockade

The Prophet ﷺ travelled with the Muhajirun and the Ansar, and the party included women and children. They took a route that avoided the main Quraysh patrols, guided by a man familiar with the desert tracks. When they reached Hudaybiyyah, the Prophet’s ﷺ camel knelt and refused to go further; he interpreted this as a sign that the Quraysh were determined to prevent the pilgrimage.

Negotiations began with envoys. The Quraysh demanded that the Muslims turn back and return the following year. The Prophet ﷺ sent Uthman ibn Affan to Makkah to explain the peaceful intention, and a rumour spread that Uthman had been killed. The Muslims responded with the pledge of Ridwan, placing their hands in the Prophet’s ﷺ hand under a tree, vowing not to flee. The Qur’an commemorates the pledge:

“Indeed, Allah was pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you under the tree, and He knew what was in their hearts, so He sent down tranquillity upon them and rewarded them with an imminent conquest.”
Qur’an 48:18

The pledge was not a declaration of war. It was a commitment of loyalty in a moment of uncertainty. The tree itself later became a site of devotion, and the second caliph Umar had it cut down to prevent it from being treated as a shrine. The report is in Bukhari and reflects the concern to keep devotion directed to Allah alone.

3. The Terms of the Treaty

The agreement that emerged is known as the treaty of Hudaybiyyah. Its main terms, as reported in the Sirah and the hadith collections, were:

  • A truce between the Muslims and Quraysh for ten years.
  • The Muslims would return to Madinah that year without performing umrah, and would come back the following year for three days.
  • Any person from Quraysh who came to the Muslims without the permission of his guardian would be returned to Quraysh; any Muslim who went to Quraysh would not be returned.
  • The Arab tribes were free to ally with either side, and an attack on an ally would be treated as an attack on the party to which it belonged.
  • The Muslims would enter Makkah unarmed, apart from the weapons of a traveller.

The wording of the document caused further friction. The Prophet ﷺ dictated “In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate”, and the Quraysh envoy objected; he accepted “In Your name, O Allah”. He dictated “This is what Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah, has agreed”, and the envoy objected to the title; he accepted “Muhammad ibn Abdullah”. To the companions this looked like surrender.

The asymmetry of the return clause is real and should not be explained away. It meant that a Muslim who escaped Makkah could be handed back to his persecutors. The Prophet ﷺ accepted it because the treaty as a whole served a greater purpose, and because the clause was later used sparingly and eventually superseded. The episode shows that prophetic leadership sometimes accepts a lesser evil for a greater good, and that the companions were allowed to struggle with the decision.

4. Why It Looked Like a Defeat

The companions were deeply distressed. Umar ibn al-Khattab questioned the Prophet ﷺ about the terms and about the dream of entering Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ replied that he was the servant and messenger of Allah and would not disobey Him. The tension was real, and the sources do not hide it. Some companions were so upset that they hesitated to shave their heads and sacrifice the animals at the end of the journey.

The Qur’an responded with Surah al-Fath, revealed on the return journey, which called the treaty a clear victory:

“Indeed, We have given you a clear conquest.”
Qur’an 48:1

The title “al-Fath” is usually translated as “the victory” or “the opening”. The surah explains that the treaty brought tranquillity, that Allah knew what the believers did not, and that the hostile Quraysh had been restrained from fighting them. It also promises that they would enter the Sacred Mosque in safety, which happened the following year.

The lesson is not that appearances never matter. It is that a leader may see a horizon that the community cannot yet see, and that loyalty in such moments is tested precisely by disappointment. The companions who stayed with the Prophet ﷺ were later able to look back on Hudaybiyyah as the beginning of the great expansion.

5. The Fruits of Hudaybiyyah

The strategic results were substantial. For the first time, Quraysh dealt with the Muslims as a political party rather than as outlaws. The truce opened the roads, and the tribes could now choose alliances without fear of Quraysh reprisal. Delegations came to Madinah, and the number of conversions increased markedly. The following year the Muslims performed the umrah of qada in Makkah, and the Quraysh withdrew for three days as agreed.

The treaty also allowed the Prophet ﷺ to turn his attention to other fronts. He sent letters to neighbouring rulers — the Byzantine emperor, the Persian king, the Negus of Abyssinia, the governor of Egypt and others — inviting them to Islam. The authenticity and exact wording of some of these letters are debated, but the diplomatic activity is well attested. The expedition to Khaybar in the seventh year removed a hostile Jewish stronghold north of Madinah.

Hudaybiyyah therefore functioned as a diplomatic revolution. It converted a military stalemate into recognition, and recognition into expansion. The Qur’an’s description of it as a conquest is not rhetorical inflation; it is a judgement about the long-term consequences of a treaty that looked like a concession.

6. The Breach of the Treaty

The truce held for about two years. Then the tribe of Banu Bakr, allied with Quraysh, attacked the tribe of Khuza’ah, allied with the Muslims, in the sacred territory of Makkah. Quraysh supported the attack with men and weapons. The treaty had made an attack on an ally equivalent to an attack on the party itself, so the breach was clear.

Khuza’ah appealed to the Prophet ﷺ, and he gave them a choice: Quraysh could either pay blood-money, or dissolve the alliance with Banu Bakr, or consider the treaty void. Quraysh chose to dissolve the alliance, then sent Abu Sufyan to Madinah to repair the agreement. His mission failed. The Prophet ﷺ began preparations for a march on Makkah and concealed the destination, so that the city would not have time to organise a defence.

The sources report that the Prophet ﷺ prayed, “O Allah, blind the spies and the news from Quraysh until we come upon them in their land.” The secrecy was effective. The Muslims assembled a force of about ten thousand, including many tribes who had joined since Hudaybiyyah, and marched in Ramadan of the eighth year.

7. The Secret March and the Camp at Marr al-Zahran

The army camped at Marr al-Zahran, near Makkah, and lit ten thousand fires. Abu Sufyan came out to investigate and was brought to the Prophet ﷺ. He eventually accepted Islam, and the Prophet ﷺ granted protection to anyone who entered his house or the Sacred Mosque or stayed in his own home. The conversion of Abu Sufyan was a political and psychological victory: the chief of the opposing city had crossed over.

The Prophet ﷺ divided the army into columns and gave strict orders about who should be fought and who should not. He instructed that no one should fight except those who resisted, and he named a small number of individuals who had committed serious crimes and were to be seized even if they clung to the Kaaba. The list is reported in the Sirah and includes people who had killed or mutilated Muslims.

The entry was almost bloodless. A skirmish occurred at the pass of Khandamah, where Khalid ibn al-Walid’s column met resistance and a small number were killed on both sides. The Prophet ﷺ disapproved of the fighting and later paid blood-money for the dead. The main body entered Makkah without a battle.

8. The Conquest: Entry and Amnesty

The Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah on his camel, with his head bowed in humility, reciting Surah al-Fath. He did not enter as a triumphant king demanding submission. He went first to the Kaaba, performed the circumambulation, and touched the Black Stone with a stick because the crowd was too dense.

The people of Makkah gathered, expecting punishment. The Prophet ﷺ asked them what they thought he would do. They said that he was a noble brother and the son of a noble brother. He replied with the words of Joseph to his brothers: “No blame will there be upon you today. May Allah forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful” (12:92). Then he said, “Go, for you are free.”

“No blame will there be upon you today. May Allah forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful.”
Qur’an 12:92, recited by the Prophet ﷺ at the conquest

The amnesty was not absolute. A small number of individuals accused of murder, mutilation or persistent treachery were excluded, and some were executed. The exceptions are reported in the Sirah and are a reminder that the general pardon was a political decision, not a legal abolition of all accountability. Even so, the contrast with the customary sack of a conquered city was stark. The Prophet ﷺ had the power to destroy Makkah and chose mercy.

Source caution: the famous words “Go, for you are free” are reported by Ibn Hisham and later historians, not in an authentic hadith with a sound chain. The general amnesty itself is well established in the early sources.

9. The Cleansing of the Kaaba and Bilal’s Adhan

The Prophet ﷺ ordered the idols around and inside the Kaaba to be removed and destroyed. He recited, “The truth has come, and falsehood has vanished; indeed falsehood is ever bound to vanish” (17:81). The Kaaba was restored as the house of the one God, and the pilgrimage was reformed. The change was theological and political at once: the sanctuary that had been the centre of the tribal cult became the centre of the worship of Allah alone.

The Prophet ﷺ then ordered Bilal ibn Rabah, the formerly enslaved Abyssinian companion, to climb the Kaaba and give the call to prayer. The image is one of the most powerful in the Seerah: the man who had been tortured in Makkah now stood above the sanctuary and called the city to prayer. It was a public statement that the new order was not based on tribe, wealth or race.

The Prophet ﷺ stayed in Makkah for a period, teaching and reorganising the sanctuary. He did not force the Makkans to convert. Many did, but the Qur’an’s principle of no compulsion remained, and the city was integrated into the growing polity through treaties, education and the gradual transformation of its institutions.

10. Hunayn, Ta’if and the Consolidation

The conquest of Makkah did not end the fighting. The tribes of Hawazin and Thaqif gathered at Hunayn, and the Muslims marched to meet them. The Qur’an describes the initial rout and the steadfastness of the Prophet ﷺ and a group of companions:

“And on the day of Hunayn, when your multitude was pleasing to you but it availed you nothing, and the earth, for all its breadth, was constrained for you, and then you turned back, fleeing.”
Qur’an 9:25

The Muslims eventually won, and the spoils were distributed. The Prophet ﷺ gave generously to the new converts of Makkah, including Abu Sufyan and others, to reconcile their hearts, while some Ansar felt that they had been overlooked. He addressed them and reassured them, and the report is in Bukhari and Muslim. The episode shows that the politics of reconciliation continued after the victory.

Ta’if was besieged but not taken at that time; its people later accepted Islam voluntarily. By the end of the ninth year, delegations were arriving from across Arabia, and the community had become the dominant power in the region. The conquest of Makkah was the turning point that made this possible.

11. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for Hudaybiyyah and the conquest are:

  • The Qur’an — especially 48:1–29, 9:25–27, 12:92 and 17:81.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — the treaty, the pledge of Ridwan, the conquest, the amnesty and Hunayn.
  • Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah — the fullest narrative of the negotiations, the march and the entry.
  • Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk — parallel reports and chronology.
  • Al-Waqidi, Kitab al-Maghazi — detail used with caution because of its weak standing.
  • Ibn Kathir, al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah — a later synthesis with grading comments.

The treaty text survives in several recensions with minor differences. The exact number of companions at Hudaybiyyah is given as fourteen hundred or fifteen hundred. The speeches at the conquest, including “Go, for you are free”, are from the historians and should be labelled as such. The Qur’anic verses and the main events are established in the sound sources.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic and hadith evidence from the later narrative detail. It does not present the historians’ speeches as verbatim words of the Prophet ﷺ.

12. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Why is Hudaybiyyah called a victory?

The Qur’an calls it a clear conquest (48:1) because the treaty gave the Muslims political recognition, opened the roads, allowed alliances and led to a large expansion of Islam. The military and diplomatic gains outweighed the apparent concessions.

2. What was the pledge of Ridwan?

It was a pledge of loyalty by the companions under a tree at Hudaybiyyah, after a rumour that the Quraysh envoy Uthman ibn Affan had been killed. The Qur’an praises those who pledged (48:18).

3. Why did the Prophet ﷺ accept the return of Muslim converts to Quraysh?

The clause was part of a package that secured a ten-year truce and recognition. It was a painful concession, and the companions struggled with it, but the treaty as a whole served the community. The clause was later used sparingly and became obsolete after the conquest.

4. Was the conquest of Makkah violent?

It was almost bloodless. A skirmish at Khandamah caused a small number of deaths, which the Prophet ﷺ disapproved of and compensated. The main entry was peaceful, and a general amnesty was granted, with exceptions for a small number of people accused of serious crimes.

5. Did the Prophet ﷺ forgive everyone in Makkah?

He granted a general amnesty and protected those who entered the Sacred Mosque, his house or Abu Sufyan’s house. A small number of individuals accused of murder, mutilation or persistent treachery were excluded. The amnesty was a political decision, not an abolition of all accountability.
