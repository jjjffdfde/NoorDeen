Musa: Pharaoh, the Exodus and the Law

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Musa is the prophet most often named in the Qur’an. His story runs from the slaughter of the male infants in Egypt to the giving of the law at the mountain, and it is retold in several surahs with different emphases: the rescue of a condemned child, the call at the fire, the contest with the sorcerers, the judgement of Pharaoh, the crossing of the sea, the covenant and the golden calf.

It is also the story in which the Qur’an most directly confronts political power. Pharaoh is not merely a bad king; he is the archetype of a ruler who claims divinity and treats a people as expendable. Musa is not merely a miracle-worker; he is a reluctant messenger who asks for help, doubts his own tongue and is sustained by his brother. This article follows the Qur’anic text, separates the layers of later narrative, and marks clearly where the evidence stops.

Key takeaways
  • Musa is the most frequently named prophet in the Qur’an, and his story is distributed across several surahs rather than told once.
  • The Qur’an does not name Pharaoh, his wife, the Egyptian killed by Musa, or the mountain of the law.
  • The confrontation with Pharaoh turns on the difference between sorcery and a sign from Allah, not on a contest of political programmes.
  • The crossing of the sea is presented as a divine rescue of an oppressed people and a judgement on the pursuer.
  • The golden calf episode shows that deliverance does not guarantee obedience, and that leadership must confront the community’s failures.
  • The Khidr passage is a lesson in the limits of human knowledge; the Qur’an does not name the servant or define his status.
  • Qarun is the Qur’anic archetype of wealth that forgets its source and refuses social responsibility.
  • Later elaborations — the exact date of the exodus, the identification of Sinai, the names of Pharaoh’s magicians and the “split-sea” archaeology — are not established by revelation.

1. Why Musa Matters

Musa is named more than a hundred and thirty times in the Qur’an, more than any other prophet. His story is a cycle of scenes rather than a single biography: the child condemned by the state, the call at the fire, the contest with the sorcerers, the crossing of the sea, the covenant at the mountain and the golden calf. The longest passages are 7:103–162, 20:9–98, 26:10–68 and 28:3–46, with further material in 2:49–61, 5:20–26, 10:75–93, 17:101–104, 18:60–82, 27:7–14, 28:76–82, 37:114–122, 44:17–33 and 79:15–26.

The story rewards close reading rather than a single chronological summary, and it demands source discipline. The Qur’an gives the theological spine; the hadith add a few details; the commentaries and the qisas al-anbiya literature add much more, some edifying and some unverifiable. This article keeps those layers distinct.

2. The Child in the River

Pharaoh has ordered the killing of the sons of the Children of Israel because he fears a challenger. Allah inspires Musa’s mother to place him in a chest and cast it into the river, promising to return him and make him one of the messengers (28:7). The command is terrifying, and the Qur’an says her heart was nearly empty of everything except the promise (28:10).

The chest floats to Pharaoh’s household. His wife sees the child and loves him: “A comfort of the eye for me and for you. Do not kill him; perhaps he may benefit us, or we may adopt him as a son” (28:9). The one who decreed death for the children of Israel becomes the guardian of the child who will confront him.

The child refuses the wet-nurses. His sister, watching from a distance, suggests the family who bore him, and he is returned to his mother so that her eye might be comforted and she might know that the promise of Allah is true (28:13). The narrative names neither the mother, the sister, Pharaoh nor his wife.

Source caution. The name “Asiya” for Pharaoh’s wife and the names given to Musa’s mother appear in later narrative literature, not in the Qur’an or the authentic hadith. They may be used as traditional labels, but they are not part of the revealed text.

3. The Killing, the Flight and Madyan

When Musa reaches maturity, he enters the city and finds two men fighting, one from his own people and one from his enemies. He strikes the Egyptian and kills him. The text says this was “from the work of Satan” and that Musa prayed, “My Lord, I have wronged myself, so forgive me” (28:15–16). He is forgiven.

He reaches Madyan, waters the flocks of two women who cannot compete with the shepherds, and prays for help. One of them returns to say that her father invites him. The old man offers marriage to one of his daughters in exchange for eight years of service, with ten as a completion rather than a condition (28:27). The Qur’an does not name the father; identifying him with Shu‘ayb is a later interpretive tradition.

4. The Fire, the Call and the Signs

On the journey Musa sees a fire and tells his family to wait while he brings news or a brand to warm them (28:29). When he reaches it, he is called from the right side of the valley, from the blessed tree: “O Musa, indeed I am Allah, Lord of the worlds” (28:30). He is commanded to remove his sandals because he is in the sacred valley of Tuwa (20:12).

The first sign is the staff: he throws it and it becomes a serpent, and he is told to take it and not fear. The second is his hand: he puts it under his arm and it comes out white without disease (20:17–22; 28:31–32). These are not magic tricks. They are signs given so that Pharaoh may believe, and they are accompanied by the command to establish prayer and remember Allah (20:14).

Musa immediately asks for help: “My Lord, expand for me my breast, and ease for me my task, and untie the knot from my tongue, that they may understand my speech” (20:25–28). He asks that Harun share the burden because he is more eloquent (28:34), and the request is granted. Prophethood is a burden carried with support, not a solitary performance of power.

5. Before Pharaoh: Dialogue and Demand

Musa and Harun are commanded to go to Pharaoh and to speak with gentle words so that he may remember or fear (20:43–44). They ask, “Who is the Lord of the worlds?” and Pharaoh answers by pointing to his court and his own claim: “I am your lord, the most high” (79:24). The exchange is a confrontation between a messenger who speaks for Allah and a ruler who has made himself the final authority.

Musa reminds Pharaoh of the favours shown to the Children of Israel and of the signs. Pharaoh responds by recounting his own kindness in raising Musa, and by accusing him of ingratitude and of the killing (26:18–19). Musa answers that he did the deed while astray and that Allah forgave him. The argument then moves to the staff and the hand, which Pharaoh calls “clear magic” before assembling the sorcerers (26:34–38).

Source caution. The Qur’an calls the ruler “Pharaoh” (Fir‘awn), a title rather than a personal name. The identification of the Pharaoh of the exodus with a specific Egyptian king, and the dating of the event, are historical questions on which the Qur’an is silent and the evidence disputed.

6. The Contest with the Sorcerers

The sorcerers assemble on a day of festival and throw their ropes and staffs, which appear to the audience to move. The Qur’an is careful with the language: “their ropes and staffs seemed to him, by their magic, to be moving” (20:66). The effect is real enough to frighten Musa, but it is an illusion produced by magic, not a transformation of nature.

Musa throws his staff, and it devours what they have produced. The sorcerers recognise the difference immediately. They fall in prostration and say, “We have believed in the Lord of the worlds, the Lord of Musa and Harun” (20:70). Pharaoh threatens to cut off their hands and feet and crucify them. They answer that they will not prefer him to what has come to them, and ask their Lord for patience (20:71–76; 7:120–126).

The scene is the moral centre of the Egyptian narrative. The professional magicians, whose livelihood depends on illusion, are the first to see that this is not a greater illusion. They choose death over recantation. The Qur’an does not report their names, and later lists of magician names are not reliable historical data.

7. The Plagues, the Night Departure and the Sea

After the sorcerers believe, Pharaoh persists. The Qur’an describes years of drought, then flood, locusts, lice, frogs and blood, “distinct signs”, yet the people remain arrogant (7:130–133). Each time they promise to believe if the punishment is lifted, and each time they break the promise. The narrative is a study in the failure of punishment to produce genuine change.

Musa is commanded to depart with the believers at night. Pharaoh discovers the departure and gathers his forces. At the sea the people fear that they will be overtaken. Musa says, “No! Indeed, with me is my Lord; He will guide me” (26:62). He is commanded to strike the sea with his staff, and it parts, each side like a great mountain. The believers cross. Pharaoh and his army follow and are drowned while Musa and his people watch (26:63–66; 2:50; 20:77–78).

Pharaoh’s end is described in 10:90–92: when drowning he says, “I believe that there is no deity except Him in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am of the Muslims.” The answer comes: “Now? But you disobeyed before and were of the corrupters.” His body is saved as a sign for those who come after. The Qur’an does not say his repentance was accepted; the confession is too late.

8. The Mountain, the Tablets and the Covenant

After the crossing, Musa is summoned to the mountain for forty nights and leaves Harun in charge. The Qur’an says that Allah spoke to Musa and gave him the tablets containing instruction and explanation for everything (7:142–145). The covenant of the Children of Israel is renewed, and they are commanded to hold fast to what has been given.

The Qur’an describes the mountain being raised above the people as a sign of the seriousness of the covenant (2:63; 7:171). It also records their reluctance: they are told to enter the gate prostrating and to say “forgiveness”, but they change the word and refuse (2:58–59; 7:161–162). Deliverance from Egypt does not produce a community of automatic obedience.

The tablets are not described in detail. The Qur’an calls them “the tablets” and says they contain guidance and mercy. It does not give their material, number, language or complete contents. The later stories that fill in these details are not revelation. What matters is the covenant, the law and the community’s response.

9. The Golden Calf and the Failure of Leadership

While Musa is on the mountain, the people make a calf from their ornaments and worship it. The Qur’an says the Samiri cast something into the fire and produced a calf that lowed (20:87–88). Harun had warned them, but they did not listen. When Musa returns, he is angry and grieved, and seizes his brother by the head. Harun explains that he feared the people would divide and that Musa would blame him (20:92–94).

The account is more complex than a simple story of a prophet punishing idolaters. It shows the weakness of a community that had just witnessed the sea, the failure of a leader left in charge, and the rage of a prophet who must carry both the law and the people. Musa prays for forgiveness for himself and his brother and for mercy for the people (7:151–152).

The Samiri is given a distinctive punishment: Musa tells him to go, and in this world he will have a life of isolation and a promise of reckoning (20:97). The Qur’an does not identify the Samiri with a historical person or tribe, and the name should not be treated as a verified proper name.

10. Water, Manna and the Wilderness Tests

In the wilderness the people complain about food and water. The Qur’an says they were given shade from clouds, manna and quail (2:57; 7:160), and that water came from the rock when Musa struck it with his staff. Twelve springs flowed, one for each tribe, so that no group would fight over the water (2:60; 7:160).

The gifts come with a warning: “Eat and drink from the provision of Allah, and do not commit abuse on the earth, spreading corruption” (2:60). The Qur’an repeatedly links material provision to moral responsibility. The wilderness is a school in which the community learns dependence on Allah and the limits of its demands.

The people ask for the vegetables of Egypt — cucumbers, garlic, lentils and onions — and are told to go down to a city where they can find what they ask (2:61). The request expresses a nostalgia for the familiar, even when the familiar was a life of oppression. The Qur’an diagnoses the psychology of a people who prefer known slavery to unknown freedom.

11. Musa and the Servant of Allah

Surah al-Kahf contains a journey that has fascinated readers for centuries. Musa says he will not stop until he reaches the confluence of the two seas, and travels with his young companion. They lose a fish, which returns to the sea, and meet a servant of Allah whom Allah has taught mercy and knowledge (18:60–65). Musa asks to follow him and is warned that he will not bear what he will see.

The servant makes a hole in a boat, kills a boy, and repairs a wall in a town that refused hospitality. Each time Musa objects and is reminded of his promise of patience. At the end the servant explains: the boat belonged to poor people and was being taken by a king; the boy’s parents were believers and he would have oppressed them; the wall concealed a treasure left for two orphans (18:71–82).

The Qur’an does not name the servant. The name “Khidr” comes from later tradition. Nor does the Qur’an settle his status as a prophet, an angel or a righteous servant; the classical scholars disagreed. What the passage teaches is clear: human knowledge is partial, and a believer may not judge a divine plan by the fragment he can see.

12. Qarun and the Ethics of Wealth

Qarun is introduced in Surah al-Qasas as a man from the people of Musa who is given so much treasure that its keys would burden a group of strong men (28:76). He is warned not to exult, and told to seek the home of the Hereafter while not forgetting his share of the world, and to do good as Allah has done good to him. He refuses, claiming his wealth came from knowledge he possesses.

The Qur’an’s answer is that Allah knows best who deserves what, and that Qarun’s wealth is a test, not proof of favour. He goes out among his people in his adornment, and those who desire this world wish they had the like. The knowledgeable among them say that the reward of Allah is better for those who believe and do righteous deeds (28:79–80).

Qarun is then swallowed by the earth with his dwelling. The next morning those who had envied him say that it is as if Allah extends provision to whom He wills and restricts it. The story is not a celebration of poverty; it warns against reading wealth as a sign of divine approval and against arrogance that refuses social obligation.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

The Musa cycle is a sustained meditation on liberation and its aftermath. The Qur’an does not end the story with the crossing of the sea. It follows the freed people into the wilderness and shows that political liberation is not the same as moral transformation. The community that leaves Egypt must still learn to worship one God, obey the law, accept leadership and resist the temptation to manufacture a comfortable idol.

The cycle also insists that prophets are human. Musa asks for help, fears, becomes angry, makes a mistake and seeks forgiveness. The Qur’an does not present him as a demigod. His authority comes from Allah, and his strength is exercised through signs, patience and the support of his brother.

Source caution. The Qur’an is the primary source for this article. Authentic hadith are cited only where they add a specific point. The names of Pharaoh, Asiya, Musa’s mother, the magicians and Khidr, the exact date of the exodus, the identification of Mount Sinai and the location of the sea crossing come from later tradition, archaeology or modern speculation and are not part of the revealed text.
Editorial note: this is an educational synthesis, not a critical edition of the commentaries. The article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith and later narrative, and it does not adjudicate every classical dispute.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Who was Pharaoh in the Qur’an?

The Qur’an uses “Pharaoh” (Fir‘awn) as a title, not a personal name. It does not identify him with a specific Egyptian king, and the dating of the exodus is a historical question on which the Qur’an is silent. The later attempts to match him with a particular dynasty are hypotheses, not revelation.

2. Did Musa really split the sea?

The Qur’an states that the sea was parted for the Children of Israel and that Pharaoh and his army were drowned (2:50; 26:63–66). It does not describe the mechanism, the location or the width of the path. A believer accepts the Qur’anic account; the attempt to explain it by a natural event is speculation, not a requirement of faith.

3. Who was Khidr?

The Qur’an does not name him. It calls him “a servant from among Our servants” (18:65). The name Khidr and the claim that he is a prophet, an angel or a saint come from later tradition, and classical scholars disagreed about his status. The passage’s lesson is the limits of human knowledge, not the existence of a secret hierarchy of saints.

4. Was the Torah given at Mount Sinai?

The Qur’an says that Musa was given the tablets at the mountain (al-tur) and that Allah spoke to him there. It does not identify the mountain with a specific peak. The identification of Jabal Musa in the Sinai peninsula is a later tradition and a modern tourist designation, not a Qur’anic statement.

5. Did Musa commit a sin when he killed the Egyptian?

The Qur’an records the killing, Musa’s immediate repentance and Allah’s forgiveness (28:15–16). Classical theology distinguishes between the protection of prophets from major sins and the possibility of lesser mistakes followed by repentance. The Qur’an presents the event as a wrong that was forgiven, not as a model to imitate.
