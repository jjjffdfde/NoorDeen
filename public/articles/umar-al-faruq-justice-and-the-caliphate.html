Umar: Justice, Conquest and the Caliphate

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Umar ibn al-Khattab is the companion who turned the small Madinan polity into a world empire and then spent the rest of his life trying to keep that empire accountable to a standard higher than itself. He was a man of immense physical strength, a formidable debater before Islam, and a caliph who patrolled the streets at night, carried grain on his back and wept over a single wronged woman.

His story is also the story of the early conquests, with all their glory and all their moral difficulty. The sources celebrate his justice and record his severity. They describe his reforms and his uncertainties. This article follows the established record, notes where the famous stories are weakly attested, and refuses to turn him into either a saint or a caricature.

Key takeaways
  • Umar was a Qurayshi noble of the Banu Adi and one of the fiercest early opponents of Islam.
  • His conversion is associated with hearing Surah Ta-Ha in his sister’s house; the sources differ on the details.
  • He was called al-Faruq, “the one who distinguishes,” and was known for his strength and severity.
  • He was a close adviser to Abu Bakr and became the second caliph in 13 AH.
  • His caliphate saw the conquest of Syria, Iraq, Egypt and Persia, and the surrender of Jerusalem.
  • He established the diwan, the Hijri calendar and a system of stipends and provincial administration.
  • He died by assassination in 23 AH and was buried beside the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr.
  • Later stories such as the burning of the library of Alexandria and the “Umar covenant” with Jerusalem are not established by the earliest sources.

1. Why Umar Matters

Umar’s caliphate lasted about ten and a half years, from 13 AH to 23 AH. In that decade the armies of the community defeated the two great empires of the age, the Byzantines and the Sasanians, and established garrisons from the Euphrates to the Nile. Umar did not lead most of those armies in person, but he directed them, appointed their commanders, and tried to control what conquest did to the conquerors.

He is also the caliph most associated with the idea of justice as a visible, institutional practice. The sources are full of stories about his night patrols, his refusal of luxury, his treatment of non-Muslim subjects and his readiness to be corrected by ordinary people. Some of these stories are later embellishments, but the pattern is early and consistent.

At the same time, Umar’s record raises hard questions. The conquests were wars of expansion, and the sources record harsh decisions. His treatment of the conquered peoples, the status of non-Muslims and the distribution of wealth are debated by historians. An honest account must hold the justice and the violence together, rather than choosing one and ignoring the other.

2. The Man Before Islam

Umar was born in Makkah into the Banu Adi, a clan of Quraysh known for its role in arbitration and diplomacy. His father, al-Khattab, was a harsh man, and the sources describe a difficult relationship. Umar grew up strong, tall and skilled in wrestling, riding and public speech. He was known for his temper and his readiness to defend the honour of his tribe.

He was a trader and a diplomat. The sources say that he was sent as an envoy by the Quraysh and that he had a reputation for fair dealing in a society that did not always value it. He was also a man of the world, aware of the politics of the peninsula and the weaknesses of the tribes.

Before Islam he was not a philosopher or a seeker. He was a practical man of his society, and he saw the new movement as a threat to the order he knew. That makes his conversion more than a change of opinion: it was a reorientation of a whole life.

3. The Conversion and Surah Ta-Ha

The best-known account of Umar’s conversion begins with his anger at the spread of Islam. He took his sword and went out to find the Prophet ﷺ. On the way he was told that his own sister Fatimah and her husband Sa‘id ibn Zayd had accepted Islam. He went to their house and found them reading a page of the Qur’an.

He struck his sister, then saw blood and was ashamed. He asked for the page. It contained the opening of Surah Ta-Ha. He read it and was moved. He went to the Prophet ﷺ and accepted Islam. The sources differ on the exact sequence and on which verses he read, but the core of the story is early and widely reported.

The conversion changed the balance of power in Makkah. Umar was not a marginal figure; he was a man whose strength and connections mattered. The Muslims had prayed in secret; after his conversion they prayed openly near the Ka‘bah. The sources present this as a turning point, and the Prophet ﷺ is said to have called him al-Faruq, the one who distinguishes truth from falsehood.

Source caution. The story of Umar reading Surah Ta-Ha is preserved in early biographical sources but with variations in wording and detail. The title al-Faruq is also explained in more than one way. The article follows the common account without claiming that every sentence of dialogue is verbatim.

4. Al-Faruq in Makkah

After his conversion, Umar became one of the most visible Muslims in Makkah. The sources report that he challenged the Quraysh, that he protected the weak, and that he was willing to use force when necessary. His reputation for severity was already established, and it now served the community.

He was not, however, a man without restraint. The Qur’an and the Prophet ﷺ corrected him more than once. The sources record his occasional excess of zeal and the Prophet’s interventions. This is important: the later image of Umar as the infallible enforcer obscures the fact that he was a disciple who was taught.

His time in Makkah also gave him a deep knowledge of the tribes, their alliances and their grievances. That knowledge would become one of his greatest assets as caliph, when he had to hold together a coalition of Arab tribes and conquered peoples.

5. Hijrah, Badr and the Pledge

Umar migrated to Madinah openly, according to the sources, and became one of the closest companions of the Prophet ﷺ. He fought at Badr, Uhud and the Trench, and he was present at Hudaybiyyah. At Hudaybiyyah he is reported to have questioned the terms of the treaty and to have been reassured by the Prophet ﷺ and by Abu Bakr.

The episode is a useful corrective to the legend of Umar as a man who never doubted. He was human, and the treaty of Hudaybiyyah was difficult for many of the companions. The Qur’an later called it a clear victory (48:1), and Umar’s willingness to submit after his objection is part of his character.

He was also one of those who pledged under the tree at al-Ridwan (48:18). The pledge was a response to a rumour that Uthman had been killed, and it became a mark of loyalty to the Prophet ﷺ. Umar’s participation is reported in the hadith literature.

6. The Right Hand of Abu Bakr

When the Prophet ﷺ died, Umar initially refused to believe it. Abu Bakr calmed him and recited the verse that Muhammad ﷺ was a messenger who had passed away (3:144). Umar later described this as the moment when the reality became clear to him.

At the Saqifah, Umar supported Abu Bakr’s candidacy and took his hand in pledge. He became the caliph’s closest adviser and the enforcer of his decisions. When Abu Bakr fell ill, he consulted the senior companions and nominated Umar as his successor. The nomination was not unanimous, but it was accepted.

Umar’s first act as caliph was to continue Abu Bakr’s policies, including the Ridda campaigns and the collection of the Qur’an. He also began to build the institutions that would govern the expanding state. The partnership between the two men is one of the most important in early Islamic history.

7. The Caliphate and the Great Conquests

The conquests of Umar’s caliphate were rapid and vast. The armies of the community defeated the Byzantines at Yarmuk in 15 AH, took Damascus and the Levant, and pushed into Egypt under Amr ibn al-As. In the east, the Sasanian empire collapsed after the battles of al-Qadisiyyah and Nihawand, and the Muslims took Iraq and Persia.

Umar directed the campaigns from Madinah through letters and appointed commanders. He insisted that the conquered peoples be treated according to treaties and that the spoils be distributed according to law. He also tried to prevent the Arab fighters from becoming a landed aristocracy, preferring to keep them in garrison towns such as Kufa and Basra.

The conquests were not a simple story of religious expansion. They were driven by a combination of political instability in the two empires, the mobility of the Arab tribes, the appeal of the new faith and the promise of booty. The sources present them as a divine opening, but they also record the human calculations, rivalries and atrocities that accompanied them.

8. Jerusalem and the Ethics of Conquest

In 16 or 17 AH, Jerusalem surrendered to the Muslims. Umar travelled to receive the surrender in person, a gesture that impressed the local population. The sources describe a written assurance that guaranteed the lives, property and churches of the inhabitants in exchange for tribute.

The document known as the “Umar covenant” is preserved in later forms, and its exact wording is disputed. Some versions include restrictions on the building of new churches and on public displays of non-Muslim worship. Historians disagree about how much of this goes back to Umar and how much reflects later legal developments.

What is clear is that Umar refused to pray inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, according to the sources, so that Muslims would not later claim it as a mosque. He prayed outside instead. The story is a powerful image of a conqueror who understood the danger of turning a victory into a permanent religious grievance.

Source caution. The “Umar covenant” as it circulates today is a later composite. Its core — a treaty of protection in exchange for tribute — is consistent with early practice, but the detailed restrictions are not securely attributable to Umar. The article does not present the later document as his verbatim words.

9. The Year of Ashes and the Plague

Umar’s caliphate was not only a story of victory. A severe drought and famine struck the Hijaz and Syria, and the period became known as the Year of Ashes (sanat al-ramadah) because the land looked grey. Umar organised relief, brought grain from Egypt and Syria, and is reported to have refused to eat butter or meat while the people suffered.

The plague of Amwas struck the army in Syria and killed many companions, including Abu Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah. Umar had travelled toward Syria but turned back when he learned of the plague, and he was criticised for it. He answered that he was fleeing from the decree of Allah to the decree of Allah. The episode is a classic case of his combination of faith and practical judgement.

These crises show a different side of Umar from the conqueror. He was an administrator who had to feed people, bury the dead and hold an empire together during natural disaster. The sources preserve his grief and his improvisations as well as his victories.

10. Administration, the Diwan and the Calendar

Umar is credited with establishing the diwan, a register of soldiers and stipends, and with organising the provinces of the empire. He appointed governors, judges and tax collectors, and he tried to supervise them through reports and inspections. The details are reported in the sources with some variation, but the direction is clear: the community was moving from a simple polity to a bureaucracy.

He also established the Hijri calendar, beginning with the year of the Prophet’s migration. The decision gave the community a shared sense of time and separated it from the local calendars of the conquered peoples. The sources differ on who first proposed it, but Umar is consistently associated with its adoption.

His administrative reforms were not always successful. The sources record disputes over land, taxation and the distribution of spoils. The conquered peoples were not passive; they negotiated, resisted and sometimes revolted. Umar’s system was an attempt to manage an empire that had grown faster than its institutions.

11. Justice, Patrol and the Poor

The stories of Umar’s justice are among the most beloved in Islamic literature. He is said to have patrolled the streets at night, carried food to a poor woman, and refused to let his son ride a horse that had been given as a gift. He judged between a Muslim and a non-Muslim according to the same standard, and he punished his own family when they overstepped.

Some of these stories are later embellishments, and the famous saying attributed to him — “If a mule stumbled in Iraq, I would fear that Allah would ask me about it” — is not a hadith and may be a later summary of his sense of responsibility. But the underlying picture is early: Umar was a caliph who understood his authority as a trust to be audited.

His justice had a harsh edge. He was severe with governors who lived in luxury, and he could be abrupt with petitioners. The sources do not hide his temper. The point is not that he was gentle; it is that his severity was directed at the powerful as well as the weak.

12. The Assassination

Umar was assassinated in 23 AH by Abu Lu’lu’a, a Persian slave of al-Mughirah ibn Shu‘bah, during the dawn prayer in the mosque of Madinah. The sources say that he had complained about a tax or a dispute with his master and that he stabbed Umar and several others before killing himself or being killed.

The motives are reported differently. Some sources present it as a personal grievance; others connect it to the political tensions of the conquest. What is clear is that the caliph died from the wounds and that the community was thrown into another succession crisis.

Umar appointed a committee of six companions to choose his successor, and he was buried beside the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr. His caliphate had lasted ten years and six months, and his death marked the end of the first phase of the early caliphate.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

Umar’s life teaches that justice is not an abstract ideal but a set of practices: registers, audits, courts, provisions for the poor and a refusal to let power become privilege. It also teaches that strength without self-criticism becomes tyranny. Umar was corrected by the Qur’an, by the Prophet ﷺ and by ordinary people, and the sources remember those corrections.

The sources also warn against romanticising the conquests. The early Islamic state was built through war, and war produces suffering, even when it is conducted according to rules. Umar’s caliphate should be studied with both the victories and the costs in view.

Finally, the reader should be careful with the huge body of later stories about Umar. Some are authentic, some are early reports of uncertain strength, and some are legends. The authentic hadith, the Qur’an and the earliest histories are the safest guides. The rest may be read as edification, but not as revelation.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith, the early historical reports and later narrative. It does not claim that every famous story about Umar is historically established, and it does not treat the later “Umar covenant” or the Alexandria library story as fact.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Why is Umar called al-Faruq?

Al-Faruq means “the one who distinguishes.” The title is associated with his role in distinguishing truth from falsehood, especially after his conversion. The sources give more than one explanation, and the title became one of his standard epithets.

2. Did Umar burn the library of Alexandria?

No early source establishes this. The story appears in later literature and is rejected by most historians. It should not be presented as a historical fact.

3. What was the “Umar covenant” with Jerusalem?

It is a later composite document. Its core — a treaty of protection in exchange for tribute — is consistent with early practice, but the detailed restrictions as they circulate today are not securely attributable to Umar.

4. How did Umar become caliph?

Abu Bakr nominated him after consulting the senior companions, and the community pledged allegiance to him. The nomination was not unanimous, but it was accepted and avoided a succession crisis.

5. Was Umar infallible?

No. The sources record his mistakes, his temper and his corrections by the Qur’an, the Prophet ﷺ and other companions. Sunni tradition honours him as a great caliph, not as a sinless figure.
