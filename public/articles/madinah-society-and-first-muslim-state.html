Madinah: Society, Law and the First Muslim Polity

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The Hijrah ended one phase of the Seerah and opened another. In Makkah the believers had been a persecuted minority without public authority. In Madinah they became responsible for a plural city: its defence, its disputes, its markets, its treaties and its poor. The change was not a change of message. It was a change of responsibility.

This article examines the institutions that took shape in the first years after the Hijrah: the document of Madinah, the mosque, the brotherhood between the Muhajirun and the Ansar, the change of qibla, the administration of law and the problem of the hypocrites. It also asks a question that popular writing often answers too quickly: was Madinah a “state”? The honest answer is that it was a new kind of polity, not a modern nation-state, and the difference matters.

Key takeaways
  • The Prophet ﷺ was invited to Yathrib as an arbitrator, not as a conqueror; his authority began with consent and reputation.
  • The document of Madinah was a compact between communities, not a modern constitution; its clauses and unity are discussed by historians.
  • The mosque was the centre of worship, teaching, arbitration, welfare and diplomacy at once.
  • The brotherhood of Muhajirun and Ansar was a practical welfare arrangement, later adjusted as the community grew.
  • The change of qibla, the fast of Ramadan and the zakat created a distinct public identity without erasing earlier prophets.
  • The hypocrites were tolerated outwardly; the Prophet ﷺ refused to run an inquisition into hearts.
  • “State” is a useful shorthand but anachronistic if it implies bureaucracy, standing armies and monopolised violence.

1. From Persecuted Community to Public Responsibility

Yathrib was an oasis settlement with a mixed population. Two large Arab tribes, the Aws and the Khazraj, had fought a long and exhausting feud, culminating in the battle of Bu’ath shortly before the Hijrah. Several Jewish groups — including Banu Qaynuqa, Banu al-Nadir and Banu Qurayzah — lived in the town and its outskirts, with their own alliances, forts and economic interests. There were also clients, craftsmen and people with no powerful clan behind them.

The Prophet ﷺ did not arrive as a conqueror. He came because delegations from the town had pledged allegiance at Aqabah and invited him to arbitrate their disputes. That detail is essential. His authority in Madinah began with consent, reputation and the expectation that he would be a fair judge. It was strengthened by revelation, by the loyalty of the Muhajirun and the Ansar, and by the practical success of his leadership, but it was not imposed by an invading army.

The first acts of the new leader were telling. He built a mosque, paired the emigrants with helpers, and concluded a written agreement among the communities of the town. These were not separate “religious” and “political” projects. In the seventh-century Arabian setting, worship, law, welfare and defence were aspects of one public order, and the Prophet ﷺ was the centre of that order.

2. The Document of Madinah

The agreement known as the document of Madinah (al-sahifah) is preserved in the Sirah of Ibn Ishaq through Ibn Hisham. It is a series of clauses rather than a single polished constitution, and scholars debate whether the text we have is one document, several documents, or a later compilation of agreements. Its broad content, however, is clear enough to describe.

The document declares that the believers and Muslims of Quraysh and Yathrib, together with those who follow them, are one ummah distinct from other people. It regulates blood-money between the clans, insists that a believer must not leave another believer burdened with debt or injustice, and provides that disputes are referred to Allah and to Muhammad ﷺ. It also addresses the Jewish groups: they are to be treated as allies, they retain their religion, they share in the costs of war when fighting alongside the Muslims, and they are not to be wronged or aided against.

“The Jews shall maintain their own religion and the Muslims theirs.”
Document of Madinah, in a widely used English translation

That sentence is often quoted, but the translation varies and the clause should not be read as a modern charter of secular pluralism. The agreement was a treaty of mutual obligation in a specific crisis. It assumed separate communities bound by kinship and religion, and it aimed at security, arbitration and common defence rather than at creating a neutral public square.

Historical caution: some clauses in the received text may be later additions, and the document’s relationship to the later expulsions of Jewish groups is disputed. This article treats it as an early compact whose general shape is reliable, not as a verbatim modern constitution.

3. The Mosque as the Centre of Public Life

The Prophet ﷺ built the mosque of Madinah on land that belonged to two orphans, purchased with his own wealth after the camel he was riding knelt there. The first structure was simple: mud-brick walls, palm trunks for pillars, a roof of palm branches. It was enlarged as the community grew, and the Prophet’s ﷺ own apartments adjoined it.

The mosque was never only a prayer hall. It was a school where the Qur’an was recited and explained; a courtroom where disputes were heard; a shelter where the homeless companions of the Suffah slept and studied; a treasury where donations were received and distributed; a hostel for delegations; and, when necessary, a planning room for defence. The call to prayer, the adhan, gave the town a public rhythm. Reports attribute its form to a dream seen by Abdullah ibn Zayd and confirmed by Umar, but the essential practice — a public call at the fixed times — is established in the Qur’an and the earliest practice.

This integration is easy to sentimentalise. It also created tensions. The mosque was a place of worship and of politics, and the Prophet ﷺ had to protect its sanctity while using it for public business. Later Islamic societies would separate these functions in different ways, sometimes sharply. Madinah represents the earliest model in which they were held together under prophetic authority.

4. The Brotherhood of Muhajirun and Ansar

The emigrants from Makkah had left homes, property and businesses. Many arrived with almost nothing. The Ansar — the helpers of Madinah — were asked to receive them, and the Prophet ﷺ established a formal bond of brotherhood (mu’akhah) between pairs of Muhajirun and Ansar. In some cases the helper offered to share his wealth and even his household; the Qur’an praises the Ansar for preferring others over themselves despite their own need:

“And those who settled in the home [of Madinah] and embraced the faith before them love those who emigrated to them and find not any want in their breasts of what they were given but give [them] preference over themselves, even though they were in need.”
Qur’an 59:9

The brotherhood was not a permanent redistribution of all property. It was an emergency welfare arrangement that allowed a displaced community to survive and integrate. Some reports suggest that the bond initially carried rights of inheritance, later modified by the Qur’anic law of inheritance and by the verse that “those related by blood are nearer to one another” (8:75; 33:6). The details are debated, but the direction is clear: the bond was adjusted as the emergency passed.

The most famous anecdote is that of Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf, who was offered half his brother’s wealth and replied that he needed only to be shown the market. He then traded and prospered. The story is preserved in the hadith collections and captures the ethos: help was real, but it was meant to enable independence, not create clients.

5. The Qibla, Ramadan and a Distinct Community

In the second year after the Hijrah, the qibla was changed from Jerusalem to the Sacred Mosque in Makkah. The Qur’an addresses the change directly, acknowledging that it would disturb some people and explaining that the direction is a test of obedience:

“We have certainly seen the turning of your face toward the sky, and We will surely turn you to a qiblah with which you will be pleased. So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram.”
Qur’an 2:144

The change did not repudiate the earlier prophets. The Qur’an calls the Muslims a “middle community” (2:143), witnesses to humanity, and it insists that righteousness is not the direction of the face but faith and good deeds (2:177). Jerusalem retained its sanctity; the Prophet ﷺ had been taken there on the night journey. But the public orientation of the community was now towards the house of Ibrahim, and that was a statement about the restoration of the religion of Ibrahim rather than the invention of a new one.

Ramadan fasting was prescribed in the same period, along with the zakat al-fitr and the public prayers of Eid. These practices gave the community a shared calendar and a shared discipline. They also distinguished the Muslims from their neighbours without requiring hostility towards them. The Qur’an permits eating the food of the People of the Book and marriage to their chaste women (5:5), and it insists on justice even towards those who oppose the believers (5:8). Identity and coexistence were held together.

6. Governing a Plural City

Madinah contained Muslims, Jews, polytheists and people with no strong religious commitment. The document of Madinah assumed that these groups would remain distinct and would deal with one another through agreements. The Prophet ﷺ did not require the Jewish groups to convert, and he did not abolish their internal religious authority. He adjudicated when disputes were brought to him and when the public peace was at stake.

Relations deteriorated over time. Banu Qaynuqa were besieged and expelled after a market dispute and an alleged breach of the agreement; Banu al-Nadir were expelled after a reported assassination plot against the Prophet ﷺ; Banu Qurayzah were dealt with after the siege of the Trench. These events are treated in the next article. What matters here is that the framework was political and contractual, and that the later conflicts were justified in the sources by treaty violation and security, not by religion as such.

Christians were also part of the wider picture. The delegation of Najran came to Madinah in the ninth year and debated with the Prophet ﷺ; the Qur’an addresses the encounter in Surah Al Imran and ultimately calls for a mutual invocation of curses (3:61) after argument fails. A treaty was concluded with the Najran Christians. The Qur’an’s stance towards the People of the Book combines theological disagreement with a command to argue in the best manner (16:125; 29:46).

7. Law, Arbitration and the Prophet as Judge

The Prophet ﷺ judged by revelation, but he also judged within the customs and capacities of the people. The Qur’an commands him to judge with justice and warns against following desire (4:105; 5:42–49). Where revelation was silent, he used consultation, analogy and the testimony of the parties. The famous principle that the burden of proof lies on the claimant and the oath on the one who denies is reported in the collections of al-Bayhaqi and others and became a foundation of Islamic procedure.

Justice was not tribal. The Qur’an commands believers to stand firm for justice, even against themselves and their relatives (4:135), and to be witnesses for Allah even against a people they hate (5:8). The Prophet ﷺ applied this in a case involving a woman of the Makhzum clan who had stolen. When Usama ibn Zayd tried to intercede, the Prophet ﷺ became angry and asked whether he was interceding in one of the punishments prescribed by Allah. He then addressed the people and said that the nations before them were destroyed because they punished the weak and let the noble go. The report is in Bukhari and Muslim.

Punishments were not applied casually. The Qur’an sets high thresholds of proof for sexual offences (24:4, 24:13), commands that suspicion not replace evidence (49:12), and insists that the accused be treated justly. The Prophet ﷺ warned against judging between people while angry, and he refused to decide a case in favour of a claimant merely because one party was eloquent. The picture is of a judge conscious that his rulings could be wrong and that the truth of hearts belongs to Allah.

8. The Hypocrites and the Limits of Belonging

Not everyone in Madinah who professed Islam was sincere. The Qur’an describes the munafiqun, the hypocrites, at length: they say they believe with their tongues while their hearts are elsewhere (2:8–20), they mock the believers, they withdraw from battle and they spread rumours. The most prominent figure was Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul, a chief of the Khazraj who had expected to be king of Yathrib before the Prophet’s ﷺ arrival.

The Prophet ﷺ did not create a system of religious police. He accepted the outward profession of Islam, judged on the evidence available, and left the hearts to Allah. When Abdullah ibn Ubayy’s intrigues were exposed, the Prophet ﷺ sometimes chose not to punish him, fearing that people would say Muhammad kills his own companions. When Ibn Ubayy died, the Prophet ﷺ prayed over him, despite Umar’s objection, until a verse was revealed that discouraged prayer over the hypocrites who had died in disbelief (9:84).

This restraint was a governance principle, not naivety. The Qur’an exposes hypocrisy and warns of its consequences, but it also leaves room for repentance and does not authorise a purge on suspicion. The episode of the slander against Aisha (24:11–20), in which the hypocrites spread a rumour and the Qur’an vindicated her, shows how destructive rumour can be and how the community was taught to demand witnesses rather than circulate accusation.

9. Economic Life, Zakat and the Market

Madinah’s economy rested on date palms, wells, livestock and trade. The Muhajirun had lost their Makkan businesses, and the Ansar owned most of the agricultural land. The early economy was therefore a transfer economy: the Ansar supported the emigrants until they could establish themselves, and the Prophet ﷺ distributed land and opportunities as they became available.

Zakat became an obligatory annual levy on wealth, with eight categories of recipients named in the Qur’an (9:60). It was not charity in the modern sense of voluntary giving; it was a right of the poor upon the wealth of the community. The Prophet ﷺ appointed collectors, set rates for different kinds of property, and warned against withholding it. Voluntary sadaqah, waqf endowments and the redemption of slaves supplemented the system.

The Qur’an also regulated the market. It condemns fraudulent weighing (83:1–3), commands fair measure (6:152), forbids riba (2:275–279) and prohibits the consumption of wealth by false means (4:29). The Prophet ﷺ established a market in Madinah and discouraged monopolistic practices and intercepting goods before they reached the market. The details were elaborated by later jurists, but the ethical direction was set in the Madinan period: exchange must be honest, and the weak must not be crushed by the strong.

10. Was Madinah a “State”?

Popular accounts often call the document of Madinah “the first written constitution” and describe the Prophet ﷺ as the founder of the first Islamic state. The phrases are memorable, but they can mislead. A modern state is normally defined by a territory, a sovereign authority, a monopoly on legitimate violence, a bureaucracy, a standing army, a tax system and a constitution in the legal sense. Madinah had some of these features and lacked others.

It had a defined territory and a defended sanctuary, a recognised authority, a written compact, a system of adjudication, a treasury fed by zakat and spoils, and the capacity to conclude treaties and wage war. It did not have a professional bureaucracy, a standing army, a police force, a codified legal code or a modern conception of impersonal sovereignty. Authority was personal and prophetic: people obeyed the Prophet ﷺ because he was the Messenger of Allah, because they had consented, and because his rule was just and effective.

The most accurate description is therefore something like a prophetic city-polity or a commonwealth of allied communities under a single arbiter. It was a real political order, not a vague religious club, but it was not a nation-state and it should not be used to bless modern institutions that it did not possess. The enduring principles are more important than the label: consent, covenant, justice, consultation, welfare and the protection of the weak.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the historical Madinan order from later theories of the caliphate and from modern state ideology. The sources describe a compact and a practice, not a political science textbook.

11. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for this period are:

  • The Qur’an — especially 2:143–150, 4:105, 5:8, 8:72–75, 9:60, 24:11–20, 33:6, 49:12–13 and 59:9.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — the mosque, the brotherhood, the adhan, the Makhzum woman, the slander of Aisha and the Prophet’s ﷺ judgments.
  • Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah — the text of the document of Madinah, the building of the mosque and the early expeditions.
  • Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk — the chronology and parallel reports, including weak ones.
  • Al-Waqidi, Kitab al-Maghazi — detailed but widely regarded as weak in hadith criticism; used only where corroborated.
  • Modern critical studies of the document, the economy of Yathrib and the social history of the Hijaz.

The document’s text survives through a literary chain, not as an excavated original. Its clause order and completeness are debated. The numbers of combatants, the exact dates and the details of individual houses are reported by later historians and are approximate. Where the sources differ, this article says so rather than smoothing the difference away.

Editorial note: this is an educational synthesis, not a critical edition. Reports from Ibn Hisham, al-Tabari and al-Waqidi are identified as such and are not presented as authentic hadith. The description of Madinah as a “state” is explicitly qualified.

12. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Did the Prophet ﷺ found the first Islamic state in Madinah?

He founded a political community with a written compact, a treasury, a court and a defence force, but it was not a modern state. It lacked a bureaucracy, a standing army and impersonal sovereignty. “Prophetic city-polity” is a more accurate description than “nation-state”.

2. Was the document of Madinah a constitution?

It is often called that, but it is better described as a treaty or compact between communities. It regulated blood-money, arbitration, mutual defence and the status of the Jewish groups. Its unity and dating are debated by historians.

3. Did the document grant religious freedom?

It allowed the Jewish groups to retain their religion and internal arrangements within a framework of alliance and mutual obligation. That is not the same as modern secular religious freedom, which rests on a different conception of the state.

4. Why did the Prophet ﷺ not punish the hypocrites?

He accepted outward Islam, judged on evidence and left hearts to Allah. He sometimes declined to punish Abdullah ibn Ubayy for prudential reasons, and the Qur’an warns against acting on suspicion. The community was not run as an inquisition.

5. How were the Muhajirun supported?

Through the bond of brotherhood with the Ansar, through shared households and wealth in the emergency, through work and trade, and later through zakat, spoils and land distribution. The aim was to enable independence, not permanent dependency.
