Contracts and Promises in Islam: Fulfilling the Covenant in Word and Deed

NoorDeen Editorial · 12 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

A contract is a promise with consequences. It may be a marriage, a job, a lease, a loan, a business partnership or a click on a website. Islamic law treats these commitments as moral acts, not merely technical instruments. The Qur'an opens Surah al-Ma'idah with a command that covers the whole of life: “O you who have believed, fulfil all contracts” (5:1).

This article explains the Quranic and Prophetic teaching on covenants, the basic elements of a valid contract, the discipline of writing things down, what to do when circumstances change, and how the same principles apply to digital agreements. It is an educational guide, not a fatwa or legal advice on a specific dispute.

Key takeaways
  • Fulfilling contracts is a Quranic obligation, not a courtesy.
  • Promise-keeping is presented as a sign of faith and hypocrisy as its opposite.
  • A valid contract needs clarity, capacity, mutual consent and a lawful subject matter.
  • Deferred obligations should be written and witnessed.
  • Conditions are generally binding unless they permit what is forbidden or forbid what is required.
  • Breach should be repaired honestly, through negotiation and, where needed, arbitration or law.

1. The Covenant in the Qur’an

The Qur'an uses several related words: 'aqd (contract, knot), 'ahd (covenant), mithaq (solemn pledge) and wa'd (promise). Together they describe a life in which words create obligations.

“And fulfil the covenant. Indeed, the covenant is that about which one will be questioned.”
Qur'an 17:34

The command is repeated with emphasis on the act of committing oneself: “And fulfil the covenant of Allah when you have taken it, and do not break oaths after their confirmation while you have made Allah, over you, a witness” (16:91). The Qur'an warns against treating oaths as a means of deceit between groups, and it uses the image of a woman who unravels her spun thread after it was strong (16:92).

Believers are described as those “who are to their trusts and their promises attentive” (23:8). The Qur'an also says that Allah loves the righteous who fulfil their promise (3:76), and it lists promise-keeping among the marks of true righteousness (2:177). These verses place reliability at the centre of religious identity. A person who prays and fasts but treats agreements as disposable has separated worship from character.

2. Why Promise-Keeping Matters

The Prophet ﷺ made promise-keeping one of the defining signs of a believer. He said: “The signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks he lies, when he makes a promise he breaks it, and when he is entrusted he betrays” (Sahih al-Bukhari 33; Sahih Muslim 59). The wording is stark because broken promises destroy the trust on which families, markets and communities depend.

Trust is also a form of amanah, a trust for which a person is accountable. The Qur'an commands that trusts be rendered to their owners and that judgment be given with justice (4:58), and it forbids betraying Allah, the Messenger or one's trusts knowingly (8:27). A contract is a trust placed in the hands of both parties.

The Prophet ﷺ was known as al-Amin, the trustworthy, before he received revelation. His opponents entrusted him with their goods even while opposing his message. That reputation was not a public-relations strategy; it was a life of consistent reliability. Keeping a promise is hardest when it becomes inconvenient, when the alternative is attractive, or when the other party can no longer enforce it.

3. What Makes a Contract Valid

Classical jurists analysed contracts through a set of conditions. The details differ between schools, but the core is widely shared:

  • Offer and acceptance — a clear expression of consent by both parties, in words, writing or a recognised digital equivalent.
  • Capacity — the parties are sane, adult and free to act, with special protections for minors and the vulnerable.
  • Known subject matter — what is being sold, hired or exchanged must be identifiable and deliverable.
  • Known price or consideration — the cost and terms must be clear enough to prevent dispute.
  • Lawful purpose — the contract cannot require or facilitate what Islam prohibits.
  • Absence of excessive uncertainty — gharar that one party could exploit is prohibited.

The Qur'an permits trade only by mutual consent (4:29) and forbids consuming wealth unjustly (2:188). The Prophet ﷺ forbade the sale of gharar (Sahih Muslim 1513) and the sale of what one does not possess (reported by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi). A contract that depends on a hidden gamble is not made valid by a confident handshake.

Formality is not an end in itself. A simple sale of bread is a contract, even without paper. But as the stakes, duration and uncertainty rise, the need for clarity rises with them.

4. Writing, Witnesses and Evidence

The longest verse in the Qur'an is devoted to documenting debt:

“O you who have believed, when you contract a debt for a specified term, write it down. And let a scribe write it between you in justice... And bring to witness two witnesses from among your men.”
Qur'an 2:282

The verse continues with detailed guidance on the scribe, the witnesses, the weak, the young and the traveller, and it ends by urging that testimony not be concealed. The purpose is not bureaucracy. It is to protect the creditor from loss, the debtor from false claims, and the community from disputes that poison relationships.

The next verse provides for a pledge when a scribe is unavailable on a journey, and it commands the one entrusted with a deposit to discharge the trust and fear Allah (2:283). Even in a low-documentation setting, the trust remains.

Modern equivalents include written contracts, receipts, bank records, emails, signed PDFs and auditable digital timestamps. A family loan of a few hundred pounds may seem too small for paperwork, but undocumented family loans are a common source of inheritance disputes and estrangement. Writing it down is an act of care, not distrust.

Practical note: a document is only useful if it is understandable and stored safely. Record the amount, the date, the repayment terms, the currency and the identities of the parties. Give a copy to each side.

5. Conditions, Penalties and Fairness

The Prophet ﷺ said that Muslims are bound by their conditions, except a condition that makes lawful what is forbidden or forbids what is lawful (reported by al-Tirmidhi and Abu Dawud). Conditions are therefore part of the contract, not an optional appendix.

This has consequences for modern clauses. A late-payment penalty that operates as a charge for the use of money over time raises riba concerns; a genuine administrative cost or a charitable penalty clause may be analysed differently. A non-compete clause that prevents a former employee from earning a living indefinitely may be unjust. A clause that waives a consumer's statutory rights may be legally ineffective and morally suspect.

Force majeure, price-adjustment clauses and termination rights are attempts to allocate risk. They are permissible when they are clear, negotiated and not used to trap the weaker party. The Qur'an's standard is justice, not the cleverness of the drafting: “Be persistently standing firm for Allah, witnesses in justice, and do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just” (5:8).

Where a contract is genuinely unfair, the response is not automatically to walk away after benefiting from it. Renegotiation, lawful termination, compensation and, where necessary, adjudication are the orderly routes.

6. Promises, Oaths and Expiation

Not every promise is a contract, but every promise carries moral weight. The Qur'an criticises those who say what they do not do (61:2–3) and commands truthfulness in speech (33:70). A repeated pattern of casual promises teaches others that one's word is worthless.

An oath is a stronger form. The Qur'an provides an expiation for breaking an oath: feeding ten needy people from the average of what one feeds one's family, or clothing them, or freeing a slave; and if one cannot afford that, fasting three days (5:89). The expiation does not make oath-breaking trivial. It is a remedy for a failure, not a licence.

Some promises are conditional on ability. If a person promises to help with a task and then falls seriously ill, the obligation is not absolute in the way a commercial debt is. The honest course is to communicate early, explain the change, and offer an alternative where possible. Silence is what turns a reasonable inability into a betrayal.

Promises made under pressure, or to do something unlawful, are not binding in the same way. A promise to commit fraud, to conceal a crime or to marry under coercion does not create a religious obligation to perform the sin. The general principle remains: no obedience to a creature in disobedience to Allah.

7. Breach, Dispute and Repair

Breach happens. The question is what follows. The Qur'an commands reconciliation between believers and warns against transgression (49:9), and it praises those who conduct their affairs by consultation (42:38). A dispute is not an excuse to abandon justice.

A workable sequence is:

  • Put the facts in writing, including dates, payments, messages and the specific clause relied on.
  • Speak directly and privately before involving a wider audience.
  • Separate the remedy you need from the punishment you feel like inflicting.
  • Consider mediation or arbitration by a trusted person with relevant expertise.
  • Use the courts where necessary; a legal right is not cancelled by a religious obligation to be fair.
  • If you are the party at fault, admit it, compensate promptly and do not hide behind procedure.

The Prophet ﷺ warned against the delay of a wealthy debtor (Sahih al-Bukhari 2404; Sahih Muslim 1564), and he praised ease in demanding repayment (Sahih al-Bukhari 2076). Those two reports together describe the balance: the creditor may insist on the right, but should not humiliate; the debtor must not evade. Apology without restitution is incomplete where money is involved, and restitution without apology may repair the account but not the relationship.

8. Digital Contracts and Clickwrap

Online agreements are contracts. A click, a tick box or a continued use of a service can create obligations, and the same requirements of clarity and consent apply. The medium changes the evidence, not the ethics.

Problems arise when consent is manufactured: pre-ticked boxes, buried renewal terms, a “free” service that takes ownership of user content, or a unilateral right to change the price or the rules at any time. The Qur'anic prohibition of deception and unjust consumption applies to a dark pattern as much as to a false statement in a shop.

Users also have duties. Reading the key terms, checking the renewal date, keeping confirmation emails and not sharing an account in breach of the licence are part of honest dealing. “I did not read it” may be true, but it does not always describe what a reasonable person should have done. Smart contracts raise new questions about code errors and irreversible transfers, but the technology cannot make an unjust bargain just.

9. A Practical Checklist

Before making or accepting a commitment:

  • Write down who owes what, to whom, by when, and in what currency.
  • Identify the conditions that would change the obligation, and state them clearly.
  • Check that the subject matter and the price are known, not left to one party's discretion.
  • Ask whether any term requires or facilitates something haram.
  • Keep copies of the signed document, receipts and relevant messages.
  • If circumstances change, communicate before the deadline rather than after it.
  • If you have broken a promise, repair what can be repaired and ask forgiveness for what cannot.

Reliability is a form of worship that costs little in the easy case and everything in the hard case. The Qur'an's standard is not a vague intention to do better. It is the fulfilment of what one has undertaken, with justice and mercy.

10. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Is a verbal contract valid?

In principle, yes, when the essential elements are clear and the parties consent. Writing is strongly recommended, and the Qur'an commands it for deferred debts (2:282), because it prevents dispute. Some contracts, such as marriage in many jurisdictions, require specific formalities under local law.

2. Do I have to keep a promise I made under pressure?

Pressure can affect whether consent was genuine. A promise to do something unlawful is not binding. For ordinary promises made reluctantly but freely, the general duty remains, though a person may explain the difficulty and seek release. Specific cases need a qualified scholar.

3. What if the other party breached first?

A breach may give you a right to terminate, claim compensation or withhold performance under the contract and the law. It does not give you a right to deceive or to take more than you are owed. Document the breach and seek advice before acting.

4. Are terms and conditions binding if I did not read them?

They can be, especially where the key terms were clearly presented and you chose to continue. But terms that are hidden, unfair or unlawful may be unenforceable, and a Muslim should not rely on a technicality to escape a fair obligation. Read the important clauses before agreeing.

5. What is the expiation for a broken oath?

The Qur'an specifies feeding ten needy people from the average of what you feed your family, or clothing them, or freeing a slave; if you cannot afford that, fasting three days (5:89). This is a remedy for a broken oath, not permission to break promises casually.

  • A qualified scholar or lawyer for the interpretation of a specific contract or dispute.
