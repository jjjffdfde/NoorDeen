Yusuf: Beauty, Chastity and Forgiveness

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Surah Yusuf is the only surah in the Qur’an that tells a single prophet’s story from beginning to end, and the Qur’an calls it “the best of stories” (12:3). It has a plot, a cast, reversals, a trial, a rise to power and a reconciliation. It is also a sustained study of patience, chastity, family jealousy, political responsibility and forgiveness.

The story is popular, and popularity has cost it accuracy. The name Zulaykha, the seven-year famine, the exact years in prison and the romantic elaborations of later literature are not in the Qur’an. This article follows the Qur’anic narrative closely, keeps the hadith in their proper place, and separates the text from the legend.

Key takeaways
  • Yusuf’s dream of eleven stars, the sun and the moon frames the surah and is fulfilled at its end (12:4; 12:100).
  • The brothers’ jealousy leads to his being cast into a well and sold into slavery in Egypt.
  • He refuses the advances of his master’s wife, is imprisoned unjustly, and interprets dreams in prison.
  • He interprets the king’s dream, is released, and is appointed over the storehouses of the land.
  • The cup is placed in Binyamin’s bag to keep him, and the brothers return to Ya’qub in grief.
  • Yusuf forgives his brothers with the words of 12:92, which the Prophet ﷺ later recited at the conquest of Makkah.
  • The name Zulaykha, the seven-year famine and the exact prison term are later tradition, not Qur’anic text.

1. Why Yusuf Matters

Surah Yusuf is a complete narrative in a single surah, and it is placed in the Qur’an among the surahs that address the Prophet ﷺ in a time of difficulty. The opening and closing verses make the purpose explicit: “We relate to you the best of stories” (12:3) and “There was certainly in their stories a lesson for those of understanding” (12:111). The story is consolation and instruction at once.

The surah is also a study in how a believer lives inside a hostile system. Yusuf is a slave, a prisoner and then a minister. He does not withdraw from public life, and he does not compromise his faith. He interprets dreams, administers a famine, saves a nation and forgives the brothers who wronged him. The Qur’an presents political responsibility as a field of service, not a temptation to be avoided.

Finally, the surah is a masterpiece of narrative technique. It withholds information, shifts point of view, uses clothing as a recurring symbol, and lets the reader know more than the characters. The Qur’an’s claim that it is “the best of stories” is not only a theological judgement; it is a literary one.

2. “The Best of Stories”: Structure and Setting

The surah moves through a series of settings: the family home in Canaan, the well, the caravan, the Egyptian household, the prison, the royal court, the storehouses and the final reunion. Each setting tests Yusuf in a different way, and each is resolved before the next begins. The movement is from the private to the public, from the victim to the minister.

The recurring symbols are clothing and dreams. The shirt is stained with false blood (12:18), torn from the back (12:25–28), sent to restore Ya’qub’s sight (12:93), and finally the shirt of honour. Dreams appear at the beginning, in prison and at the court, and the final scene is the interpretation of the first dream. The surah is built so that the ending answers the beginning.

The Qur’an also uses the brothers as a collective character. They speak with one voice, plot together, accuse one another and finally confess. Their change is not sudden; it is the cumulative effect of famine, guilt and the generosity of the brother they wronged. The surah is as much about their repentance as about Yusuf’s patience.

3. The Dream and the Brothers’ Jealousy

The surah opens with a boy’s dream: “O my father, indeed I have seen eleven stars and the sun and the moon prostrating to me” (12:4). Ya’qub recognises its significance and warns him: “O my son, do not relate your vision to your brothers, lest they devise a plan against you. Indeed, Satan is an enemy to man, a manifest one” (12:5). The father’s caution is not superstition; it is an understanding of human envy.

The brothers’ jealousy is stated in their own words: “When they said, ‘Joseph and his brother are more beloved to our father than we, while we are a clan. Indeed, our father is in clear error’” (12:8). They propose to kill him or cast him out, and one of them suggests the well instead: “Do not kill Joseph, but throw him into the bottom of the well; some caravan will pick him up” (12:10).

The plan is carried out with a cruelty that the Qur’an does not soften. They ask their father to send Yusuf with them, promise to protect him, and then throw him into the well. The Qur’an inserts a divine reassurance: “And We inspired to him, ‘You will surely inform them of this affair of theirs while they do not perceive’” (12:15). The boy in the well is given the end of the story before he lives it.

4. The Well, the Caravan and Egypt

The brothers return to their father at night, weeping, with the shirt stained with false blood: “They said, ‘O our father, indeed we went racing each other and left Joseph with our possessions, and a wolf ate him’” (12:17). Ya’qub does not believe them. “He said, ‘Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so patience is most fitting. And Allah is the one sought for help against that which you describe’” (12:18).

The caravan finds Yusuf in the well and sells him: “And they sold him for a reduced price — a few dirhams — and they were, concerning him, of those content with little” (12:20). The Qur’an’s detail is precise and unromantic. A human being is priced at a few coins. The verse is a quiet indictment of the slave trade that the later story embellishes with golden caravans.

In Egypt, “the one who bought him” tells his wife to treat him well, and the Qur’an notes that “We established Joseph in the land that We might teach him the interpretation of events” (12:21). The Egyptian household becomes the next test. The Qur’an calls the buyer’s wife “the one in whose house he was” (12:23), not by name. The name Zulaykha comes from later literature.

5. Temptation, Chastity and Prison

The temptation scene is one of the most discussed passages in the Qur’an. “And she, in whose house he was, sought to seduce him. She closed the doors and said, ‘Come, you.’ He said, ‘I seek refuge in Allah. Indeed, he is my master, who has made good my residence. Indeed, wrongdoers will not succeed’” (12:23). The refusal is immediate, and it is framed as a refuge in God rather than a calculation of consequences.

The Qur’an then describes the race to the door and the shirt torn from the back (12:25). A witness from her family suggests that if the shirt is torn from the front she is telling the truth, and if from the back he is (12:26–27). The shirt becomes evidence, and the husband concludes that it is “of your plan” (12:28). Yusuf asks to be imprisoned rather than return to the situation: “My Lord, prison is more to my liking than that to which they invite me” (12:33).

In prison he meets two young men and interprets their dreams: one will pour wine for his master, the other will be crucified and the birds will eat from his head (12:36–41). He uses the occasion to preach: “O my two companions of prison, are separate lords better or Allah, the One, the Prevailing?” (12:39). The scene shows that da‘wah is not confined to free men or comfortable places.

He asks the one he believes will be released to remember him, but “Satan made him forget the mention [to] his master, and he remained in prison several years” (12:42). The Qur’an does not blame Yusuf for the request, but it records the delay honestly. The servant of God can do everything right and still wait.

6. Dreams, Famine and the Rise

The king’s dream is the turning point: seven fat cows eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and seven dry (12:43). The court’s interpreters fail, and the released prisoner remembers Yusuf. Yusuf interprets: seven years of plenty followed by seven years of famine, with a plan to store grain and keep seed for the next planting (12:47–49).

The king summons him, but Yusuf refuses to leave prison until his innocence is established: “Return to your master and ask him, ‘What is the case of the women who cut their hands? Indeed, my Lord is Knowing of their plan’” (12:50). The investigation vindicates him, and the wife confesses: “Now the truth has become evident. I attempted to seduce him, and he is of the truthful” (12:51).

Only then does Yusuf accept office: “Appoint me over the storehouses of the land. Indeed, I will be a knowing guardian” (12:55). The verse is a model of public responsibility: he asks for a position because he has the competence for it, and he uses it to serve. The Qur’an says that “We established Joseph in the land, to settle therein wherever he willed” (12:56).

The seven-year famine is not stated in the Qur’an as a fixed number of years for the famine itself; the text says seven years of plenty and then “a hard year” or years of scarcity (12:48–49). The popular “seven years of famine” is an inference. The exact chronology is not the point; the administration and the mercy are.

7. The Brothers Return: Binyamin and the Cup

The famine brings the brothers to Egypt, and the surah’s most delicate scenes follow. Yusuf recognises them, but they do not recognise him (12:58). He supplies them generously and asks them to bring their brother: “Bring me a brother of yours from your father” (12:59). He takes a pledge from them and warns them that if they do not return, he will not measure grain for them (12:63–66).

When they return with Binyamin, Yusuf reveals himself to him: “Indeed, I am your brother, so do not despair over what they used to do” (12:69). He then has a drinking cup placed in Binyamin’s bag. When the caravan leaves, a herald announces the theft, and the brothers deny it. The cup is found, and Binyamin is detained according to the law of the land (12:70–76).

The brothers are devastated, not because they love Binyamin less but because they fear their father. They offer themselves in his place, and Yusuf refuses: “Allah forbid that we take other than him with whom we found our property. Indeed, if we did so, we would be wrongdoers” (12:79). The verse is a statement of legal integrity: the innocent are not punished for the guilty.

The brothers return to Ya’qub with the news, and he turns away and says, “O my sons, go and find out about Joseph and his brother and despair not of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, no one despairs of the mercy of Allah except the disbelieving people” (12:87). The father’s hope is the theological heart of the surah.

8. “No Blame Upon You Today”: Forgiveness and Power

The reconciliation begins with a question: “He said, ‘Do you know what you did with Joseph and his brother when you were ignorant?’ They said, ‘Are you indeed Joseph?’ He said, ‘I am Joseph, and this is my brother. Allah has certainly favoured us. Indeed, he who fears Allah and is patient, then indeed, Allah does not allow the reward of the doers of good to be lost’” (12:89–90).

The brothers confess: “By Allah, certainly has Allah preferred you over us, and indeed, we have been sinners” (12:91). Yusuf’s answer is one of the most famous sentences in the Qur’an: “No blame will there be upon you today. Allah will forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful” (12:92).

The verse is remarkable because Yusuf has the power to punish. He is the minister of Egypt, and his brothers are foreigners in his hands. He chooses forgiveness not from weakness but from strength. The Prophet ﷺ recited the same verse at the conquest of Makkah, when he had the power to destroy the city that had persecuted him. The parallel is deliberate in the Sirah and it is one of the clearest links between the two stories.

The forgiveness is not amnesia. Yusuf says, “No blame upon you today,” and then he acts to save the family from famine: “Take this, my shirt, and cast it over the face of my father; he will become seeing. And bring me your family, all together” (12:93). Forgiveness leads to restoration, not to a polite distance.

9. Ya’qub’s Patience and the Fulfilment of the Dream

Ya’qub’s patience runs through the surah as a counterpoint to Yusuf’s. He refuses to believe the false blood, he refuses to believe the loss of Binyamin, and he says, “So patience is most fitting. Perhaps Allah will bring them to me all together. Indeed, it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise” (12:83). He also says, “I only complain of my suffering and grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know” (12:86).

The shirt restores his sight, and he says, “Indeed, I find the scent of Joseph” (12:94). The family travels to Egypt, and the dream is fulfilled: “And he raised his parents upon the throne, and they fell in prostration to him. And he said, ‘O my father, this is the interpretation of my vision from before. My Lord has made it true’” (12:100).

The prostration of the family is not worship of Yusuf. The Qur’an is explicit that worship belongs to Allah alone, and the classical commentators explain the gesture as a greeting or a recognition of his rank, permitted in the law of that time. The verse closes with Yusuf’s prayer: “My Lord, You have given me [something] of sovereignty and taught me of the interpretation of dreams. Creator of the heavens and earth, You are my protector in this world and the Hereafter. Cause me to die a Muslim and join me with the righteous” (12:101).

The surah ends by returning to the Prophet ﷺ and to the reader: “That is from the news of the unseen which We reveal to you, [O Muhammad]. And you were not with them when they decided their plan while they were plotting” (12:102). The story is presented as revelation, and the proof of its truth is its coherence and its agreement with what the earlier scriptures report.

10. Yusuf in Hadith and Later Literature

The authentic hadith honour Yusuf as one of the noble line: “The noble son of the noble son of the noble son of the noble: Yusuf son of Ya’qub son of Ishaq son of Ibrahim” (Sahih al-Bukhari). The report establishes his lineage and his rank among the prophets, and it is a reminder that the Qur’anic story is not an isolated tale.

The Prophet ﷺ also recited 12:92 at the conquest of Makkah, as the Sirah reports, and the verse became the model of prophetic amnesty. The connection is one of the most important in the Seerah: the wronged man who becomes powerful chooses mercy.

The later literature adds the name Zulaykha, the name of the Egyptian minister (Qitfir in some reports), the exact number of years in prison, the age of Yusuf at each stage, the romantic sequel in which Zulaykha marries him, and the detailed description of his beauty. None of this is in the Qur’an or the sound hadith. Some of it is edifying poetry; some of it is fantasy. It should be labelled as literature, not as revelation.

Source caution: the Qur’an does not name the wife of the Egyptian minister, does not give the length of Yusuf’s imprisonment and does not describe his physical beauty. Those details come from later narrative and poetry.

11. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for this article are:

  • The Qur’an — Surah Yusuf (12:1–111) in full, with 12:92 as the central verse on forgiveness.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari — the lineage of Yusuf and the virtues of the prophets.
  • Sahih Muslim — parallel reports on the prophets and the lessons of patience.
  • Al-Tabari, Jami‘ al-Bayan — the classical commentary and its narrative material.
  • Ibn Kathir, Tafsir and al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah — the later synthesis with grading.
  • Al-Qurtubi, al-Jami‘ li Ahkam al-Qur’an — legal and theological discussion.

Surah Yusuf is a single continuous narrative, which makes it easier to follow than the scattered Adam and Ibrahim passages. But the surah is still not a chronicle: it does not give dates, ages or the names of most characters. The later literature supplies those details, and this article marks them as later.

Editorial note: this is an educational synthesis, not a critical edition. The article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith and later narrative, and it does not present legend as revelation.

12. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Why is Surah Yusuf called the best of stories?

The Qur’an itself uses the phrase in 12:3. The surah tells a complete narrative from dream to fulfilment, with a coherent plot, a moral arc and a resolution. The description is both literary and theological: the story is beautifully told and it teaches patience, chastity and forgiveness.

2. Did Yusuf marry Zulaykha?

The Qur’an does not say. The name Zulaykha and the story of her later marriage to Yusuf come from later literature, not from the Qur’an or an authentic hadith. The Qur’an mentions only that she confessed his innocence (12:51).

3. How long was Yusuf in prison?

The Qur’an says “several years” (12:42) without giving a number. The figure of seven or twelve years comes from later tradition. The exact length is not established.

4. Why did Yusuf not reveal himself to his brothers earlier?

The Qur’an does not explain his motive in detail. The narrative shows him testing them, providing for them and waiting for the right moment. The delay allows the brothers to confront their guilt and the family to be reunited in a way that fulfils the dream.

5. Was Yusuf’s forgiveness unconditional?

He says, “No blame will there be upon you today” (12:92) before any request for pardon. The forgiveness is freely given. It is followed by practical restoration: he sends his shirt to heal his father’s sight and asks for the whole family to come to Egypt.
