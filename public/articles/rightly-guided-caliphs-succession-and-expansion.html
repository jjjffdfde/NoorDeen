The Rightly Guided Caliphs: Succession, Expansion and the First Fitna

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The first four caliphs after the Prophet ﷺ — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali — ruled for fewer than thirty years, yet they created the political vocabulary that Muslims have argued about ever since. In that generation a small Arabian community became an empire stretching from the Atlas Mountains to the Oxus, and the questions raised by that transformation — who may rule, how he is chosen, what limits his power, how wealth is distributed — have never gone away.

The four are called al-khulafa al-rashidun, the rightly guided caliphs. The label is a judgement of piety and of fidelity to the prophetic model, not a description of a constitutional system. They did not rule through a written constitution, a standing bureaucracy or a fixed law of succession. They improvised under pressure, and their improvisations became precedents that later dynasties cited or ignored.

This article treats the Rashidun as an institution rather than repeating the four biographies already published on this site. It follows the early historical and biographical sources, marks where those sources are late or tendentious, and refuses to turn a civil war into a simple morality play. The first fitna was a tragedy that the community survived, not a template for later conflict.

Key takeaways
  • The Rashidun caliphate lasted from 632 to 661 CE: Abu Bakr (632–634), Umar (634–644), Uthman (644–656) and Ali (656–661).
  • Abu Bakr was chosen at the Saqifa of the Banu Sa‘ida by a group of the Ansar and Muhajirun; the bay‘ah was real but not a general election.
  • The ridda wars were not a single war of apostasy: some tribes rejected the caliphate, and some refused the zakat while professing Islam.
  • Umar built the institutions of empire: the garrison towns, the diwan, the Hijri calendar and a system of stipends.
  • Uthman standardised the Qur’anic codex and sent copies to the provinces; the decision is accepted by the community even where the politics of his caliphate are disputed.
  • Ali’s caliphate was consumed by the first fitna: the Battle of the Camel, Siffin, the arbitration and the Kharijite secession.
  • The caliphate was not a papacy: the caliphs governed, judged and led prayer, but they did not define doctrine or claim infallibility.
  • Later Sunni and Shi‘i memories of this period differ sharply, and both are shaped by the conflicts of later centuries.

1. Why the Rashidun Matter

The Rashidun matter because they are the only period in which the community was governed by people who had known the Prophet ﷺ intimately and who had no inherited claim to power. Abu Bakr was a merchant and a close friend. Umar was a former wrestler and a stern administrator. Uthman was a wealthy trader from a leading clan. Ali was the Prophet’s cousin and son-in-law. None of them was a king, and none of them founded a dynasty.

That combination — proximity to the Prophet ﷺ, absence of dynastic legitimacy and the pressures of rapid conquest — produced both the achievements and the crises of the period. The achievements were administrative and military: the unification of Arabia, the defeat of the Sasanian empire, the conquest of Syria and Egypt, the creation of a treasury and a calendar. The crises were political: the murder of two caliphs, a civil war and the emergence of a dissident faction that would outlast the caliphate itself.

2. The Saqifa and the First Bay‘ah

When the Prophet ﷺ died in 11 AH / 632 CE, he left no written instruction naming a successor. The community faced an immediate practical problem: the Ansar gathered at the Saqifa of the Banu Sa‘ida to discuss a leader, and the Muhajirun arrived to insist that the leader should come from Quraysh. Abu Bakr and Umar were among them. After debate, Abu Bakr was given the bay‘ah, first by those present and then in the mosque.

The sources do not describe a unanimous assembly. Sa‘d ibn Ubada, the Ansar leader, was ill and later withdrew from public life. Ali was not present at the Saqifa; he was occupied with the Prophet’s burial, and his own bay‘ah is reported to have come later. These facts are recorded in the earliest sources, and they explain why the event became a focus of later dispute.

The bay‘ah was nonetheless a real act of consent by the community’s most influential figures. It was not a modern election, and it was not a hereditary succession. It was a negotiated choice made under the pressure of a possible split between the Muhajirun and the Ansar. The distinction matters: the Rashidun caliphate rested on a practice of consultation and acclamation, not on a fixed rule.

Source caution. The Saqifa narratives are preserved in Ibn Ishaq, al-Tabari and the biographical works. The broad outline is agreed; the speeches are later compositions, and the sequence of the bay‘ah reports varies. The article does not claim that the event was uncontested.

3. Abu Bakr and the Consolidation of the Community

Abu Bakr’s caliphate lasted barely two years, and it was almost entirely consumed by the question of survival. Several Arab tribes withheld the zakat or rejected the authority of Madinah after the Prophet’s death. Abu Bakr refused to separate the prayer from the zakat, and he sent armies to restore the authority of the centre. The campaigns are conventionally called the ridda wars, the wars of apostasy.

That label flattens a complex reality. Some tribes had genuinely renounced Islam and followed rival prophets such as Musaylima. Others professed Islam but refused to pay the zakat to a caliph they did not accept. Abu Bakr treated both as rebellion against the community, and his firmness preserved the unity of the state. Whether every campaign was a war of religion or a war of politics is a question the sources allow us to ask.

The most consequential decision of his caliphate came after the Battle of Yamama, where many reciters of the Qur’an were killed. Umar urged the caliph to collect the Qur’an into a single written corpus, and Abu Bakr commissioned Zayd ibn Thabit to do it. The sheets were kept with the caliph and later with Hafsah, Umar’s daughter. That collection became the basis of the standard codex produced under Uthman.

Source caution. The ridda campaigns are reported in al-Tabari and al-Baladhuri, but the most detailed accounts depend on Sayf ibn Umar, whose reliability was criticised by hadith scholars. The outline is secure; the battle speeches and numbers are not.

4. Umar and the Machinery of Empire

Umar inherited a state that was expanding faster than its institutions. His caliphate (634–644) turned a victorious army into an administration. He founded or developed the garrison towns of Kufa, Basra and Fustat, where soldiers could settle with their families rather than dissolving into the conquered populations. He established the diwan, a register of those entitled to stipends from the treasury, and he organised the distribution of the fay’.

He also gave the community a calendar. The Hijri era, beginning with the Prophet’s migration, was adopted during his caliphate and remains the Islamic lunar reckoning. He appointed and dismissed governors with unusual severity, and he insisted that officials be accessible to complainants. The stories of his night patrols and his refusal of luxury are part of the period’s moral memory, and they express a real expectation that the ruler should live like the ruled.

Source caution. The details of the diwan and the stipend scales are reported by al-Baladhuri and later administrative writers. The figures should be read as later reconstruction, not as contemporary budget documents.

5. Uthman and the Standard Codex

Uthman’s caliphate (644–656) is remembered for two things: the standardisation of the Qur’anic text and the political crisis that ended in his murder. The two are connected, because both involved the question of how far the caliph could act on behalf of the whole community.

As the conquests spread, Muslims in different provinces recited the text with different dialectal conventions. Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman warned the caliph that the differences could become a source of conflict. Uthman commissioned a committee, again led by Zayd ibn Thabit, to produce a standard codex from Abu Bakr’s collection. Copies were sent to the major centres, and variant personal codices were suppressed.

The decision was controversial at the time and has been accepted by the community since. It preserved the unity of the text, but it also centralised authority over the text in the caliph’s hands. Critics of Uthman accused him of favouring his relatives and of appointing young men from the Umayyad clan to key posts. Some of those accusations are corroborated by the sources; others are polemical. In 656 a group of rebels besieged his house in Madinah and killed him while he was reciting the Qur’an.

Source caution. The reports of Uthman’s codex are preserved in al-Bukhari and in the biographical works. The accusations against his administration come largely from sources written under the Abbasids, who had reasons to portray the Umayyads badly. They are not therefore false, but they require care.

6. Ali and the First Fitna

Ali was given the bay‘ah in Madinah after Uthman’s death, and his caliphate was immediately contested. The Prophet’s widow A’ishah, Talhah and al-Zubayr demanded that the killers of Uthman be punished before Ali’s authority was recognised. Ali argued that he needed time to establish control. The dispute ended in the Battle of the Camel in 656, near Basra, where Talhah and al-Zubayr were killed and A’ishah’s forces were defeated.

The deeper conflict was with Mu‘awiya, the governor of Syria and a relative of Uthman, who refused to give the bay‘ah until the murder was avenged. The two armies met at Siffin in 657. After weeks of skirmishing, the Syrians called for arbitration, and a group of Ali’s own supporters forced him to accept it. The arbitration was inconclusive, and a faction rejected it as a sin against God. That faction, the Kharijites, withdrew from Ali’s camp and were defeated at Nahrawan in 658.

In 661 a Kharijite named Ibn Muljam stabbed Ali in the mosque of Kufa. His caliphate had lasted five years, most of them at war with other Muslims. The first fitna ended with Mu‘awiya as the undisputed ruler, and the question of how the community should be governed had been answered by force rather than by consultation.

Source caution. The narratives of the Camel, Siffin and Nahrawan are preserved in al-Tabari, Ibn al-Athir and the biographical works. The speeches and letters attributed to the protagonists are literary compositions of a later date, however early their core may be.

7. How the Community Chose Its Leaders

The Rashidun were chosen in four different ways. Abu Bakr was chosen at the Saqifa by acclamation. Umar was nominated by Abu Bakr and confirmed by the community. Uthman was chosen by a committee of six appointed by Umar. Ali was given the bay‘ah in Madinah after Uthman’s death, though his authority was never accepted by the whole community.

These four procedures do not add up to a single theory of succession. Later jurists tried to derive one, and the results differed: some emphasised the consensus of the qualified, others the nomination of the previous caliph, others the role of the Quraysh. The historical record is more modest. The community improvised, and each improvisation became a precedent that could be cited or ignored.

The Qur’an speaks of consultation in the conduct of affairs (42:38) and commands the Prophet ﷺ to consult the believers (3:159), but it does not prescribe a mechanism for choosing a ruler. The famous hadith that urges obedience to the Prophet’s Sunnah and the Sunnah of the rightly guided caliphs is a guide to loyalty, not a constitutional manual. The gap between the text and the institution is one reason the question has remained open.

8. Taxation, Land and the Garrison Towns

The conquests posed an economic question that the Qur’an addresses in principle but not in detail. The fay’ — property taken without fighting — is assigned in Surah al-Hashr to God, the Messenger, the near relatives, the orphans, the poor and the wayfarer, “so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich from among you” (59:7). How that principle should be applied to whole provinces was left to the caliphs.

Umar decided that conquered agricultural land should not be divided among the fighters but should remain with its cultivators, who would pay kharaj, a land tax, while non-Muslim subjects would pay jizya, a poll tax, in return for protection and exemption from military service. The decision kept the army from becoming a landed aristocracy and preserved the tax base of the state. It was not universally popular; some companions argued that the fighters had a right to the land.

Source caution. The tax categories were not fixed in the Qur’an and were elaborated over centuries. The article describes the early practice, not the fully developed fiqh of kharaj and jizya, which differs between schools.

9. The Conquests and Their Terms

The speed of the conquests is one of the most remarkable facts of the period. Within a generation the Muslims defeated the Sasanian empire, took Syria and Egypt from Byzantium, and raided into North Africa and the Iranian plateau. The decisive battles — Yarmuk in 636, al-Qadisiyya in 636 or 637, the fall of Ctesiphon, the surrender of Jerusalem in 637 or 638, the conquest of Egypt between 639 and 642 — became foundational memories.

The terms of surrender varied. Some cities were taken by force; others capitulated and received treaties that guaranteed their lives, property and places of worship in return for tribute. The treaty of Jerusalem, attributed to Umar, is the most famous example, though its text survives in later recensions. The conquered populations were not forced to convert. Conversion was gradual, and in some regions it took centuries.

Source caution. The conquest narratives are preserved in al-Baladhuri, al-Tabari and Ibn Abd al-Hakam, all writing well after the events. The treaties are later recensions, and the numbers of combatants are unreliable.

10. The Rashidun in Sunni and Shi‘i Memory

Sunni tradition honours all four caliphs and places them in the order of their rule. The hadith of the ten promised Paradise, reported in al-Tirmidhi and other collections, includes Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali, and the phrase “the rightly guided caliphs” became a standard way of referring to the first four. Their period is treated as the model age of the community.

Shi‘i tradition honours Ali and the Prophet’s family above the other caliphs and regards the first three caliphates as illegitimate. The events of the Saqifa, the death of Fatima, the murder of Uthman and the wars of Ali are read through a theology of rightful succession. This is not a minor difference of emphasis; it is one of the deepest divisions in Islamic history.

Editorial note: this article follows the Sunni ordering and valuation of the caliphs while describing the Shi‘i position as a serious theological and historical tradition, not as a political error.

11. The First Fitna and Its Legacy

The first fitna was the community’s first experience of Muslims killing Muslims on a large scale. The Prophet ﷺ had warned that sedition would come, and the hadith literature contains many warnings against taking sides in it. The generation that fought at the Camel and Siffin was the generation that had accompanied the Prophet ﷺ, and that fact made the conflict unbearable for many believers.

The political legacy was the Umayyad dynasty. Mu‘awiya moved the capital to Damascus, instituted a hereditary succession and built a state that lasted for nearly a century. The religious legacy was more divided. The Kharijites became a permanent dissident tradition, famous for piety and for excommunication. The Shi‘a became a distinct community with its own law, theology and commemorations. The Sunnis developed a political ethic of obedience to the ruler and patience under injustice, qualified by the duty to speak the truth.

12. What the Rashidun Did Not Invent

The Rashidun did not invent the caliphate as a fully formed institution, and they did not leave a constitutional blueprint. They inherited the Prophet’s authority without his prophethood, and they had to improvise the difference. The word khalifa means successor or deputy, and the early caliphs were careful to present themselves as deputies of the Prophet ﷺ and trustees of the community, not as sources of revelation.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

The Rashidun period teaches three lessons. The first is the importance of consultation: the community’s best decisions came when its leading figures deliberated, and its worst crises came when deliberation collapsed into faction. The second is the danger of idealisation: the period was not a golden age of harmony, and treating it as one makes it useless as a model. The third is the possibility of reform: Umar changed the tax system, Uthman standardised the text and Ali tried to restore a shattered polity.

The limits of the sources are severe. Al-Tabari wrote three centuries after the events and collected every report he could find, including contradictory ones. Al-Baladhuri and Ibn Abd al-Hakam wrote administrative and regional histories with their own agendas. The biographical works preserve memory and piety as much as chronology. A modern reader can reconstruct the outline of the period with confidence, but not the detail of every speech and battle.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the sound hadith, the early chronicles and later polemic. It does not claim that every detail of the period is settled, and it does not use the first fitna to justify later sectarian violence.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Who were the Rightly Guided Caliphs?

They are Abu Bakr (632–634), Umar (634–644), Uthman (644–656) and Ali (656–661), the first four caliphs after the Prophet ﷺ. Sunni tradition calls them al-khulafa al-rashidun because of their proximity to the Prophet ﷺ and their role in establishing the community.

2. How was the first caliph chosen?

Abu Bakr was chosen at the Saqifa of the Banu Sa‘ida by the leading Muhajirun and Ansar present, and then given the bay‘ah in the mosque. It was a negotiated choice under pressure, not a general election, and Ali’s bay‘ah is reported to have come later.

3. What were the ridda wars?

They were the campaigns of 632–633 against tribes that rejected the authority of Madinah after the Prophet’s death. Some had renounced Islam; others professed Islam but refused to pay the zakat. Abu Bakr treated both as rebellion and restored the unity of the state.

4. Why did Uthman standardise the Qur’an?

Because Muslims in different provinces were reciting the text with different dialectal conventions, and Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman warned that the differences could cause conflict. Uthman commissioned a standard codex from Abu Bakr’s collection and sent copies to the major centres.

5. What caused the first fitna?

The murder of Uthman in 656 created a dispute over punishment and authority. Ali’s caliphate was contested by A’ishah, Talhah and al-Zubayr at the Camel, and by Mu‘awiya at Siffin. The arbitration that followed split Ali’s camp and produced the Kharijite secession.

15. Sources & Further Reading

This article cites the Qur'an by surah and verse, and identifies hadith by collection and, where verified, book or number. Where scholars differ, the difference is stated rather than resolved by assertion.

  • The Qur’an — especially 42:38 on consultation, 3:159 on consulting the believers, 4:59 on obedience to those in authority and 59:7 on the distribution of fay’. Translations adapted from Saheeh International unless stated.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — the hadith on the best generation and the reports on the caliphs’ virtues.
  • Sunan Abi Dawud and Jami‘ at-Tirmidhi — the report urging adherence to the Sunnah of the rightly guided caliphs, graded hasan by several scholars.
  • Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk — the Saqifa, the ridda wars, the conquests and the first fitna, with full chains.
  • Al-Baladhuri, Futuh al-Buldan — the treaties, the garrison towns and the tax arrangements.
  • Ibn Abd al-Hakam, Futuh Misr wa Akhbaruha — the conquest of Egypt and North Africa.
  • Ibn Ishaq, Sirat Rasul Allah, as transmitted by Ibn Hisham — the Saqifa and the Prophet’s final instructions.
  • Ibn Kathir, al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah, and al-Dhahabi, Siyar A‘lam al-Nubala — later synthesis with grading and biographical detail.
  • Hugh Kennedy, The Prophet and the Age of the Caliphates; Fred Donner, The Early Islamic Conquests; Chase Robinson, Islamic Historiography — modern critical reconstruction and source analysis.
