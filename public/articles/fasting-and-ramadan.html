Understanding Fasting and Ramadan: A Complete Learning Guide

NoorDeen Editorial · 12 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic lunar calendar and the month in which the Qur'an began to be revealed. Fasting (sawm) is one of the five pillars of Islam, obligatory for every adult Muslim who is able, with clear exemptions for illness, travel, pregnancy, breastfeeding, menstruation and advanced age. This guide explains the law and the practice together, and flags where the schools of jurisprudence differ.

It is a learning guide, not a personal fatwa. For medical conditions or complex circumstances, consult a qualified scholar and a physician.

Key takeaways
  • Fasting Ramadan is obligatory for able adult Muslims and is one of the five pillars.
  • The purpose is taqwa — God-consciousness and self-restraint.
  • Exemptions are real and merciful; missed fasts are made up or compensated.
  • Suhoor is a recommended pre-dawn meal; iftar is at sunset.
  • Laylat al-Qadr is sought in the last ten nights, especially the odd nights.
  • Zakat al-Fitr is a separate obligation from Zakat on wealth.

1. The Obligation and Purpose of Fasting

Fasting was prescribed in the second year after the Hijrah. The Qur'an states the command and its purpose in a single verse:

“O you who have believed, decreed upon you is fasting as it was decreed upon those before you that you may become righteous.”
Qur'an 2:183

The Arabic word taqwa is often translated as piety or God-consciousness. Fasting trains it by suspending lawful desires for a set period, teaching the believer that the self can be governed rather than obeyed.

The Prophet ﷺ said that Islam is built on five: the testimony of faith, prayer, zakat, fasting Ramadan, and Hajj (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). Fasting is therefore not a voluntary devotion but a foundational obligation.

Ramadan is also the month of the Qur'an. The Qur'an was revealed in it, and the Prophet ﷺ reviewed the Qur'an with Jibril every Ramadan. Reading and studying the Qur'an is thus integral to the month, not an optional extra.

2. When Ramadan Begins: Sighting and Calculation

The Islamic calendar is lunar. A new month begins with the sighting of the crescent or, according to many scholars, with the completion of thirty days of the previous month. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Fast when you see it and break when you see it, and if it is cloudy then complete thirty days” (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

Muslims differ on whether astronomical calculation may be used when physical sighting is impossible or disputed. This is a genuine juristic difference, not a sign of unbelief. NoorDeen's Hijri calendar tool provides calculated dates and should be used alongside your local mosque or authority.

Practical advice: Follow the announced date of the recognised authority in your country or community. Avoid turning the start of Ramadan into a sectarian dispute.

3. Who Is Exempt, and What They Do

The Qur'an explicitly mentions exemptions:

“So whoever among you is ill or on a journey — then an equal number of other days. And upon those who are able [to fast, but with hardship] — a ransom of feeding a poor person.”
Qur'an 2:184

The classical scholars list the following categories:

  • Illness: if fasting would harm or severely worsen the condition, one may break the fast and make it up later.
  • Travel: travellers may break the fast and make it up later; the details depend on distance and duration according to each school.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: if there is risk to mother or child, one may break the fast. Schools differ on whether make-up, fidyah, or both are required.
  • Menstruation and postnatal bleeding: fasting is not valid during these periods, and the missed days are made up later.
  • Advanced age or chronic illness: those permanently unable to fast may feed a poor person for each day (fidyah).
  • Children: not obligatory before puberty, though training is encouraged.

A person who deliberately breaks a Ramadan fast without a valid excuse commits a serious sin and may owe make-up plus expiation (kaffarah) according to the school. The expiation is normally freeing a slave, or fasting two consecutive months, or feeding sixty poor people — the first two are not applicable or possible for most people today, so feeding is the practical option.

4. Suhoor and Iftar: The Daily Rhythm

Suhoor is the pre-dawn meal. It is a recommended practice, not an obligation. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Take suhoor, for in suhoor there is blessing” (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). He also advised delaying suhoor and hastening iftar.

Iftar is the sunset meal. The recommended practice is to break the fast with dates and water, then pray Maghrib, then eat the main meal. The Prophet ﷺ said: “The people will remain upon goodness as long as they hasten the iftar” (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

A common supplication at iftar is: “Dhahaba al-zama'u wa ibtallat al-'uruqu wa thabata al-ajru in sha' Allah” — “The thirst has gone, the veins are moistened, and the reward is confirmed, if Allah wills” (narrated by Abu Dawud).

Nutritionally, the priority is hydration, slow-release carbohydrates, protein and moderation. Ramadan is not a month of feasting after sunset; the point is restraint.

5. What Invalidates the Fast

The core invalidators are deliberate eating, drinking, sexual intercourse, and deliberate vomiting. Menstrual and postnatal bleeding also invalidate the fast. Accidental eating or drinking does not break the fast; the Prophet ﷺ said that if one forgets and eats or drinks, he should complete his fast, for Allah fed him (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

Matters that scholars discuss in detail include:

  • Injections and IV fluids: non-nutritive injections are generally permitted by many contemporary scholars; nutritive drips invalidate. Opinions differ.
  • Inhalers: many scholars permit them for asthma, comparing them to dust or miswak; some require make-up.
  • Brushing teeth: permitted if one avoids swallowing toothpaste; the miswak is explicitly recommended.
  • Rinsing the mouth and nose: permitted without exaggeration.
  • Blood tests and dialysis: depend on the type and quantity; consult a scholar and doctor.
  • Medication patches and eye drops: generally discussed as non-invalidating by many, but differences exist.
Principle: uncertainty about a medical matter is not a licence to ignore the fast, nor is it a reason to abandon treatment. Ask a qualified scholar with medical knowledge of your case.

6. Tarawih and Night Prayer

Tarawih is the voluntary night prayer performed after Isha during Ramadan. The Prophet ﷺ prayed it with the companions for a few nights, then stopped for fear it would become obligatory. Umar later revived the practice of praying it in congregation.

The number of rak'ahs is a matter of juristic difference: the Hanafi, Shafi'i and Hanbali schools commonly pray twenty, while the Maliki school and some hadith-oriented scholars prefer eight plus witr. Both practices are attested and accepted. The quality of recitation and humility matters more than the count.

Tarawih is not obligatory, but it is a emphasised Sunnah. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Whoever stands in prayer during Ramadan out of faith and seeking reward, his previous sins will be forgiven” (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

7. Laylat al-Qadr: The Night of Decree

Laylat al-Qadr is the night on which the Qur'an was revealed and which the Qur'an describes as better than a thousand months:

“Indeed, We sent it down during the Night of Decree. And what can make you know what is the Night of Decree? The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months.”
Qur'an 97:1–3

The Prophet ﷺ instructed believers to seek it in the last ten nights, especially the odd nights (21st, 23rd, 25th, 27th, 29th), though the exact night is not fixed. Aisha asked what to say if she found it, and he taught her: “Allahumma innaka 'afuwwun tuhibb al-'afwa fa'fu 'anni” — “O Allah, You are Forgiving and love forgiveness, so forgive me” (narrated by al-Tirmidhi, graded authentic).

The night is an opportunity for repentance, supplication, Qur'an and prayer — not for elaborate rituals invented for the occasion.

8. Zakat al-Fitr and Eid

Zakat al-Fitr is a mandatory charity at the end of Ramadan, distinct from the annual Zakat on wealth. It is due from every Muslim who possesses more than their basic needs for the day and night of Eid, and it is paid on behalf of dependants.

The standard amount is one sa' of the staple food of the locality — approximately 2.5 to 3 kilograms of rice, wheat, dates or similar. Many scholars permit paying its monetary equivalent, especially where that serves the poor better. It must be paid before the Eid prayer, ideally in the last days of Ramadan.

Eid al-Fitr begins with the sighting of Shawwal. The recommended acts include the takbir, a bath, wearing one's best clothes, eating dates before the prayer, and taking a different route to and from the prayer. Zakat al-Fitr is what allows the poor to celebrate without begging.

9. I'tikaf: Spiritual Retreat

I'tikaf is seclusion in the mosque for worship, especially in the last ten nights of Ramadan. It is a confirmed Sunnah. The Prophet ﷺ used to observe it, and Aisha reported that he would enter the mosque and stay there for worship (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

The minimum is a short period of intention and staying in the mosque; many scholars require fasting and a valid mosque. I'tikaf is not a holiday from responsibilities: one may leave for necessities, and it should not cause harm to family or work obligations.

Its purpose is to remove distractions and focus the heart on Allah in the most sacred nights of the year.

10. A Practical Daily Plan for Ramadan

  • Before Fajr: wake for suhoor, make intention, pray Tahajjud if able, and eat a balanced meal.
  • Fajr: pray in congregation, then read Qur'an or reflect until sunrise if possible.
  • Morning: work or study, with a short Qur'an portion and dhikr.
  • Afternoon: rest if needed, prepare iftar, avoid arguments and gossip.
  • Maghrib: break the fast with dates and water, pray, then eat moderately.
  • Isha and Tarawih: pray, listen attentively, and avoid distracting conversations in the mosque.
  • Night: a small portion of Qur'an, supplication, and sleep early enough for suhoor.

The most common Ramadan failures are not theological but practical: oversleeping, overeating, excessive social media, and losing tempers. Plan for them. The Prophet ﷺ said that fasting is a shield, so when one is fasting he should not behave obscenely or foolishly (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

11. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Can I brush my teeth while fasting?

Yes, provided you do not swallow toothpaste or water. The Prophet ﷺ recommended the miswak while fasting. Rinsing the mouth is permitted without exaggeration.

2. Do injections break the fast?

Contemporary scholars generally distinguish between nutritive and non-nutritive injections. Most permit non-nutritive injections (such as many vaccines and local anaesthetics) and prohibit nutritive drips. Opinions differ on some cases; consult a qualified scholar and your physician.

3. What if I miss a fast due to menstruation?

You do not fast during menstruation, and you make up the missed days after Ramadan. This is established in authentic hadith and agreed upon by the schools.

4. Can I pay Zakat al-Fitr in cash?

Many contemporary scholars permit paying the monetary equivalent, especially where cash serves the poor better. Others prefer the literal staple food. Follow your local mosque or the school you follow.

5. Is it permissible to travel during Ramadan?

Yes. Travellers have a concession to break the fast and make it up later, subject to the conditions of travel in your school. If the journey is easy and fasting causes no hardship, many scholars prefer fasting.
