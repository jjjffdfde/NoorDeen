Ali: The Gate of Knowledge and the Fitna

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Ali ibn Abi Talib is the companion around whom the early community divided most deeply. He was the Prophet’s cousin, the husband of his daughter Fatima, the father of Hasan and Husayn, and one of the bravest and most learned of the companions. He was also the fourth caliph, and his caliphate was consumed by civil war.

The sources for his life are rich, partisan and often contradictory. Sunni and Shia traditions both honour him, but they interpret key events differently. This article follows the historical record as far as it can be established, marks the points where the sources disagree, and refuses to turn him into either a semi-divine figure or a political villain.

Key takeaways
  • Ali was the cousin of the Prophet ﷺ and was raised in his household from childhood.
  • He was among the first to accept Islam and slept in the Prophet’s bed on the night of the hijrah.
  • He married Fatima, the Prophet’s daughter, and their sons Hasan and Husayn are central figures in Islamic history.
  • He fought at Badr, Uhud, the Trench and Khaybar, where the Prophet ﷺ gave him the banner.
  • The hadith of Ghadir Khumm is authentic in its core, but its interpretation is disputed between Sunni and Shia.
  • He became caliph after Uthman’s death and faced the rebellions of the Camel and Siffin.
  • The Kharijites split from him after Siffin, and he was assassinated in Kufa in 40 AH.
  • Later claims such as his birth inside the Ka‘ba and the “city of knowledge” hadith are weakly attested or disputed.

1. Why Ali Matters

Ali is central to Islamic history for three reasons. He was a member of the Prophet’s household and one of the earliest believers. He was a warrior and a judge whose courage and learning are remembered in both Sunni and Shia traditions. And he was the caliph whose reign defined the first fitna and the split between the early factions that later became Sunni and Shia Islam.

His life cannot be understood without the political crisis that followed Uthman’s assassination. Ali inherited a community that was already divided, and his attempts to restore unity were resisted by powerful opponents. The sources present him as a man of principle who was also politically isolated, and the debate about his decisions has never ended.

This article is an educational synthesis. It does not adjudicate the theological claims that later traditions built around him. It tries to give a clear account of his life, his words and his actions, and to distinguish the historical record from the legends that grew up on both sides.

2. The Boy in the Prophet’s Household

Ali was born in Makkah, the son of Abu Talib, the uncle of the Prophet ﷺ, and Fatimah bint Asad. The sources say that he was born around 600 CE, about ten years before the beginning of the revelation, though the exact date is not certain. His father was a respected but poor member of the Banu Hashim.

When a famine struck Makkah, the Prophet ﷺ took Ali into his household to relieve his uncle’s burden. Ali grew up under the Prophet’s care, and the sources describe a close bond between them. He was among the first to accept Islam, and the majority Shia tradition and many Sunni reports place him as the first male believer.

The exact order of the first conversions is disputed, and the dispute is theological as well as historical. What is clear is that Ali was a child or a very young man when he accepted Islam, and that his whole formation was shaped by the Prophet’s teaching.

3. The Night of the Hijrah

On the night of the hijrah, the Quraysh plotted to kill the Prophet ﷺ. Ali slept in the Prophet’s bed while the Prophet ﷺ left with Abu Bakr for the cave of Thawr. The plan depended on the assassins believing that the figure in the bed was the Prophet ﷺ.

The Qur’an may allude to this event in 2:207: “And of mankind is he who would sell himself, seeking the pleasure of Allah; and Allah is full of kindness to His servants.” Many commentators applied the verse to Ali, though the connection is interpretive rather than explicit.

Ali also stayed behind to return the trusts that the Prophet ﷺ had been holding for the people of Makkah. The episode captures two sides of his character: physical courage and scrupulous honesty. He was willing to risk his life, and he was careful with the property of those who opposed him.

4. Fatima and the Household

Ali married Fatima, the daughter of the Prophet ﷺ, in Madinah. The marriage was simple, and the sources describe a household of poverty, work and devotion. Fatima ground grain and carried water; Ali worked and struggled to provide. Their sons Hasan and Husayn, and their daughters Zaynab and Umm Kulthum, became central figures in Islamic history.

The Prophet ﷺ loved the household deeply. The hadith of the cloak (hadith al-kisa’) reports that he gathered Ali, Fatima, Hasan and Husayn under a cloak and prayed for them, and the verse of purification (33:33) is connected with this event in many reports. The interpretation of the verse is disputed, but the affection of the Prophet ﷺ for the household is not.

The household was not a political party in the modern sense. It was a family that was close to the Prophet ﷺ and that suffered greatly after his death. The later traditions built a theology around it, but the historical family was human, vulnerable and often divided by the same politics that divided the community.

5. Badr, Uhud and the Trench

Ali fought at Badr, where he is reported to have killed several of the Qurayshi champions. At Uhud he was among those who stayed with the Prophet ﷺ when many others fled, and he was wounded. The sources praise his courage in both battles, though the details of the individual duels vary.

At the Trench (al-Khandaq), the confederates besieged Madinah, and the fighting was dominated by skirmishes rather than a full battle. The most famous episode is the duel between Ali and Amr ibn Abd Wudd, a Qurayshi warrior who had crossed the trench. The sources report that Ali killed him, and the story became one of the defining images of his bravery.

The historical Ali was not only a fighter. He was also a judge and a teacher, and the sources preserve his rulings and his sermons. His courage on the battlefield was part of a larger vocation that included law, governance and the interpretation of the Qur’an.

6. Khaybar and the Banner

At Khaybar in 7 AH, the Muslims besieged the Jewish fortresses of the Khaybar oasis. The sources report that the Prophet ﷺ said, “I will give the banner tomorrow to a man who loves Allah and His Messenger, and whom Allah and His Messenger love.” The next day he called Ali, who had been suffering from an eye ailment, treated his eye, and gave him the banner.

The hadith is reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, and it is one of the most important authentic reports about Ali’s rank. The conquest of Khaybar followed, though the sources differ on the details of the fighting and the terms of the surrender.

The banner hadith is often used in Sunni-Shia polemic, but its core is a statement of the Prophet’s love for Ali and Ali’s love for the Prophet ﷺ. It does not by itself settle the question of succession, and it should not be isolated from the rest of the Prophet’s teaching.

7. The Farewell and Ghadir Khumm

On the return from the farewell pilgrimage in 10 AH, the Prophet ﷺ stopped at a place called Ghadir Khumm and addressed the people. The sources report that he said, “Whoever I am his mawla, Ali is his mawla.” The hadith is widely transmitted and is regarded as authentic by many scholars, though its interpretation is disputed.

Sunni scholars generally understand mawla as “friend” or “ally,” and read the statement as a defence of Ali against those who had complained about him during the Yemen expedition. Shia scholars understand it as a designation of Ali as the Prophet’s successor. The word itself can mean both, and the historical context is debated.

What is not disputed is that the Prophet ﷺ honoured Ali publicly and that the statement became one of the most important texts in the later debates about authority. The reader should approach it with a knowledge of the Arabic language, the context and the subsequent history, rather than with a slogan.

Source caution. The Ghadir Khumm report is authentic in its core, but its interpretation is not settled by the text alone. Sunni and Shia scholars disagree about the meaning of mawla and about whether the event was a designation of political succession. This article presents the dispute without pretending that it can be resolved by a single translation.

8. The Caliphate and the Fitna

After Uthman’s assassination in 35 AH, the people of Madinah pledged allegiance to Ali. He became the fourth caliph, but his authority was immediately contested. Mu‘awiyah ibn Abi Sufyan, the governor of Syria and a relative of Uthman, refused to pledge until the killers of Uthman were punished. A’ishah, Talha and al-Zubayr also opposed Ali’s authority.

Ali’s caliphate was thus born in crisis. He moved the capital to Kufa, where he had more support, and he tried to consolidate his authority. The sources describe his efforts to reform the administration and to distribute wealth fairly, but the civil war consumed his energy and his resources.

The fitna was not a simple conflict between good and evil. It involved competing claims of justice, revenge, legitimacy and tribal interest. Ali’s supporters believed he was the legitimate caliph; his opponents believed he was protecting Uthman’s killers. The sources preserve the arguments of both sides.

9. The Battle of the Camel

The first major battle of the fitna was fought near Basra in 36 AH. A’ishah, Talha and al-Zubayr gathered forces to demand justice for Uthman and to challenge Ali’s authority. Ali met them and tried to negotiate, but the fighting began and became known as the Battle of the Camel, after the camel on which A’ishah sat.

Talha and al-Zubayr were killed, and A’ishah was escorted back to Madinah with respect. The battle was a tragedy for the community: two groups of Muslims who had fought together against the Quraysh now killed each other. The sources record Ali’s grief and his efforts to honour the dead.

The Battle of the Camel set the pattern for the fitna. It showed that the community had no agreed mechanism for resolving disputes about authority, and that the memory of Uthman’s death could be mobilised for political ends.

10. Siffin and the Kharijites

The confrontation with Mu‘awiyah culminated at Siffin in 37 AH. The fighting was inconclusive, and the two sides agreed to arbitration. A group of Ali’s supporters rejected the arbitration, arguing that “judgement belongs to Allah alone” and that Ali had no right to submit his claim to human judges. They withdrew from his army and became known as the Kharijites.

The Kharijites were not a unified movement. They included pious ascetics, tribal malcontents and radicals, and their slogan “no judgement but Allah’s” was used to justify rebellion against any ruler who did not meet their standards. Ali tried to reason with them, but the movement grew and became violent.

At Nahrawan in 38 AH, Ali defeated a Kharijite force. The victory did not end the movement. The Kharijites survived as a radical current in Islamic history, and one of their members would eventually assassinate Ali.

11. Nahrawan and the Fragmentation

The battle of Nahrawan was a turning point. Ali’s army was exhausted, his authority was contested, and the arbitration with Mu‘awiyah had damaged his position. The Kharijites were defeated on the battlefield, but their ideas spread among those who were disillusioned with the political elite.

Ali’s later years were marked by loss. His supporters in Kufa were divided, his governors were unreliable, and Mu‘awiyah consolidated his control over Syria and Egypt. The sources describe Ali’s loneliness and his continuing commitment to justice, even as his political project unravelled.

The fragmentation of the community was not caused by Ali alone. It was the result of structural problems that had been building since the conquests: the absence of a clear succession mechanism, the rivalry of the Qurayshi clans, the tension between the early companions and the newer converts, and the difficulty of governing a vast empire from a garrison town.

12. The Assassination in Kufa

Ali was assassinated in 40 AH in Kufa by Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam, a Kharijite. He struck Ali with a poisoned sword during the dawn prayer in the mosque. Ali died a few days later, and he was buried near Kufa, at a site that later became the shrine of Najaf.

His caliphate had lasted about four years and nine months. He was succeeded by his son Hasan, who abdicated in favour of Mu‘awiyah after a short period in order to avoid further bloodshed. The year of Hasan’s abdication is called the Year of Unity in some Sunni sources.

Ali’s death did not end the fitna. It deepened the divisions that would shape the later history of Islam. The Sunni and Shia traditions both mourn him, but they interpret his legacy in different ways.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

Ali’s life teaches that courage and wisdom are not enough to resolve a political crisis that has structural causes. He was a man of great personal integrity, but he inherited a community that was already fractured, and his attempts to restore unity were resisted by forces he could not control.

The sources also teach the danger of hagiography. The later traditions, both Sunni and Shia, sometimes turn Ali into a figure who transcends history. The historical Ali was a human being who made decisions, suffered losses and was opposed by other Muslims. His greatness does not depend on denying his humanity.

The reader who wants to understand him should begin with the Qur’an, the authentic hadith, the early biographical reports and the sermons and letters attributed to him. The Nahj al-Balagha is a later compilation and its attribution is debated; it should be read as literature and history, not as a verbatim record.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith, the early historical reports and later sectarian narrative. It honours Ali without adopting either the Sunni or the Shia theological interpretation of the disputed events, and it does not present later legends as established history.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Was Ali the first Muslim?

Many Shia and some Sunni reports place him as the first male believer, while other reports place Abu Bakr first among adult men. The exact order is disputed. What is clear is that Ali accepted Islam as a child or young man and was raised in the Prophet’s household.

2. What does “mawla” mean in the Ghadir Khumm hadith?

The word can mean friend, ally, client or master. Sunni scholars generally read it as friendship and support; Shia scholars read it as designation of succession. The report is authentic in its core, but its interpretation is disputed.

3. Why did Ali move the capital to Kufa?

Kufa was a major garrison town with a large body of supporters, and Madinah had become politically unstable after Uthman’s assassination. The move was a practical attempt to consolidate authority, though it also alienated some of the Madinan elite.

4. Who were the Kharijites?

They were a radical group that rejected Ali’s arbitration with Mu‘awiyah and adopted the slogan “judgement belongs to Allah alone.” They later became a violent movement that rejected all rulers who did not meet their standards. One of them assassinated Ali.

5. Was Ali infallible?

Sunni tradition honours him as a great companion and caliph but does not consider him infallible. Shia tradition holds a different view of the Imams. This article presents the historical record without adjudicating the theological dispute.
