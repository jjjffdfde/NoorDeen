Al-Isra’ wal-Mi’raj: The Night Journey and the Gift of Prayer

NoorDeen Editorial · 13 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The night journey (al-Isra’) and ascension (al-Mi’raj) stand at the turning point of the Makkan period. The Prophet ﷺ had lost his uncle and his wife, been rejected at Ta’if and faced relentless opposition. Then, in a single night, he was taken from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque and raised through the heavens. He returned with the five daily prayers.

The event is affirmed by the Qur’an and by authentic hadith, but the sources do not answer every question people ask about it. This article sets out what is established, what is disputed, and what should be left to Allah. It avoids the elaborate descriptions that later storytellers added.

Key takeaways
  • The Qur’an affirms the journey from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque in 17:1 and alludes to the vision in 53:1–18.
  • The ascension is narrated in authentic hadith, most famously in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim.
  • The exact date is not established; the 27th of Rajab is a later popular commemoration, not a confirmed historical date.
  • Scholars differ on whether the journey was bodily or spiritual; the majority position affirms the body, but the difference is not a basis for takfir.
  • Jerusalem and the Masjid al-Aqsa are sacred to Muslims, independently of later political disputes.
  • The central legal gift of the night is the five daily prayers, reduced from fifty.
  • The event is a lesson in hope: Allah can restore a servant after the hardest year.

1. The Qur’anic Anchor

The primary Qur’anic text is the opening verse of Surah al-Isra’:

“Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him of Our signs. Indeed, He is the Hearing, the Seeing.”
Qur’an 17:1

The verse establishes several things: the journey was by night; it was from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque; the surroundings of the Farthest Mosque were blessed; and the purpose was to show the Prophet ﷺ signs of Allah. It does not describe the means of travel, the heavens, the prophets he met or the prayers. Those details come from hadith.

Surah al-Najm also describes a vision: “While he was in the highest part of the horizon... Then he approached and came closer... And he certainly saw him in another descent at the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary” (53:7–14). Exegetes differ on whether this refers to the same night, to an earlier vision, or to the Prophet’s ﷺ sight of Jibril. The Qur’an itself does not force a single chronological reconstruction.

The verse also names Jerusalem. Al-Masjid al-Aqsa means “the farthest mosque”, and the site is the Haram al-Sharif in Jerusalem. The Qur’an calls its surroundings blessed, a point that classical scholars connected to the land of the prophets. This sanctity is religious, not a licence for political aggression by any party.

2. The Hadith Evidence

The most detailed narration is the long hadith of Malik ibn Sa’sa’ah, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. It describes the Prophet ﷺ being brought al-Buraq, travelling to Jerusalem, leading the prophets in prayer, ascending through the heavens, meeting Adam, Isa, Yahya, Yusuf, Idris, Harun, Musa and Ibrahim, reaching the Lote Tree, and receiving the command of fifty prayers, which was reduced to five after Musa’s counsel.

A shorter narration from Anas ibn Malik, also in the two Sahihs, describes the journey and the ascension. The reports differ in some details — the order of the prophets, the exact conversation, the number of heavens — and scholars have discussed these differences. What is firmly established is the journey, the ascension, the meeting with the prophets and the gift of the five prayers.

Some popular accounts add vivid descriptions of al-Buraq’s appearance, the gates of heaven, the angels’ names, the Prophet’s ﷺ physical sensations and conversations that are not in the authentic texts. These should not be presented as certain. The hadith are rich enough without embellishment.

The event is also mentioned in the Prophet’s ﷺ statement that he was taken to Bayt al-Maqdis and then to the heavens. The Companions treated it as a real event, and the Qur’an’s affirmation in 17:1 settles the basic fact for believers.

3. When Did It Happen?

The sources do not give a fixed date. Most reports place the journey shortly before the Hijrah, after the Year of Sorrow and the visit to Ta’if, when the Prophet ﷺ was about fifty. Some scholars suggest the tenth or eleventh year of prophethood; others place it earlier or later. The 27th of Rajab is widely observed as Laylat al-Isra’ wal-Mi’raj, but this date is not established by an authentic text.

The commemoration developed later in Muslim history, with local customs varying widely. There is no authentic report that the Prophet ﷺ or the Companions marked the night with a specific ritual, communal prayer or food. A Muslim may remember the event, read the Qur’an and reflect on the gift of prayer, but should not invent a liturgy and attribute it to the Prophet ﷺ.

This is not a rejection of all commemoration. It is a call to distinguish between historical memory and religious legislation. The night is significant because of what happened, not because of a particular date on the calendar.

4. Bodily or Spiritual?

Scholars have discussed whether the journey was with the body or in the soul. The majority of classical scholars affirmed that it was bodily, based on the Qur’an’s “He took His Servant by night” and on the hadith’s physical descriptions. Others, including some early scholars, understood it as a vision or a spiritual journey.

The difference is not a matter of faith in the event itself. Both positions affirm that Allah showed the Prophet ﷺ signs and that the journey was real in the sense that it was not a dream invented by his opponents. The disagreement concerns the mode, not the truth.

Some reports attributed to Aisha say that the Prophet’s ﷺ body did not leave, but these reports are debated by hadith scholars. The safest position is to affirm the journey as the Qur’an and authentic hadith describe it, to avoid speculative details, and to refrain from condemning those who hold a different scholarly view.

5. Jerusalem and the Farthest Mosque

The journey links the Kaaba in Makkah to the Haram al-Sharif in Jerusalem. The Qur’an calls the latter “al-Masjid al-Aqsa” and says its surroundings are blessed. The Prophet ﷺ led the prophets in prayer there, according to the hadith, a sign of the continuity of the prophetic mission.

Jerusalem is sacred to Muslims, Jews and Christians, and its history is layered with conquest, pilgrimage, coexistence and conflict. The Qur’an’s affirmation of the sanctity of the site does not justify the displacement or humiliation of others. Classical Muslim rule generally protected the lives and worship of non-Muslims under agreed terms, even when those terms were imperfectly applied.

For Muslims, the night journey is a reminder that the land of the prophets is part of their religious memory. It is not a substitute for justice. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the rights of others are not cancelled by a sacred claim, and the Qur’an commands justice even against those you dislike (5:8).

6. The Ascent Through the Heavens

The hadith describe the Prophet ﷺ ascending through the heavens and meeting prophets at different levels: Adam, Isa and Yahya, Yusuf, Idris, Harun, Musa and Ibrahim. He then reached the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary (sidrat al-muntaha), beyond which no created being passes.

The meeting with Musa is particularly significant. When the Prophet ﷺ was commanded to establish fifty prayers, Musa advised him to return and ask for a reduction because his community would not be able to bear it. The Prophet ﷺ returned repeatedly until the command was reduced to five, with the promise that five would be rewarded as fifty.

This dialogue is not a negotiation about Allah’s knowledge; Allah knew what would happen. It is a teaching device that shows the Prophet’s ﷺ concern for his community and the mercy embedded in the final command. The five prayers are described as a gift, not a burden imposed without consideration.

The hadith also mention the Prophet’s ﷺ vision of Paradise and Hellfire, the angels and the Lote Tree. These descriptions are part of the unseen. They should be accepted as reported without attempting to draw or physically locate them.

7. The Gift of the Five Prayers

The central legal outcome of the night is the five daily prayers. The Qur’an commands prayer in many places, but the details of the five times and their structure were taught by the Prophet ﷺ. The night journey is the moment when the command was given in the heavens, and the Prophet ﷺ described it as the most important gift he received.

The five prayers are a daily connection between the servant and Allah. They are not a magic formula that makes a person sinless, and they are not a substitute for justice, charity or honest work. But they structure the day around remembrance and remind the believer that the world is not the whole of reality.

The Prophet ﷺ said that the prayer is the first thing a servant will be asked about on the Day of Judgment. He also described it as a means of expiating sins, like a river at the door of one who bathes in it five times a day. These reports are found in the Sunan and the Sahih collections. The point is not that prayer automatically cancels every consequence of wrongdoing, but that it is the foundation of a life turned towards Allah.

For a new Muslim or a person returning to prayer, the night journey is an invitation to begin. The command was reduced from fifty to five out of mercy. The response should be gratitude, not despair over past neglect.

8. The Test of Faith and the Response of Abu Bakr

When the Prophet ﷺ told the people about the journey, Quraysh mocked him. They knew the distance to Jerusalem and could not imagine a night journey. Some believers wavered. The report says that Abu Bakr, when told, said: “If he said it, then he has spoken the truth.” He was thereafter called al-Siddiq, the truthful.

The story is preserved in the Sirah and later sources rather than in the most rigorous hadith collections, but it captures the essence of the test. The night journey was not a public miracle designed to compel Quraysh. It was a private gift that deepened the faith of those who already trusted the Prophet ﷺ.

The Qur’an refers to the vision as a trial for people: “And We did not make the sight which We showed you except as a trial for the people” (17:60). The event separated those who followed the Prophet ﷺ because of evidence and character from those who followed only what they could physically verify.

This is a recurring Qur’anic theme. Faith is not credulity; it is trust grounded in evidence, character and the coherence of revelation. The night journey tested whether that trust could survive a report that seemed impossible.

9. Lessons for Believers

The night journey offers several lessons:

  • Allah restores after loss. The Prophet ﷺ was at his lowest point when he was honoured with the ascent.
  • Prayer is a gift. The five prayers came from the heavens, reduced in mercy.
  • Jerusalem matters. The journey links the Kaaba and the Haram al-Sharif in one prophetic geography.
  • The unseen is real. The prophets, the Lote Tree and the heavens are part of revelation, not fantasy.
  • Do not embellish. The authentic reports are sufficient; invented details distract from the message.
  • Faith is tested. The event separated sincere trust from superficial belief.

The night journey is not an excuse for sectarian rivalry or political violence. It is a call to prayer, to hope and to humility before what we do not know. The Prophet ﷺ returned from the highest creation with a command for ordinary life: pray, and do not give up.

10. Sources and Their Limits

The primary sources are:

  • The Qur’an — 17:1 and 53:1–18, with the surrounding context.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari — the long narration of Malik ibn Sa’sa’ah and the report of Anas ibn Malik.
  • Sahih Muslim — parallel narrations of the journey and the ascension.
  • Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah — the chronology and the reaction of Quraysh and Abu Bakr.
  • Ibn Kathir, al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah and his tafsir — the scholarly discussion of the date and mode.
  • Later works on the night journey — useful for history of interpretation, but often contain weak or legendary reports.

This article does not claim to resolve every difference. It distinguishes the Qur’anic anchor, the authentic hadith, the early Sirah reports and later popular additions. Where a detail is not established, it is not presented as certain.

Editorial note: the 27th of Rajab is a later commemoration, not a date established by the Qur’an or authentic Sunnah. Muslims should avoid attributing specific rituals to the Prophet ﷺ without evidence.

11. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Is the night journey mentioned in the Qur’an?

Yes. Surah al-Isra’ 17:1 explicitly mentions the journey from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque. Surah al-Najm 53:1–18 describes a vision that many exegetes connect to the ascent.

2. Did the Prophet ﷺ travel in body or soul?

Scholars differ. The majority affirm a bodily journey; others understand it as a spiritual vision. Both affirm the event as real. The difference concerns the mode, not the truth of the journey.

3. Is the 27th of Rajab the date of the night journey?

It is a popular commemoration, but the date is not established by the Qur’an or authentic hadith. The sources place the event shortly before the Hijrah without fixing a date.

4. Why is Jerusalem important to Muslims?

The Qur’an calls al-Masjid al-Aqsa the Farthest Mosque and says its surroundings are blessed. The Prophet ﷺ led the prophets in prayer there according to the hadith. Jerusalem is sacred to Muslims, Jews and Christians, and its sanctity does not justify injustice to any community.

5. What was the main gift of the night?

The five daily prayers. The hadith describe the command being reduced from fifty to five, with the reward of fifty, out of Allah’s mercy.
