Dawud and Sulayman: Power, Justice and Gratitude

NoorDeen Editorial · 13 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Dawud and Sulayman are the two prophet-kings of the Qur’an. Dawud is the shepherd who defeats a giant and receives a book, a kingdom and a voice that the mountains and birds answer. Sulayman inherits both the prophecy and the throne, is taught the language of birds, commands the wind and the jinn, and judges with a wisdom that his father recognises.

Their story is also a warning about power. The Qur’an gives them extraordinary gifts, then shows them tested by those gifts: a disputed judgement, a pair of litigants, a missing bird, a queen who worships the sun, a throne that arrives before its owner, and a death that no one notices. This article follows the Qur’anic text and marks where the famous legends begin.

Key takeaways
  • Dawud kills Jalut, receives the kingdom and wisdom, and is given the Zabur (Psalms).
  • The mountains and birds join Dawud in praise, and iron is softened for him to make armour.
  • Dawud and Sulayman both judge a dispute over a field; the Qur’an says Sulayman understood it and that both were given judgement and knowledge.
  • Sulayman is taught the language of birds and commands an army of jinn, humans and birds.
  • The Queen of Sheba is not named in the Qur’an; “Bilqis” is later tradition.
  • Sulayman’s throne is brought by one with knowledge of the Book, and the queen submits after seeing the glass palace.
  • The jinn did not know the unseen: Sulayman’s death was discovered only when a creature ate through his staff.
  • The Qur’an explicitly denies that Sulayman taught magic (2:102); the ring, the demon and the forty-year legends are not revelation.

1. Why Dawud and Sulayman Matter

Dawud and Sulayman are named in the Qur’an as recipients of prophecy, scripture, kingdom and judgement. Dawud is mentioned in 2:251, 4:163, 5:78, 6:84, 17:55, 21:78–80, 27:15, 34:10–11 and 38:17–26. Sulayman appears in 2:102, 4:163, 6:84, 21:78–82, 27:15–44, 34:12–14 and 38:30–40.

The passages are not a court chronicle. They are selected scenes that teach three things at once: that prophethood and political power can coexist, that power is a test, and that knowledge, justice and gratitude are the qualities that make rule legitimate. The Qur’an is as interested in Sulayman’s prayer for a grateful heart as in his command of the wind.

The story also has a long afterlife in legend. The ring that controlled the jinn, the demon who stole the throne, the forty years of wandering, the marriage of Sulayman and Bilqis, and the exact identity of the hoopoe’s news are later narrative, not Qur’anic revelation. This article keeps the text and the legend apart.

2. Dawud, Talut and the Giant

The Qur’an introduces Dawud in the context of the Children of Israel’s demand for a king. They ask their prophet to appoint a king so that they may fight in the way of Allah, then hesitate when Talut is chosen because he is not wealthy enough. Their prophet answers that Allah has chosen him and increased him in knowledge and stature (2:246–247).

The sign of the appointment is the ark (tabut) containing tranquillity (sakinah) from their Lord and relics of the family of Musa and Harun, carried by angels (2:248). Talut leads the army, tests his soldiers at a river, and advances with a small faithful remnant against Jalut and his forces (2:249–250).

Dawud, a young man in the army, kills Jalut. The Qur’an says that Allah gave him the kingdom and wisdom and taught him what He willed (2:251). The verse is careful: the victory is not a celebration of military prowess but a demonstration that Allah repels some people by means of others, and that the earth would be corrupted if He did not.

3. The Psalms, the Mountains and the Softened Iron

Dawud is given the Zabur, the Psalms (4:163; 17:55). The Qur’an does not quote the Zabur or describe its contents in detail; it presents it as a revealed book given to Dawud alongside the Torah and the Gospel in the chain of revelation. The existing Psalms of the Hebrew Bible are not identical with the Qur’anic Zabur, and the Qur’an does not claim that they are.

The mountains and birds are subjected to join Dawud in praise: “We subjected the mountains to glorify with him in the evening and at sunrise, and the birds gathered” (38:18–19; see 34:10). The image is not a nature poem. It presents all creation as participating in the worship that human beings so often neglect.

Allah also softened iron for Dawud and taught him to make coats of mail, commanding him to measure the links well (21:80; 34:10–11). The Qur’an presents technology as a divine gift and a form of mercy: armour protects the body, and the skill to make it is part of the provision by which a society survives.

Source caution. The Qur’an does not preserve the text of the Zabur, and it does not identify the existing biblical Psalms as the book given to Dawud. The relationship between the two is a question for historians of scripture, not something the Qur’an settles.

4. The Judgement of the Field

The Qur’an gives one of its clearest pictures of prophetic justice in 21:78–79. Two parties bring a dispute: the sheep of one group have strayed into the field of another at night. Dawud gives a judgement, and Sulayman gives another. The Qur’an says that Allah made Sulayman understand the case, while giving both of them judgement and knowledge.

The passage is remarkable for what it does not say. It does not describe the field, the number of sheep, the damage, or the exact ruling of either prophet. It does not say that Dawud was wrong in a moral sense; it says that Sulayman understood the case better. Both are praised. The lesson is that a judge may be sincere and still be corrected by a deeper understanding.

The story also shows a father and son in a shared vocation. Sulayman is not presented as a rival who displaces Dawud. He is a younger prophet whose insight is recognised, and the Qur’an uses the episode to teach that wisdom is not a function of age or office. The later elaborations that supply the exact verdict are commentary, not revelation.

5. The Litigants and the Ninety-Nine Sheep

Surah Sad presents a different scene. Two litigants climb into Dawud’s private chamber (mihrab) and ask him to judge between them. One says that his brother has ninety-nine sheep and he has one, and that the brother demands he give it up. Dawud tells him that he has been wronged by the demand for his single sheep to add to the ninety-nine (38:21–24).

Then Dawud realises that he has been tested. He asks forgiveness, falls bowing, and is forgiven. The Qur’an says that he has nearness to Allah and a good return (38:25). The passage does not explain the exact nature of his error. The later story that he gave a hasty judgement without hearing the other side is a plausible reading, but the text does not state it.

What is clear is the moral: a judge must not decide on the basis of one party’s account, however sympathetic it sounds. The episode is a warning to anyone with authority, and it is placed immediately before the Qur’an’s praise of Dawud’s devotion and his kingdom. The two themes belong together: the ruler who is close to Allah is the ruler who is willing to be corrected.

Source caution. The Qur’an does not specify Dawud’s mistake in this episode. The detailed stories that explain it — the wife of Uriah, the hasty verdict, the exact number of the sheep — are later narrative and should not be presented as the plain sense of the Qur’an.

6. Sulayman the Heir and the Language of Birds

Sulayman inherits Dawud in prophecy and kingship. He says, “O people, we have been taught the language of birds, and we have been given of all things. Indeed, this is clear favour” (27:16). The Qur’an presents the gift as a sign of Allah’s favour, not as a personal achievement.

His army includes jinn, humans and birds, and it is marshalled in ranks (27:17). When they reach the valley of the ants, an ant warns the others to enter their dwellings lest Sulayman and his soldiers crush them. Sulayman smiles at her words and prays, “My Lord, enable me to be grateful for Your favour which You have bestowed upon me and upon my parents, and to do righteousness of which You approve” (27:18–19).

The scene is easy to sentimentalise, but its point is serious. The king who commands the wind and the jinn is taught gratitude by an ant. The Qur’an places the prayer for thankfulness at the moment of greatest power, not after a defeat. That is the ethical centre of the Sulayman cycle.

7. The Hoopoe and the Queen of Sheba

Sulayman inspects the birds and finds the hoopoe absent. He threatens to punish or slaughter it unless it brings a clear authority (27:20–21). The hoopoe arrives with news of a city called Saba: its queen rules over a people who prostrate to the sun instead of Allah, and Satan has made their deeds attractive to them (27:22–24).

The Qur’an does not name the queen. The name “Bilqis” comes from later tradition, and the detailed descriptions of her throne, her palace and her conversion are partly Qur’anic and partly legendary. The Qur’anic text says that she has a great throne (27:23) and that she and her people worship the sun (27:24).

The hoopoe is not the hero of the story. It is a messenger whose report must be verified, and Sulayman does not act on it immediately. He sends a letter, waits for a response, and tests what he hears. The episode is a model of intelligence-gathering that refuses both credulity and arrogance.

8. The Letter, the Council and the Gift

The letter is one of the most quoted passages in the Qur’an: “Indeed, it is from Sulayman, and indeed, it is ‘In the name of Allah, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful. Do not exalt yourselves above me, but come to me in submission’” (27:30–31). It is a summons to monotheism and to political submission, and it begins with the name of Allah rather than with the king’s titles.

The queen consults her nobles. They say that they are strong and that the decision belongs to her. She answers that kings, when they enter a city, ruin it and humiliate its honoured people (27:32–34). She sends a gift, hoping to test Sulayman or to buy peace.

Sulayman rejects the gift. “Allah has given me better than what He has given you,” he says, and warns that he will come with an army they cannot resist (27:36–37). The refusal is not contempt for wealth; it is a refusal to let political relations be reduced to bribery. The queen’s wisdom and Sulayman’s firmness are both on display.

9. The Throne and the Palace of Glass

Before the queen arrives, Sulayman asks which of his court can bring her throne. A powerful jinn offers to bring it before Sulayman rises from his seat; one who has knowledge of the Book offers to bring it before the blink of an eye (27:38–40). Sulayman receives it and says, “This is from the favour of my Lord to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful” (27:40).

The Qur’an does not name the one who brought the throne. The later identification with Asif ibn Barkhiya is tradition, not revelation. Nor does the Qur’an explain the mechanism. The point is theological: knowledge and power are gifts, and the test is gratitude.

When the queen arrives, she is asked whether her throne is like this. She says, “It is as if it is it” (27:42). She is then invited into a palace whose floor is made of glass. Thinking it is water, she uncovers her shins; Sulayman tells her that it is a palace paved with glass. She responds, “My Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit with Sulayman to Allah, Lord of the worlds” (27:44). The story ends with her conversion, not with a marriage or a conquest.

10. The Wind, the Jinn and the Command to Be Grateful

The Qur’an subjects the wind to Sulayman, blowing by his command toward the land that Allah has blessed (21:81), and gives him a month’s journey in the morning and a month’s in the evening (34:12). The jinn build for him mihrabs, statues, basins like reservoirs and fixed cauldrons (34:13).

The command that follows is the key to the whole cycle: “Work, O family of Dawud, in gratitude” (34:13). The gifts are not rewards for piety that can be enjoyed without responsibility. They are a commission. The family of Dawud is addressed as a household of prophets and kings whose public work is an act of thanksgiving.

The Qur’an also insists that Sulayman did not disbelieve. In 2:102 it rejects the claim that he taught magic: “Sulayman did not disbelieve, but the devils disbelieved, teaching people magic.” The verse is a direct correction of a slander that had attached itself to his name. The later legends of a magic ring and a demonic double contradict the Qur’an’s own defence of his prophethood.

Source caution. The Qur’an does not describe the jinn as a technology that believers can harness, and it explicitly denies that Sulayman practised magic (2:102). The ring, the demon, the forty years and the building projects attributed to him in later literature are not revelation and should not be treated as historical fact.

11. The Death and the Staff

Surah Saba ends the Sulayman cycle with an extraordinary image. When Allah decrees his death, nothing indicates it to the jinn except a creature of the earth eating through his staff. When his body falls, the jinn realise that if they had known the unseen they would not have remained in humiliating torment (34:14).

The passage is a polemic against the idea that jinn or any creature share knowledge of the unseen. The jinn were working for Sulayman and did not know that he had died. Their labour continued because they assumed he was alive. The staff was the only thing holding him upright, and a small creature brought the whole illusion down.

The Qur’an does not say how long he remained standing, and the later figure of forty years is tradition. Nor does it identify the creature precisely; the Arabic dabbah means a small crawling creature, often translated “termite” or “worm”. The theological point does not depend on the zoology.

12. The Horses, the Test and the Kingdom

Surah Sad presents two difficult passages about Sulayman. In the first, horses are presented to him; he says, “I have preferred the love of good over the remembrance of my Lord” until they are hidden from view, then orders them brought back and wipes their legs and necks (38:30–33). The Arabic allows more than one reading, and the classical commentators disagreed about whether he was distracted from prayer, whether he was admiring the horses, or whether he was stroking them in affection.

The second passage says, “We tested Sulayman and placed upon his throne a body; then he returned. He said, ‘My Lord, forgive me and grant me a kingdom such as will not belong to anyone after me’” (38:34–35). The “body” is not explained. The later story that a jinn took his form and sat on the throne while Sulayman wandered is a legend, not the plain text.

What the Qur’an does say is that Sulayman was tested, that he turned back to Allah, that he asked forgiveness, and that he was given a kingdom unlike any other. The passage is not a celebration of unlimited power. It is a prayer for a unique gift accompanied by an acknowledgement of dependence.

Source caution. The identity of the “body” on Sulayman’s throne, the nature of his mistake with the horses, and the story of the ring and the demon are not explained in the Qur’an. The classical commentaries preserve several readings; the popular legends are later and should be labelled as such.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

Dawud and Sulayman show that power can be compatible with prophethood, but only when power is understood as a trust. Dawud is corrected by his son’s judgement; Sulayman is taught gratitude by an ant and tested by the gifts themselves. The Qur’an does not present them as philosopher-kings who never err. It presents them as prophets who are corrected, forgiven and taught.

The cycle also insists on the limits of knowledge. The jinn do not know the unseen. The hoopoe’s report must be verified. The queen’s throne arrives by a knowledge that is not named. Human beings are not invited to build a technology of the unseen; they are invited to worship the One who knows it.

Finally, the story is a case study in source discipline. The Qur’an gives the theological core and refuses to satisfy curiosity about dates, names and mechanisms. The legends of the ring, the demon, the forty years and the marriage to Bilqis are not in the Qur’an. They may be interesting as literature, but they are not evidence for the lives of the prophets.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith and later narrative. It does not endorse the magical legends attached to Sulayman, and it does not treat the biblical or folkloric expansions as revelation.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Were Dawud and Sulayman prophets or only kings?

Both. The Qur’an names them among the prophets (4:163; 6:84) and also describes them as kings with armies, judgement and building projects. Their kingship is part of their prophetic vocation, not a separate career.

2. Did Sulayman understand the language of birds and ants?

The Qur’an says that Sulayman was taught the language of birds (27:16) and reports the speech of an ant (27:18–19). It does not describe the mechanism or extend the gift to every creature. The text presents it as a divine favour, not a natural skill.

3. Who was the Queen of Sheba?

The Qur’an calls her the queen of Saba and does not name her. “Bilqis” is a later tradition. The Qur’an describes her throne, her consultation with her nobles, her gift to Sulayman and her eventual submission to Allah (27:22–44).

4. Did Sulayman marry the Queen of Sheba?

The Qur’an does not say. It ends the episode with her acknowledgement of Allah (27:44). The marriage and the later stories about her are extra-Qur’anic tradition.

5. What was the “body” placed on Sulayman’s throne?

The Qur’an does not explain it (38:34). The classical commentators offered several readings, and the popular story of a demon taking Sulayman’s form is a later legend. The verse’s point is that Sulayman was tested and turned back to Allah.
