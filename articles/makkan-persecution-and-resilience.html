The Makkan Phase: Persecution, Patience and the Early Believers

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The Makkan period is often summarised as “thirteen years of patience”. That phrase is true but too smooth. It was thirteen years of preaching, argument, social pressure, torture, boycott, loss and repeated refusal. The early Muslims were not a large movement with a plan for statehood; they were a small, vulnerable community learning what it meant to obey Allah when obedience cost them their safety.

This article follows the main stages of the Makkan phase and the Qur’anic response to each. It relies on the Qur’an, the Sirah of Ibn Hisham, and the hadith collections. Where a famous story comes from a later historian rather than an authentic hadith, it says so.

Key takeaways
  • The first revelation began in the cave of Hira and was followed by a pause that tested the Prophet ﷺ.
  • Preaching was private for about three years, then public; the opposition was driven by religion, status, economics and politics together.
  • The first targets were the vulnerable: enslaved people, the poor and those without tribal protection.
  • The boycott of Banu Hashim and the Year of Sorrow were severe social and personal tests.
  • The migration to Abyssinia shows that seeking safety and a just ruler is a legitimate prophetic strategy.
  • Ta’if was a rejection, but the Prophet ﷺ refused vengeance and chose patience.
  • The Qur’an does not treat suffering as proof of abandonment; it reframes it as a trial and a preparation.

1. The First Revelation and the Pause

The Prophet ﷺ had begun to retreat to the cave of Hira outside Makkah for reflection. At about the age of forty, during Ramadan, the angel Jibril appeared and commanded him: “Read!” He replied that he could not read. The angel pressed him and recited the opening verses of Surah al-‘Alaq:

“Read in the name of your Lord who created — created man from a clinging substance. Read, and your Lord is the most Generous — who taught by the pen — taught man that which he knew not.”
Qur’an 96:1–5

He returned home trembling and asked Khadijah to cover him. She reassured him, saying that Allah would not abandon him because he maintained family ties, bore the burdens of others, earned what was not his, honoured guests and helped the oppressed. She then took him to her cousin Waraqa ibn Nawfal, a Christian who recognised the pattern of revelation and warned that his people would oppose him. The account is preserved in Sahih al-Bukhari (Book of Revelation) and in the Sirah.

Then the revelation paused. The Prophet ﷺ was deeply distressed, fearing that he had been abandoned. The pause ended with Surah al-Muddaththir or Surah al-Duha, depending on the report: “Your Lord has not taken leave of you, nor has He detested you” (93:3). The experience taught him that revelation was not under his control. It was a gift, and the gap between messages was itself a test.

2. Secret Preaching, Then the Public Call

For roughly three years the call was private. The Prophet ﷺ invited those closest to him: Khadijah, Abu Bakr, Ali, Zayd ibn Harithah and a small circle of trusted people. Abu Bakr in turn brought others. The first community was built through personal relationships, not public spectacle.

Then came the command to speak openly: “And warn your closest kindred” (26:214), and “So proclaim what you have been commanded” (15:94). The Prophet ﷺ stood on Safa and called the clans of Quraysh, asking whether they would believe him if he told them an army was behind the mountain. They said yes, for they knew him as al-Amin. He then warned them of a severe punishment. Abu Lahab mocked him, and Surah al-Masad was revealed: “May the hands of Abu Lahab be ruined, and ruined is he” (111:1).

The public call changed the nature of the conflict. It was no longer a private devotion but a challenge to the religious, social and economic order of Makkah. The Qur’an began to answer the objections directly, defending the oneness of Allah, the resurrection, the status of the Prophet ﷺ and the moral accountability of the powerful.

3. Why Quraysh Opposed the Message

It is tempting to explain the opposition as simple ignorance. The Qur’an presents something more complex. Quraysh understood the message; they resisted its consequences.

  • Religious authority: the Prophet ﷺ denied the efficacy of the idols and the intercession of the goddesses. This threatened the custodianship of the Kaaba and the pilgrimage economy built around it.
  • Social status: the message declared that the noble and the weak were equal before Allah. For a society organised around lineage, this was destabilising.
  • Economic interest: the sanctuary and the pilgrimage brought trade and prestige. A new religion with a universal claim could disrupt that system.
  • Political power: the clans feared that a prophet from Banu Hashim would concentrate authority in one family.
  • Ancestral custom: the Qur’an records the argument that they found their fathers on a path and would follow their footsteps (43:22–24). Tradition was a source of identity, not merely a set of ideas.

The Qur’an also records their accusations: that the Prophet ﷺ was a poet, a soothsayer, a madman or a man taught by others (16:103; 21:5; 26:224–227; 68:2). These were not neutral descriptions; they were attempts to discredit the message without engaging it. At the same time, some Makkans were moved by the Qur’an despite themselves. The Prophet ﷺ recited to them, and they called it “magic” because it divided families — a backhanded admission of its power.

4. The Vulnerable Believers and the First Martyrs

The first Muslims included people with powerful protectors and people with none. Abu Bakr, Umar (after his conversion), Hamzah and Abbas belonged to influential clans. Bilal was an enslaved Abyssinian; Khabbab ibn al-Aratt was a client; Sumayyah and Yasir were an elderly couple with no tribal shield; Ammar ibn Yasir was their son.

Quraysh targeted the unprotected. Bilal was dragged over hot stones and forced to deny his faith; he repeated “Ahad, Ahad” — One, One. Abu Bakr bought and freed him. Sumayyah was killed by Abu Jahl, becoming the first martyr in Islam. Yasir died under torture. Khabbab was made to lie on burning coals. The Prophet ﷺ could not stop the torture, but he acknowledged their suffering and promised them Paradise.

These stories are not decorative. They show that the early community was not a movement of the elite. It included the poor, the enslaved, women, young people and foreigners. The Qur’an’s insistence on justice for the weak was not an abstraction; it was written in the bodies of its first believers.

The Prophet ﷺ also advised some believers to conceal their faith when concealment would save their lives. The Qur’an permits the denial of faith under compulsion while the heart remains firm (16:106), a concession that recognises the reality of torture without pretending that heroism is always possible.

5. The Boycott and the Year of Sorrow

As the movement grew, Quraysh escalated from individual persecution to collective pressure. They imposed a social and economic boycott on Banu Hashim, the Prophet’s clan, and on Banu al-Muttalib who protected him. The agreement was written and hung in the Kaaba. The clans were to refrain from trade, marriage and social relations with the boycotted families.

The boycott lasted about three years. The Prophet’s ﷺ relatives, including some who had not accepted Islam, endured hunger and isolation in a valley outside Makkah. Eventually the agreement was revoked, according to the Sirah, after several Makkans objected to its injustice and the document was found to have been eaten by insects. The story of the document is a later report, not an authentic hadith, but the boycott itself is well attested in the earliest Sirah.

Around 619 CE, the Prophet ﷺ lost two of his strongest supports: his uncle Abu Talib, who had protected him without accepting Islam, and his wife Khadijah, his first believer and closest companion. The year is called the Year of Sorrow (‘am al-huzn). The loss was personal and political at once. Without Abu Talib’s clan protection, the Prophet ﷺ was more exposed than ever.

The Qur’an does not promise believers a life without loss. It promises that loss is not the end of the story. The Prophet’s ﷺ grief in this period is a model of how faith and sorrow can coexist without denial.

6. The Migration to Abyssinia

When persecution intensified, the Prophet ﷺ advised some believers to migrate to Abyssinia (al-Habashah), where a Christian king, the Negus (al-Najashi), was known for justice. The first group went around 615 CE; a second, larger group followed. Among them were Ja’far ibn Abi Talib, Asma bint Umays, Uthman ibn Affan and Ruqayyah.

Quraysh sent envoys with gifts to persuade the Negus to hand the Muslims over. The Negus asked the Muslims about their religion. Ja’far recited Surah Maryam, and the king and his bishops were moved. He refused to surrender the refugees, saying that he would not give up people who had come to his land seeking protection. The report is preserved in the Sirah and later histories; the exact dialogue may be elaborated, but the migration and the protection are established.

The episode matters for several reasons. It shows that the Prophet ﷺ was willing to send believers to a non-Muslim ruler when that ruler was just. It shows that Muslims could present their faith respectfully to a Christian court. And it shows that migration is not a failure; it can be a strategy of survival and witness. Some Muslims later returned to Makkah, while others stayed until the Hijrah.

7. Ta’if and the Refusal of Revenge

After the Year of Sorrow, the Prophet ﷺ travelled to Ta’if, hoping that the Thaqif tribe would accept the message or at least protect him. They rejected him and set their young men and slaves to pelt him with stones as he left. He was wounded and sought shelter in a vineyard belonging to two Makkans.

According to the well-known report, the angel of the mountains came to him and offered to crush the people of Ta’if between the mountains. The Prophet ﷺ refused, saying that he hoped Allah would bring forth from their descendants people who would worship Him alone. The report is found in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, though the wording varies.

The refusal of revenge is one of the most important moments in the Seerah. The Prophet ﷺ had been physically attacked and rejected by a whole town. He could have asked for destruction. Instead he asked for their descendants. This is not weakness; it is a deliberate choice to break the cycle of vengeance that defined the tribal order.

Ta’if also shows that the Prophet ﷺ did not rely on miracles to compel belief. He could have called down punishment; he chose to continue preaching. The Qur’an would later describe him as a mercy to the worlds (21:107), and Ta’if is one of the clearest illustrations of that mercy.

8. The Qur’anic Response to Persecution

The Makkan Qur’an speaks directly to a persecuted community. It does not tell believers that their suffering is imaginary or that faith will make them comfortable. It tells them that trials are part of the prophetic path:

“Do the people think that they will be left to say, “We believe,” and they will not be tried?”
Qur’an 29:2

It reminds them that earlier communities faced worse: “Or do you think that you will enter Paradise while such [trial] has not yet come to you as came to those who passed on before you?” (2:214). It promises that Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity (2:286) and that He is with the patient (2:153).

The Makkan Qur’an also tells stories of persecuted believers from the past. Surah al-Buruj (85) describes the people of the trench who were killed for their faith, and it promises that the perpetrators will face the Fire. Surah al-Kahf (18) tells of young men who fled to a cave to protect their faith. Surah Yusuf (12) tells of betrayal, imprisonment and eventual vindication. These narratives gave the early Muslims a vocabulary for their own experience.

At the same time, the Qur’an permits self-defence and migration. It does not command passivity in the face of aggression. The Makkan phase was a period of restraint because the community was weak and the command to fight had not yet been given. The later Madinan verses would establish the ethics of war and peace in a different context. Reading the Makkan verses as a permanent prohibition of resistance, or the Madinan verses as a permanent licence for aggression, both distort the Qur’an’s historical guidance.

9. What the Makkan Phase Teaches

The Makkan period is a school in steadfastness. Several lessons stand out:

  • Truth is not validated by numbers. The early Muslims were few and powerless, yet the message was true.
  • The vulnerable matter most. The community’s credibility was tested by how it treated the enslaved, the poor and the unprotected.
  • Patience is active. The Prophet ﷺ kept preaching, negotiating, sending believers to safety and seeking allies. He did not simply wait.
  • Revenge is not the goal. Ta’if shows that the Prophet ﷺ wanted transformation, not destruction.
  • Institutions matter. The migration to Abyssinia and later to Madinah were strategic moves to create spaces where the community could live and grow.
  • Grief is not faithlessness. The Year of Sorrow shows that the Prophet ﷺ mourned deeply and continued his mission.

For Muslims today, the Makkan phase is not a template for victimhood. It is a reminder that faithfulness may cost something, that solidarity with the weak is non-negotiable, and that the long arc of a community’s life is not determined by its first difficult chapter.

10. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for this period are the Qur’an, the Sirah of Ibn Hisham, the hadith collections and the later histories of al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir. Each has limits:

  • The Qur’an alludes to events but rarely gives names, dates or full narratives.
  • Ibn Hisham’s Sirah preserves early reports but also includes weak and legendary material.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim contain authentic reports about the first revelation, the boycott’s context and Ta’if, but not a continuous chronology.
  • Al-Tabari and later historians compiled reports without always grading them.
  • Poetry and genealogy can illuminate the period but were transmitted orally and edited later.

This article therefore distinguishes between the Qur’anic anchor, authentic hadith, early Sirah reports and later popular additions. It does not claim to settle every historical detail.

Editorial note: the Makkan chronology is approximate. The order of some events and the exact duration of the boycott vary in the sources. The article follows the conventional sequence without presenting disputed details as certain.

11. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. How long was the Makkan period?

About thirteen years, from the first revelation to the Hijrah in 622 CE. The exact length depends on the date assigned to the first revelation, but the conventional figure is thirteen years.

2. Why did the Prophet ﷺ not fight back in Makkah?

The community was small and vulnerable, and the command to fight had not yet been given. The Prophet ﷺ chose restraint, preaching, negotiation and migration. Later, in Madinah, the context changed and the Qur’an gave detailed rules for defence and peace.

3. Was the migration to Abyssinia a permanent separation?

No. Some Muslims stayed for years, and some returned to Makkah or later joined the Hijrah to Madinah. It was a temporary refuge, not a schism.

4. Did the Prophet ﷺ really refuse to destroy Ta’if?

Yes, according to reports in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. The angel of the mountains offered to crush the town, and the Prophet ﷺ refused, hoping that their descendants would worship Allah.

5. Is the story of the boycott document being eaten by insects authentic?

The boycott itself is well attested in the early Sirah. The detail that the document was eaten by insects is a later report and should be described as such, not as an authentic hadith.
