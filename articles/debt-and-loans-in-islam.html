Debt and Loans in Islam: Borrowing, Repayment and Relief

NoorDeen Editorial · 13 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Debt is one of the few subjects the Prophet ﷺ sought refuge from in the same breath as sin. It can be a mercy that allows a family to buy a home, start a business or survive illness, and it can become a chain that outlives the borrower. Islamic teaching neither romanticises poverty nor treats borrowing as shameful. It insists that the transaction be honest, documented, repayable and just.

This article explains the Quranic and Prophetic framework, the difference between a loan and a sale, the prohibition of riba, the ethics of lending and collection, and what to do when repayment becomes impossible. It is an educational overview, not a fatwa on a particular mortgage, credit card or bankruptcy.

Key takeaways
  • Borrowing is permitted, but debt is a serious trust and should not be entered casually.
  • A loan is a kindness, not a profit-making transaction; riba is prohibited.
  • Deferred debts should be written down with clear terms and witnesses.
  • Repaying on time is an obligation; delaying when able is injustice.
  • A creditor must grant time to a debtor in genuine hardship and is encouraged to forgive.
  • Guarantees and collateral are permitted, but they must not become instruments of exploitation.

1. The Seriousness of Debt

The Prophet ﷺ taught his companions to seek refuge from debt. In a well-known supplication he asked Allah to protect him from sin and from being overwhelmed by debt, and he explained that a person in debt may speak and then lie, and promise and then break the promise (reported in Sahih al-Bukhari). The point is not that every borrower is dishonest. It is that financial pressure distorts speech, judgment and relationships.

Another report states that the soul of a believer is suspended by his debt until it is paid (al-Tirmidhi 1078; Ibn Majah 2413; graded hasan by many scholars). Unpaid debt is not a private matter that disappears at the funeral.

At the same time, Islam does not forbid borrowing. The Prophet ﷺ himself borrowed and pledged his armour to a Jewish man for barley (Sahih al-Bukhari 2513), and he said that whoever takes people's wealth intending to repay it, Allah will repay on his behalf (Sahih al-Bukhari 2387). The intention to repay is the dividing line between a loan and a wrong. Borrow only when necessary, borrow an amount one can realistically repay, and treat the obligation as a trust from the first day.

2. Borrowing and the Beautiful Loan

The Qur'an speaks of lending to Allah a “goodly loan” (qard hasan), promising to multiply it many times over (2:245; 57:11; 64:17). The expression is metaphorical: what is given to a needy person is received by Allah as a loan of faithfulness.

The borrower's side of the beautiful loan is equally serious. The Qur'an praises those who fulfil their promises and warns against consuming wealth unjustly. A person who borrows with no intention to repay, or who hides assets while claiming poverty, has turned a kindness into a theft.

3. Riba and the Cost of Money

The Qur'an prohibits riba in the strongest terms. It contrasts interest with trade (2:275), warns those who persist of war from Allah and His Messenger (2:279), condemns the doubled and multiplied interest of the pre-Islamic practice (3:130), and rejects the belief that interest increases wealth with Allah (30:39). The Prophet ﷺ cursed the one who consumes riba, the one who pays it, the one who records it and the two witnesses (Sahih Muslim 1598).

Riba is not simply “any charge”. A seller may charge a profit; a service provider may charge a fee; a lender may recover genuine documented costs in some analyses. The prohibited element is an increase charged for the mere passage of time on a loan of money, or an exchange of like commodities that is unequal or delayed. Modern products mix these elements. A credit card may combine an annual fee, a merchant fee and a monthly interest charge; a mortgage may be structured as a conventional loan or as a diminishing partnership; a buy-now-pay-later plan may contain a late fee that functions as a penalty or as interest. Each needs its own analysis, and it is not honest to relabel interest as a “service charge” and assume the ruling changes.

4. Write It Down

The Qur'an devotes its longest verse to debt documentation:

“O you who have believed, when you contract a debt for a specified term, write it down. And let a scribe write it between you in justice... And bring to witness two witnesses from among your men.”
Qur'an 2:282

The verse addresses the amount, the term, the scribe, the witnesses and the vulnerability of the weak. It ends by commanding that testimony not be concealed. The purpose is to protect both sides: the creditor from denial, the debtor from exaggeration, and the community from litigation.

A useful record includes the principal, the currency, the date, the repayment schedule, any security, the identities and signatures of the parties, and what happens if a payment is missed. For a family loan, a simple signed note and a bank transfer reference are enough to prevent most disputes. For a business loan, a lawyer should draft the document.

5. Repayment, Delay and the Duty to Pay

Repayment is a religious obligation, not a favour. The Prophet ﷺ said that the delay of a wealthy debtor is injustice (Sahih al-Bukhari 2404; Sahih Muslim 1564). A person who can pay but chooses to postpone is wronging the creditor, even if the creditor is patient or embarrassed to ask.

The borrower should prioritise the debt, communicate before a payment is missed, and avoid new borrowing to service old borrowing without a realistic plan. If a payment date is genuinely impossible, the earlier the conversation, the more options remain.

The creditor should ask politely, in private, and without humiliation. The Prophet ﷺ praised the one who is easy-going when selling, buying and demanding repayment (Sahih al-Bukhari 2076). Reminding a debtor of one's generosity in public is a form of injury that the Qur'an warns against (2:264). A voluntary gift at repayment is different from a contractual increase: if the borrower chooses, without prior condition or expectation, to give a little extra as thanks, scholars generally permit it. If the extra is required by the contract, it is riba.

6. Hardship, Insolvency and Forgiveness

The Qur'an gives a clear instruction for the debtor who cannot pay:

“And if someone is in hardship, then let there be postponement until a time of ease. But if you give from your right as charity, then it is better for you, if you only knew.”
Qur'an 2:280

This is not optional courtesy. It is a command to the creditor. The next verse reminds both parties of the Day when every soul will be compensated and none will be wronged (2:281).

The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever relieves a believer of a distress in this world, Allah will relieve him of a distress on the Day of Resurrection (Sahih Muslim 2699). He also warned against the creditor who refuses to give time to a debtor in difficulty. Insolvency is not a moral failure: a business can fail, a person can lose a job, and an illness can consume savings. Running away, hiding assets or lying about one's means is a different matter. Forgiving a debt is a voluntary act of great reward, but it should not be used to pressure a creditor who cannot afford it.

7. The Ethics of Lending

A lender holds power. Islamic teaching places several restraints on it:

  • No riba: the loan may not generate a contractual increase for the passage of time.
  • No exploitation of urgency: a person in crisis should not be charged more because they have no alternative.
  • No humiliation: the debt should not be used to control, shame or gossip about the borrower.
  • No hidden conditions: any security, guarantee or fee must be disclosed and understood.
  • No unlawful purpose: one may not lend money for gambling, alcohol, fraud or other haram.
  • No indefinite entanglement: a clear repayment date protects both parties.

The Qur'an praises those who lend without expecting repayment from people, seeking only Allah's countenance (2:272; 2:264). That spirit does not cancel the contract; it shapes the manner in which the contract is enforced. A lender who uses a debtor's need to demand favours, silence or loyalty has turned a financial relationship into a form of control. The Qur'an's prohibition of consuming wealth unjustly (4:29) covers that too.

8. Collateral, Guarantees and Sureties

Islamic law permits security for a debt. The Qur'an mentions a pledge (rahn) when a scribe is unavailable on a journey (2:283), and the Prophet ﷺ pledged his armour for barley (Sahih al-Bukhari 2513). A pledge gives the creditor a right over specified property if the debt is not paid, but the property remains a trust and any surplus must be returned.

A guarantee (kafalah) is also permitted. A guarantor takes on the obligation to pay if the debtor cannot, and the Prophet ﷺ said that the guarantor is liable (reported in the Sunan). Guaranteeing a loan is a serious commitment, not a ceremonial signature. A person should know the amount, the term and the risk before agreeing.

Both collateral and guarantees can become unjust when the creditor takes more than the debt, charges excessive fees, or uses the threat of seizure to extract unrelated concessions. The objective is security, not punishment. Where the law requires registration or a written security document, a Muslim should comply: an unregistered pledge may be religiously intended but legally unenforceable, and the resulting dispute can harm both parties.

9. Modern Debt: Mortgages, Cards and Buy Now, Pay Later

Most Muslims today encounter debt through products that did not exist in the classical period. The principles still apply, but the analysis is detailed:

  • Mortgages: a conventional interest-bearing mortgage contains riba. Islamic alternatives use structures such as diminishing partnership or murabaha, and their details need review.
  • Credit cards: paying the full balance monthly may avoid interest, but the card agreement and the merchant fees still need analysis. Carrying a balance at interest is riba.
  • Buy now, pay later: the key questions are whether a fee is charged for delay, whether the price is fixed at the outset, and whether the provider is acting as a seller or a lender.
  • Student loans: the structure varies by country. Some are income-contingent and subsidised; others are commercial. The terms matter.
  • Business loans: conventional interest is riba; partnership and trade-based structures are the alternatives.
  • National and corporate debt: a Muslim may be a citizen, employee or investor in a system that uses interest without personally approving every transaction. The level of involvement and the specific role matter.

This is not a fatwa on any of these products. A person should ask a scholar who knows the local market, and should not rely on a generic website answer for a thirty-year commitment. A lender that offers “interest-free” branding may still include fees that function as riba; read the total cost, not the label.

10. A Practical Plan for Debt

For a person carrying debt, a disciplined plan is better than shame:

  • List every debt: creditor, balance, interest rate, minimum payment and due date.
  • Separate essential debts from discretionary ones, and stop adding new discretionary debt.
  • Build a bare-bones monthly budget that still covers food, housing, medicine and transport.
  • Contact creditors early if a payment will be missed; ask for a realistic arrangement in writing.
  • Prioritise the highest-cost debt while maintaining minimum payments on the rest, unless a scholar advises otherwise.
  • Sell unused assets, take available work and avoid “debt consolidation” that adds new interest and fees.
  • Seek zakat, charitable hardship funds or family help where genuinely needed, without deception.
  • Make dua, but do not treat dua as a substitute for a budget.

If the burden has become unmanageable, seek professional debt advice and, where available, a formal insolvency process. Hiding from creditors, borrowing from one to pay another without a plan, and lying to family are not solutions; they multiply the harm.

11. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Is all interest riba?

The classical prohibition covers an increase charged for the mere passage of time on a loan of money, and unequal or delayed exchange of like commodities. Modern fees and products need individual analysis. A fee that is genuinely for a service may differ from interest, but relabelling does not change the substance. Ask a qualified scholar.

2. Can I lend money to a family member?

Yes, and it can be a beautiful loan. Write down the amount and repayment terms, keep a record of payments, and do not attach interest or humiliating conditions. Clear documentation protects the relationship as well as the money.

3. What if I cannot repay a loan?

Speak to the creditor before the deadline, explain the situation honestly, and propose a realistic plan. The Qur'an commands the creditor to grant time to a debtor in hardship (2:280). Do not hide assets or disappear. Seek advice and, where needed, a formal insolvency process.

4. Does debt prevent a person from entering Paradise?

The reports warn that unpaid debt is a serious matter and that the soul may be suspended until it is paid. Scholars explain this as a warning, not a final verdict that a debtor is doomed. Repentance, repayment, and the creditor's forgiveness are the way forward. Debt is not a reason to despair of Allah's mercy.
