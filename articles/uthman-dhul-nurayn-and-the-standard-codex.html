Uthman: The Standard Codex and the Fitna

NoorDeen Editorial · 13 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Uthman ibn Affan is the caliph whose greatest achievement and deepest tragedy are bound to the same thing: the unity of the community. He gave the Muslims a standard written Qur’an so that they would not differ over recitation, and he was killed by Muslims who believed he had betrayed the trust of the caliphate. Both facts belong to his story, and neither can be understood without the other.

He was a wealthy merchant who spent his fortune on the community, a man of modesty and generosity, and a ruler whose later years were overwhelmed by grievances he could not resolve. The sources are full of praise and full of criticism. This article follows the established record and marks where the later narratives, especially the polemical ones, go beyond the evidence.

Key takeaways
  • Uthman was an early convert from the Banu Umayya and one of the wealthiest men in Makkah.
  • He married two daughters of the Prophet ﷺ, Ruqayyah and then Umm Kulthum, and was called Dhul-Nurayn.
  • He migrated to Abyssinia and then to Madinah, and financed the army of Tabuk.
  • He bought the well of Rumah and gave it to the Muslims.
  • As caliph he commissioned the standard Qur’anic codex and ordered the destruction of variant copies.
  • His caliphate saw the conquest of Armenia, Cyprus and parts of North Africa, but also growing internal grievance.
  • He was besieged in his house and killed in 35 AH, an event that opened the first fitna.
  • The claim that he was buried in a Jewish cemetery, or that he was a secret Christian, is later polemic and has no basis in the early sources.

1. Why Uthman Matters

Uthman’s caliphate lasted about twelve years, from 23 AH to 35 AH. It began with the conquest of new territories and ended with the caliph besieged in his own house. In between, he made the decision that gave the Muslim world a single written Qur’an, and he faced the first serious internal opposition to a caliph’s authority.

His story is essential for understanding the fitna, the civil conflict that followed his death. The grievances against him were real, even if some of the accusations were exaggerated. The sources preserve the voices of his critics and his defenders, and the modern reader must weigh them rather than choose a side in advance.

Uthman is also a case study in the danger of wealth and family. He was generous with his own money, but his appointment of relatives to important posts created the impression of dynastic favouritism. Whether that impression was fair is debated; that it shaped the politics of the time is not.

2. The Merchant of Umayya

Uthman was born into the Banu Umayya, one of the most powerful clans of Quraysh. His father, Affan ibn Abi al-As, was a merchant, and Uthman became one of the richest men in Makkah. The sources describe him as a successful trader who travelled to Syria and Yemen and who was known for his honesty and his modesty.

He was not a warrior by temperament. He was soft-spoken, generous and deeply shy. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said that even the angels were shy of Uthman, a report that the sources preserve with different wordings. His wealth gave him independence, and he used it for the community rather than for display.

Before Islam he was known as a man of good character. The sources do not present him as a seeker or a rebel. His conversion came through Abu Bakr, and it was immediate.

3. Conversion and the Two Migrations

Uthman accepted Islam early, through the invitation of Abu Bakr. He and his wife Ruqayyah, the daughter of the Prophet ﷺ, migrated to Abyssinia in the first migration, and then to Madinah. The sources call him one of the two migrations, and the experience shaped his later commitment to the community.

In Madinah he became one of the Prophet’s closest companions. He was not among the most prominent fighters, but he was trusted with sensitive missions. He was the Prophet’s envoy to the Quraysh at Hudaybiyyah, and the rumour of his death triggered the pledge of al-Ridwan.

His wife Ruqayyah died in Madinah, and the Prophet ﷺ later married him to her sister Umm Kulthum. The two marriages gave him the title Dhul-Nurayn, “the possessor of two lights.” The title is later, but the marriages are established.

4. The Two Lights and the Household

The marriage to two daughters of the Prophet ﷺ is unique among the companions. Ruqayyah was the wife of Uthman when the revelation began, and she migrated with him to Abyssinia. She died in 2 AH, after the battle of Badr, while Uthman was nursing her. The Prophet ﷺ later married Umm Kulthum to him.

The sources report that Umm Kulthum also died during the Prophet’s lifetime, and that the Prophet ﷺ said that if he had a third daughter he would have married her to Uthman. The report is widely cited, though its exact wording varies. It expresses the Prophet’s trust in Uthman rather than a literal plan.

The title Dhul-Nurayn became a standard way of referring to him in Sunni literature. It should not be used to imply that he was sinless or that his caliphate was beyond criticism. The title honours his family relationship to the Prophet ﷺ, not his political record.

5. The Well of Rumah and the Army of Tabuk

Two stories illustrate Uthman’s generosity. The first is the well of Rumah, a well in Madinah that belonged to a Jew and whose water was sold at a high price. Uthman bought the well and gave it to the Muslims, and the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have promised paradise to whoever bought it.

The second is the army of Tabuk. In 9 AH the Prophet ﷺ prepared an expedition to the north, and the community was short of supplies. Uthman financed a large part of the army, and the sources say that the Prophet ﷺ said, “Nothing will harm Uthman after what he has done today.” The exact number of camels and the amount of money vary in the reports.

These stories are important because they show the positive side of his wealth. The same money that later made him vulnerable to accusations of favouritism was used to feed and equip the community. The sources preserve both sides.

6. The Standard Qur’anic Codex

Uthman’s most enduring achievement was the standardisation of the Qur’anic text. As the community expanded, different regions began to recite according to different readings, and disputes arose. Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman, returning from the frontiers, warned Uthman that the community might differ over the Qur’an as the Jews and Christians had differed over their scriptures.

Uthman consulted the companions and commissioned a committee led by Zayd ibn Thabit to copy the codex that Abu Bakr had collected. The committee produced a standard text, and Uthman sent copies to the major centres, ordering that other written copies be destroyed or corrected. The sources describe the process in some detail, and the codex became known as the Uthmani mushaf.

The decision was controversial. Some companions objected to the destruction of variant copies, and some regions resisted the standardisation. But the result was a single written text that has remained remarkably stable across centuries. The Qur’an’s own promise of preservation (15:9) was understood by the community as being fulfilled through this process.

Source caution. Uthman did not create a new Qur’an. He standardised the written text based on the codex already collected under Abu Bakr. The claim that he removed or added verses is a later polemic and is contradicted by the manuscript evidence and the unanimous testimony of the early community.

7. The Caliphate and the Conquests

Uthman became caliph in 23 AH after Umar’s assassination. The conquests continued: Armenia, Cyprus and parts of North Africa were added, and the navy began to operate in the Mediterranean. The sources record campaigns in Khurasan, Sistan and the Caucasus.

The expansion was not without setbacks. The Byzantines counter-attacked, and the Muslims suffered losses at sea and on the frontiers. The caliphate also faced financial and administrative problems as the empire grew. Uthman appointed governors, some of whom were relatives, and their conduct became a source of grievance.

The conquests under Uthman are often overshadowed by the fitna, but they were substantial. The community was still expanding when the internal crisis began, and the two developments were connected: the wealth and offices of the conquests created competition among the elite.

8. The Grievances

The grievances against Uthman were of several kinds. He was accused of appointing relatives to key positions, of allowing governors to accumulate wealth, of distributing land and spoils in ways that favoured certain groups, and of being too lenient with his family. The sources preserve specific complaints from Kufa, Basra and Egypt.

Some of the complaints were exaggerated or politically motivated. Others reflected real problems. Uthman’s style was different from Umar’s: he was more trusting, less willing to confront his governors, and more inclined to conciliation. In a rapidly changing empire, that style was read as weakness or favouritism.

The opposition coalesced around a group of malcontents from Egypt and Iraq who came to Madinah with demands. Uthman negotiated with them and made concessions, but the situation escalated. The sources differ on the role of agitators, on the sincerity of the demands and on Uthman’s response. What is clear is that the caliph lost control of the political narrative.

9. The Siege

In 35 AH a group of protesters besieged Uthman’s house in Madinah. The siege lasted for weeks, according to the sources, and the caliph refused to use force against the besiegers. He is reported to have said that he would not be the first successor of the Prophet ﷺ to shed the blood of his community.

His decision has been debated ever since. Some see it as a noble refusal to start a civil war; others see it as a failure of leadership that allowed the situation to deteriorate. The sources also report that some companions urged him to resist and that others advised him to compromise.

The siege was not a simple confrontation between a tyrant and the people. It involved factions, rumours and outside manipulation. The sources are partisan, and the modern reader should be cautious about accepting any single account as complete.

10. The Assassination

Uthman was killed in his house in 35 AH. The sources say that the attackers entered the house, and that his wife Nailah was injured trying to protect him. He was reciting the Qur’an when he was killed, according to several reports, and his blood stained the page.

The identity of the killers and the exact sequence of events are disputed. The sources name different individuals and groups, and the political factions of the later period had reasons to shape the story. What is not disputed is that the caliph was killed by Muslims, and that the community was plunged into civil war.

Uthman was buried in Madinah, and his grave became a site of mourning and controversy. The claim that he was buried in a Jewish cemetery is a later polemic and has no basis in the early sources. His body was buried by the Muslims, and the location is recorded in the biographical literature.

11. The Fitna and Its Aftermath

Uthman’s death opened the first fitna. Ali ibn Abi Talib was chosen as caliph, but his authority was contested by Mu‘awiyah ibn Abi Sufyan and by A’ishah, Talha and al-Zubayr. The community divided, and the conflicts of the Camel and Siffin followed.

The fitna was not caused by Uthman’s personality alone. It was the product of rapid expansion, the distribution of wealth, the rivalry of the Qurayshi clans, the absence of a clear mechanism for succession, and the tensions between the early companions and the newer converts. Uthman’s caliphate was the point at which those tensions became visible.

The later Sunni tradition honours Uthman as the third caliph and rejects the claim that he was a tyrant. The Shia tradition takes a more critical view. The historical sources do not support a simple narrative of innocence or guilt, and the reader should approach the fitna with humility.

12. Death, Burial and Legacy

Uthman was about eighty years old when he died, according to several reports. His caliphate had lasted twelve years. He left behind the standard Qur’anic codex, a community that had expanded to three continents, and a political crisis that would define the next generation.

His legacy is inseparable from the codex. Every written Qur’an in the world today traces its ancestry to the text that his committee produced. That is an extraordinary achievement for a man whose political career ended in siege and murder.

He is also remembered for his generosity, his modesty and his family ties to the Prophet ﷺ. The Sunni tradition calls him Dhul-Nurayn and counts him among the rightly guided caliphs. The honest historian adds that his caliphate was a period of both achievement and failure, and that the failure was not only his.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

Uthman’s life teaches that a community can be united by a text and divided by politics. It also teaches that generosity and family loyalty can become political liabilities when they are not matched by clear institutions. His refusal to fight the besiegers is a reminder that the restraint of violence is a real moral choice, even when it has terrible consequences.

The sources for his caliphate are among the most partisan in early Islamic history. The Sunni and Shia narratives diverge sharply, and the medieval historians often shaped their accounts to defend a theological or political position. The modern reader should read widely and distinguish between established facts and later interpretation.

The most reliable anchors are the Qur’an, the authentic hadith on his virtues, the early biographical reports and the manuscript evidence for the codex. The polemical stories — the Jewish cemetery, the secret Christian, the forged letters — should be rejected or treated with extreme caution.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith, the early historical reports and later polemic. It honours Uthman’s achievement without claiming that his caliphate was free of serious political failure, and it does not adopt the sectarian caricatures of either side.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Why is Uthman called Dhul-Nurayn?

He married two daughters of the Prophet ﷺ, Ruqayyah and then Umm Kulthum. The title means “possessor of two lights” and honours that unique family relationship. It is a later title, but the marriages are established.

2. Did Uthman change the Qur’an?

No. He standardised the written text based on the codex collected under Abu Bakr, and he ordered variant copies to be corrected or destroyed so that the community would not differ over recitation. He did not add or remove revelation.

3. Who killed Uthman?

He was killed by a group of besiegers in his house in 35 AH. The sources name different individuals and factions, and the exact sequence is disputed. What is clear is that he was killed by Muslims, an event that opened the first fitna.

4. Was Uthman buried in a Jewish cemetery?

No. That claim is later polemic and has no basis in the early sources. He was buried by the Muslims in Madinah, and the location is recorded in the biographical literature.

5. Was Uthman a good caliph?

The Sunni tradition honours him as the third rightly guided caliph and recognises his achievements, especially the standard codex. Historians also recognise serious political failures in his later years. The answer depends on how one weighs achievement, intention and outcome.
