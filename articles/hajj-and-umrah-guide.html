Hajj and Umrah: A Learning Guide

NoorDeen Editorial · 13 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Hajj is the fifth pillar of Islam and an obligation once in a lifetime for every adult Muslim who is physically and financially able. Umrah is a lesser pilgrimage that may be performed at most times of the year. Both are acts of worship with precise rites, and both are physically demanding. This guide explains the sequence, the legal conditions, and the practical details that pilgrims most often misunderstand.

It is an educational overview, not a substitute for a qualified teacher or your country's official Hajj authority. Rites vary in small details between the schools; follow the instructions of the school you follow and the organisers guiding you.

Key takeaways
  • Hajj is obligatory once in a lifetime for those who are able.
  • Ihram begins at the miqat and imposes specific prohibitions.
  • The core rites run from 8 to 13 Dhul Hijjah, with Arafah on the 9th.
  • Tawaf is seven circuits of the Ka'bah; Sa'i is seven trips between Safa and Marwah.
  • Missing Arafah invalidates the Hajj; other omissions may be compensated.
  • Umrah consists of Ihram, Tawaf, Sa'i and shaving or trimming.

1. Hajj in the Qur'an and Sunnah

The Qur'an commands pilgrimage and describes its purpose:

“And proclaim to the people the Hajj; they will come to you on foot and on every lean camel, coming from every distant pass.”
Qur'an 22:27

It also states the obligation:

“And pilgrimage to the House is a duty owed to Allah by mankind — for whoever is able to find a way. And whoever disbelieves — then indeed, Allah is free from need of the worlds.”
Qur'an 3:97

The Prophet ﷺ performed one Hajj, the Farewell Pilgrimage in 632 CE, and taught the rites personally. He said: “Take your rites from me” (narrated in Sahih Muslim). The detailed sequence therefore comes from his practice, not from later custom.

Hajj is also a re-enactment of the story of Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il: the running between Safa and Marwah recalls Hajar's search for water, and the sacrifice recalls Ibrahim's trial.

2. Who Must Perform Hajj

The obligation applies to a Muslim who is:

  • Adult and of sound mind.
  • Free — historically this referred to slavery; today it means not under coercive restriction.
  • Physically able to undertake the journey and rites.
  • Financially able — possessing the cost of travel, accommodation and subsistence, while leaving adequate provision for dependants and debts.
  • Secure — the route and destination are safe enough to travel.

A woman's requirement of a mahram is debated. The majority of classical scholars require a mahram for travel, while others permit travel with a trustworthy group. Saudi regulations have changed over time and should be checked directly. This is a genuine difference; it should not be used to shame or pressure anyone.

Hajj is not due from someone who is in debt and unable to repay, or who has dependants in need. It is also valid to perform Hajj on behalf of a deceased or permanently incapacitated person, under conditions discussed by jurists.

3. Ihram: The Sacred State

Ihram is the state entered at the miqat — the designated boundary — with the intention to perform Hajj or Umrah. For men it involves wearing two unstitched white sheets; for women, ordinary modest clothing that does not cover the face or hands in the state of ihram according to most schools (though face covering is debated when near non-mahram men).

The prohibitions of ihram include: cutting hair or nails, using perfume, marital intimacy, hunting land game, and — for men — covering the head or wearing stitched clothing. Women may wear sewn clothes. Violations require expiation depending on the type.

The talbiyah is recited from the moment of ihram until the beginning of Tawaf for Umrah, or until the Day of Sacrifice for Hajj: “Labbayk Allahumma labbayk, labbayka la sharika laka labbayk, inna al-hamda wa al-ni'mata laka wa al-mulk, la sharika lak.”

Practical note: Carry unscented toiletries, keep your ihram clean, and learn the specific prohibitions before you travel. Many pilgrims accidentally use scented soap or deodorant.

4. The Rites Day by Day

8 Dhul Hijjah (Yawm al-Tarwiyah): Pilgrims enter ihram, travel to Mina, and pray Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha and the next Fajr there.

9 Dhul Hijjah (Yawm Arafah): The pivotal day. Pilgrims travel to Arafah, stand in supplication from after Dhuhr until sunset, and combine Dhuhr and Asr. This standing (wuquf) is the essential rite; missing it invalidates the Hajj.

Night of 9–10 (Muzdalifah): After sunset, pilgrims travel to Muzdalifah, pray Maghrib and Isha together, rest, and pray Fajr. Pebbles are collected here or at Mina.

10 Dhul Hijjah (Yawm al-Nahr): The busiest day. Pilgrims stone the large Jamarah, offer the sacrifice, shave or trim, and perform Tawaf al-Ifadah. They may then leave ihram (with some restrictions remaining until the final Tawaf).

11–13 Dhul Hijjah (Ayyam al-Tashriq): Pilgrims stay in Mina and stone all three Jamarat after Dhuhr each day. They may leave on the 12th after stoning if they depart before sunset, or stay until the 13th.

Final Tawaf: The Farewell Tawaf (Tawaf al-Wada') is performed before leaving Makkah.

The order is important but the schools differ on whether some steps may be delayed or advanced. If you forget a step, consult your group's scholar immediately rather than guessing.

5. Tawaf and Sa'i

Tawaf is seven circuits around the Ka'bah, beginning and ending at the Black Stone corner, with the Ka'bah on your left. Men are encouraged to walk briskly in the first three circuits (ramal) when possible. Each circuit includes the takbir and supplication; there is no fixed required supplication, so recite what you know.

Sa'i is seven trips between Safa and Marwah, beginning at Safa and ending at Marwah. It commemorates Hajar's search for water for Isma'il. Men may jog between the green markers; women walk.

Ritual purity is required for Tawaf according to most schools; menstruating women generally delay Tawaf until purity, though they may perform other rites. This is a significant practical issue and should be planned for.

Crowds can be dangerous. Do not push, do not stop abruptly in the middle of a circuit, and follow the designated flow. The reward of Hajj is not in physical risk-taking.

6. Arafah, Muzdalifah and Mina: The Heart of Hajj

Arafah is the spiritual summit. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Hajj is Arafah” (narrated by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi). Pilgrims stand in supplication, repentance and hope, often from midday until sunset. Many describe it as the closest experience to the Day of Judgement.

Muzdalifah is a night of simplicity: pilgrims sleep under the sky or in tents, pray, and rest. It is intentionally austere, reminding pilgrims of equality and detachment.

Mina is the tent city where pilgrims stay during the days of stoning. The Jamarat are stoned in a specific order: the small, then the middle, then the large on the 10th; all three on the 11th–13th. Pebbles should be small, and throwing should be done calmly.

The sacrifice (hady) is required for Hajj Tamattu' and Qiran, and recommended for Ifrad. It may be performed through an authorised agent. The meat is distributed to the poor.

7. Umrah: The Lesser Pilgrimage

Umrah consists of four acts: entering ihram at the miqat, Tawaf, Sa'i, and shaving or trimming the hair. It can be performed in a few hours, though crowds may make it longer.

Umrah is highly recommended but not obligatory according to the majority. It may be performed alongside Hajj (Tamattu' or Qiran) or separately. The Prophet ﷺ performed Umrah four times according to authentic reports.

The same ihram rules apply. Many pilgrims perform multiple Umrahs in one trip; scholars differ on whether this is recommended, and some discourage it because of crowding. One well-performed Umrah is better than several rushed ones.

8. Common Mistakes and Spiritual Preparation

The most common mistakes are practical and preventable:

  • Entering ihram after passing the miqat without doing so — a serious error requiring a sacrifice in many cases.
  • Using scented products while in ihram.
  • Assuming Tawaf begins anywhere other than the Black Stone corner.
  • Pushing or trampling others to touch the Ka'bah or the Black Stone.
  • Missing the standing at Arafah because of poor planning.
  • Delaying the sacrifice or shaving without understanding the order.
  • Treating Hajj as a sightseeing trip rather than an act of worship.

Spiritual preparation matters more than shopping. Settle debts, seek forgiveness, reconcile relationships, learn the rites, memorise the talbiyah and some supplications, and purify your intention. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Whoever performs Hajj and does not commit obscenity or sin returns as on the day his mother bore him” (Sahih al-Bukhari).

9. Practical and Health Checklist

  • Valid passport, visa, Hajj permit and vaccination records.
  • Copies of documents stored offline and in the cloud.
  • Comfortable footwear, blister plasters, and a small first-aid kit.
  • Unscented toiletries, sunscreen (unscented), and a water bottle.
  • Medications in original packaging with prescriptions.
  • A small bag for pebbles, a prayer mat, and a lightweight umbrella.
  • Emergency contacts and your group leader's number written on paper.
  • Physical preparation: walking, hydration and stamina in the weeks before travel.

Hajj is physically demanding, with long walks, heat and crowds. Anyone with heart, lung, kidney or mobility conditions should consult a doctor well in advance. Islam does not require you to harm yourself to perform a rite; the concession for inability is part of the law.

10. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Is Hajj valid without a mahram for a woman?

Classical schools differ. The majority require a mahram for travel, while other scholars permit travel with a trustworthy group or in safety. Saudi regulations also change. Follow your school and the official rules, and do not let this difference become a source of conflict.

2. Can I perform Hajj if I have debts?

Hajj is not obligatory until your debts are settled or you have a clear plan and permission to defer them. If you owe money and cannot repay, prioritise the debt. Voluntary Hajj while defaulting on debts is generally discouraged.

3. What happens if I miss a rite?

It depends on the rite. Missing Arafah invalidates the Hajj and requires repeating it in a later year. Other omissions may require a sacrifice, fasting, or a make-up. Consult a qualified scholar immediately; do not rely on guesswork.

4. Can someone perform Hajj on my behalf?

Yes, for a deceased person or someone permanently unable to perform it, under conditions set by jurists. The proxy must have performed Hajj for themselves first according to many scholars.

5. Is Umrah obligatory?

The majority view is that Umrah is highly recommended but not obligatory; the Hanafi school considers it obligatory once in a lifetime. Both positions are grounded in evidence and should be respected.
