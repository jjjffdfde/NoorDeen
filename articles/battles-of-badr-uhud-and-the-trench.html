Badr, Uhud and the Trench: War, Ethics and Survival

NoorDeen Editorial · 16 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Three battles in five years decided whether the community of Madinah would survive. Badr was a stunning deliverance; Uhud was a costly lesson in discipline; the Trench was a siege survived by endurance, weather and diplomacy. Together they shaped the Qur’anic vocabulary of trial, obedience, martyrdom and divine help.

This article narrates the three campaigns and the Qur’anic response to each. It also states the limits of the sources. The detailed numbers, speeches and single combat are preserved by Ibn Hisham, al-Tabari and al-Waqidi, and not all of that material meets the standard of authentic hadith. The Qur’an and the sound hadith give the theological and moral frame; the later historians give the narrative colour, which this article uses carefully.

Key takeaways
  • Badr (2 AH) was fought against a Quraysh army protecting a caravan; the Muslims were outnumbered and won.
  • The Qur’an presents Badr as divine help and a test of gratitude, not as a licence for arrogance.
  • Uhud (3 AH) was lost when the archers left their post; the Qur’an blames disobedience, not the enemy’s numbers.
  • The rumour that the Prophet ﷺ had been killed tested the community and produced the verse that he was a messenger who would die.
  • The Trench (5 AH) was a defensive siege; a trench, cold, hunger, a storm and diplomacy broke the Confederacy.
  • The aftermath with Banu Qurayzah is a difficult episode; the sources differ on numbers and the judgment was a tribal arbitration.
  • Later sources inflate numbers and add speeches; the article labels such material rather than presenting it as hadith.

1. War, Ethics and the Prophetic Example

The Qur’an does not present war as a good in itself. It permits fighting against those who fight the believers and drive them from their homes, and it commands that aggression not be exceeded:

“Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you but do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors.”
Qur’an 2:190

The Prophet ﷺ set limits that later jurists developed into a law of war: do not kill women, children, the elderly or monks; do not mutilate; do not betray; do not cut down fruit-bearing trees except when necessary; do not kill animals except for food. Abu Bakr repeated similar instructions to his armies. These rules were not always observed in later history, but they were the standard against which conduct was judged.

The early battles were not wars of conversion. The Qur’an insists that there is no compulsion in religion (2:256), and the fighting in this period was tied to the specific conflict with Quraysh: the seizure of Muslim property, the blockade of the community, and the threat of annihilation. Later conquests raised different questions, but they should not be read back into Badr as if the Prophet ﷺ had set out to conquer the world from the beginning.

2. The Road to Badr

The Muslims who left Makkah had lost homes, goods and businesses. The Qur’an acknowledges their expulsion and permits them to fight those who drove them out (22:39–40). Quraysh continued to organise caravans and to threaten the routes and allies of Madinah. The Prophet ﷺ sent small expeditions to monitor the routes and to signal that the Muslims were no longer passive.

One of these, led by Abdullah ibn Jahsh, reached Nakhlah in Rajab of the second year. A skirmish there resulted in the killing of a Quraysh man during a sacred month, which the Quraysh used for propaganda. The Qur’an responded that persecution and expulsion were worse than killing in the sacred month, while acknowledging the gravity of the act (2:217). The episode shows how easily a small operation could escalate.

The immediate occasion of Badr was a large caravan returning from Syria under Abu Sufyan. The Prophet ﷺ called the Muslims to intercept it. Abu Sufyan sent word to Makkah for help, and Quraysh marched out with a substantial army. The Muslims had about three hundred and thirteen men, few horses and limited armour. Before the battle the Prophet ﷺ consulted his companions, and the Ansar confirmed that they would fight, not merely defend their own homes. That consultation mattered: it turned a raid into a battle of the community.

3. Badr: The Day of the Criterion

The two armies met at the wells of Badr on the seventeenth of Ramadan in the second year after the Hijrah, conventionally 624 CE. The Muslims reached the water first and cut off the enemy’s access. The Prophet ﷺ spent the night in supplication, and the Qur’an describes the rain and the sleep that steadied the believers before the fight (8:11).

The battle opened with single combats. Ali, Hamzah and Ubaydah ibn al-Harith faced Utbah, Shaybah and al-Walid ibn Utbah; the Muslims prevailed. The general engagement followed. The Qur’an describes the believers as few in number and the enemy as numerous, and it attributes the victory to Allah:

“And already had Allah given you victory at Badr while you were few in number. Then fear Allah; perhaps you will be grateful.”
Qur’an 3:123

The Sirah reports that around seventy of Quraysh were killed and a similar number captured, while fourteen Muslims were martyred. These figures come from the historians and are broadly consistent across the early sources, though they should not be treated as exact statistics. The Qur’an calls the day al-Furqan, the criterion, because it separated the believers from their persecutors and vindicated the message in the eyes of many.

4. Prisoners, Spoils and the Meaning of Victory

Victory brought immediate practical questions. The fighters disagreed about the distribution of the spoils, and the Qur’an intervened: the spoils belong to Allah and His Messenger, and a fifth is set aside for Allah, the Messenger, the near relatives, orphans, the poor and the traveller (8:1, 8:41). This was not merely an accounting rule. It placed the gains of war under moral authority and made provision for the vulnerable.

The prisoners were a harder case. The Qur’an criticises the desire for worldly gain in the treatment of captives and says that Allah wants the Hereafter (8:67–68). The Prophet ﷺ consulted his companions; Abu Bakr favoured ransom, Umar favoured execution, and the Prophet ﷺ chose ransom. A report in the Sirah says that some prisoners who could read were allowed to teach Muslim children instead of paying a cash ransom. The story is well known but comes from the historians rather than an authentic hadith, and it should be labelled as such.

Badr did not end the conflict. It humiliated Quraysh, strengthened the Muslims’ standing with the tribes, and convinced many that the Prophet ﷺ was protected. But it also created a debt of revenge. The Qur’an warns the believers not to become arrogant, and it reminds them that help was a favour to be repaid with gratitude and obedience, not a permanent guarantee.

5. Uhud: The Revenge Campaign

Quraysh spent the following year preparing revenge. They gathered around three thousand men, financed in part by the profits of the caravan that had escaped at Badr, and marched towards Madinah under Abu Sufyan. The Prophet ﷺ consulted the community on whether to fight inside the town or meet the enemy in the open. He himself leaned towards defending Madinah, but the younger men and those eager for battle pressed for a march. He accepted the majority view.

The Muslim force numbered about seven hundred. Abdullah ibn Ubayy withdrew with his followers on the way, reducing the army and revealing the fragility of the coalition. The Prophet ﷺ positioned fifty archers on a pass on Mount Aynayn with strict orders to hold their ground whatever happened, so that the cavalry of Quraysh could not attack the Muslim rear.

The choice of Uhud was therefore a compromise between counsel and popular pressure. The Prophet ﷺ had warned that he had seen in a dream that he was wearing a breastplate and that a cow was being slaughtered, which he interpreted as a sign that it was better to stay in Madinah. The community chose otherwise. The episode is a study in consultation, and in the cost of ignoring wise counsel.

6. Uhud: The Battle and the Archers

The battle began well for the Muslims. They broke the Quraysh centre, and the enemy began to flee. At that point the archers on the pass saw the spoils being gathered and left their positions to join the pursuit, despite the Prophet’s ﷺ order to remain. Khalid ibn al-Walid, then still a leader of Quraysh, saw the gap and led his cavalry around the hill. The Muslims were attacked from behind and the tide turned.

The confusion was severe. A rumour spread that the Prophet ﷺ had been killed, and some believers lost heart. The Qur’an answers the rumour directly: Muhammad is a messenger, and messengers have died before him; if he died or was killed, would they turn back? (3:144). The Prophet ﷺ was in fact wounded in the face, and a link of his helmet pierced his cheek. Hamzah ibn Abd al-Muttalib, the Prophet’s uncle, was killed by Wahshi, and Hind, Abu Sufyan’s wife, mutilated the bodies of the slain.

The Sirah names around seventy martyrs, including Hamzah, Mus’ab ibn Umayr and Anas ibn al-Nadr. The Prophet ﷺ grieved deeply. He forgave the archers and did not punish them, and he prayed for the dead. The battle was a military defeat, but the community survived, and the Qur’an turned the defeat into a permanent lesson.

7. Uhud: The Qur’anic Response and the Lessons

Surah Al Imran addresses Uhud at length. It tells the believers not to weaken or grieve, because they will be superior if they are believers (3:139). It explains that the setback came through their own disobedience, while forgiveness was available (3:152–155). It distinguishes the sincere from the hypocrites who stayed behind and spoke about what they had not witnessed (3:165–168). And it declares that those killed in the way of Allah are alive with their Lord, provided for (3:169–171).

“So do not weaken and do not grieve, and you will be superior if you are [true] believers.”
Qur’an 3:139

The lessons are concrete. Obedience to the commander matters more than individual bravery. Discipline protects the community from the consequences of enthusiasm. Defeat is not proof that Allah has abandoned the believers; it is a test and a correction. The Prophet ﷺ himself was wounded, and the Qur’an does not hide his vulnerability. That honesty is part of the Seerah’s credibility.

Uhud also shaped the ethics of grief. The Prophet ﷺ wept for Hamzah and for the martyrs, and he allowed mourning. He did not demand that the community pretend that loss did not hurt. The Qur’an’s promise of Paradise for the martyrs did not cancel the pain of the survivors; it gave the pain a horizon.

8. The Trench: A Coalition Against Madinah

Two years after Uhud, Quraysh assembled a much larger coalition. The tribes of Ghatafan and others joined, and the exiled leaders of Banu al-Nadir, especially Huyayy ibn Akhtab, travelled among them urging a decisive campaign. The Sirah reports a Confederate force of about ten thousand against a Muslim force of about three thousand. The numbers are traditional and may be rounded, but the strategic imbalance is clear.

Salman al-Farsi, a Persian companion with experience of siege warfare, suggested digging a trench on the open northern approaches to Madinah, where cavalry could otherwise sweep into the town. The Prophet ﷺ accepted the advice and the Muslims dug a defensive ditch, reinforced by the natural lava fields and the fortified houses on the other sides. The work was hard, the weather cold, and food scarce.

The trench was a defensive innovation in Arabian warfare, and it neutralised the enemy’s greatest advantage: cavalry. It also bought time. The Prophet ﷺ remained in the city, organised the defences, and used diplomacy to divide the coalition. The siege that followed is known as the Battle of the Trench (Khandaq) or the Battle of the Confederates (Ahzab).

9. The Siege, Hunger and the Wind

The Confederates arrived, found the trench impassable, and settled into a siege that the sources put at roughly three to four weeks. The Muslims endured cold, hunger and constant alert. The Prophet ﷺ tied stones to his stomach to ease the pangs of hunger, and the companions shared what little they had. The Qur’an describes the fear and the testing of the believers:

“When they came upon you from above you and from below you, and when eyes grew wild and hearts reached to the throats, and you were assuming about Allah [various] assumptions.”
Qur’an 33:10

The siege was broken by a combination of factors. A severe storm — wind, rain and cold — overturned tents and extinguished fires, and the Confederates’ morale collapsed. The Qur’an presents the wind as an unseen army of Allah (33:9). Diplomacy also played a part: Nu’aym ibn Mas’ud, a man from Ghatafan who had become a secret Muslim, sowed distrust between the Confederates and the Jewish group of Banu Qurayzah, weakening the coalition from within. The story is from the Sirah, not an authentic hadith, and is reported with variation.

The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, after the enemy withdrew, that Quraysh would never again march against the Muslims in force, while the Muslims would march against them. The report is in Bukhari. From this point the strategic initiative passed to Madinah.

10. After the Trench: Banu Qurayzah and Consolidation

During the siege, Banu Qurayzah — a Jewish group in Madinah with a treaty relationship to the Muslims — were accused of negotiating with the Confederates and breaking their agreement. After the siege lifted, the Prophet ﷺ besieged their forts. They surrendered and asked that their fate be decided by Sa’d ibn Mu’adh, a chief of the Aws who had been wounded at the Trench. Sa’d judged that the fighting men should be killed, the women and children taken captive, and their property divided. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said that the judgment corresponded to the judgment of Allah.

This is one of the most difficult episodes in the Seerah, and honesty requires more than either triumphalism or caricature. The sources report that the judgment followed the tribal custom of the time for treachery in war, and that it was accepted by the group’s own allies. The numbers of those killed are given variously in the sources, sometimes in the hundreds; they cannot be verified precisely. The Qur’an refers to the event in 33:26–27. Modern scholars debate the legal and ethical reading of the episode, and the debate is legitimate.

What should not be done is to use the episode as a proof-text for hostility to Jews as such. The conflict was political and contractual, and the Prophet ﷺ had treaties with other Jewish groups and with the Christians of Najran. At the same time, the severity of the outcome should not be softened into something it was not. The article records what the sources say and marks the limits of certainty.

Source caution: the numbers and some details of the Banu Qurayzah episode come from later historians and are disputed. This article does not present them as precise or as a model for later conduct.

11. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for the three battles are:

  • The Qur’an — especially 2:190–193, 2:217, 3:121–175, 8:1–75, 22:39–40 and 33:9–27.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — selected reports on Badr, Uhud, Hamzah, the Trench and the Prophet’s ﷺ wounds.
  • Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah — the fullest early narrative of the campaigns, including speeches, letters and poetry.
  • Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk — parallel reports and chronology, with varying chains.
  • Al-Waqidi, Kitab al-Maghazi — very detailed, but widely criticised as weak; useful only where corroborated.
  • Ibn Kathir, al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah — a later synthesis with grading comments.

The historians preserve material that a strict hadith scholar would not accept: invented speeches, exact troop counts, names of minor participants and poetic exchanges. The Qur’an and the sound hadith provide the framework; the historians provide the story. This article follows that division and flags the difference where it matters.

Editorial note: dates are given in the conventional Hijri and CE chronology. Troop numbers and casualty figures are traditional reports, not modern statistics. The article does not claim that every detail in the Sirah is authentic.

12. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Why did the Muslims fight at Badr?

The immediate occasion was the interception of a Quraysh caravan, but the deeper context was the expulsion and persecution of the Muslims, the seizure of their property and the ongoing threat from Quraysh. The Qur’an permits fighting against aggression while forbidding transgression.

2. Was Badr a miracle?

The Qur’an presents the victory as divine help: the Muslims were few, exhausted and poorly equipped against a larger army. It also presents the help as a test of gratitude, not as a guarantee that the believers would always win regardless of their conduct.

3. Why were the Muslims defeated at Uhud?

The immediate cause was the archers leaving their post, which allowed Khalid ibn al-Walid’s cavalry to attack from behind. The Qur’an attributes the setback to disobedience and presents it as a test and a correction, not as proof that the enemy was right.

4. How was the siege of the Trench broken?

A combination of the trench, which neutralised cavalry; the endurance of the Muslims; a severe storm that destroyed the Confederates’ camp; and diplomacy that sowed distrust within the coalition. The Qur’an presents the wind as an unseen army of Allah.

5. What happened to Banu Qurayzah?

After the siege they were accused of breaking their treaty, besieged and forced to surrender. Their fate was arbitrated by Sa’d ibn Mu’adh, who judged that the fighting men should be killed and the women and children taken captive. The sources differ on numbers, and modern scholars debate the episode.
