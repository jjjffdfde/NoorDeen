The Umayyads: Damascus, Dynasty and the Limits of the Arab Kingdom

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The Umayyads ruled the largest empire the world had yet seen, from the Atlantic coast of Iberia to the Indus, and they did it for less than ninety years. They moved the capital from Madinah to Damascus, turned the caliphate into a hereditary monarchy and built the administrative foundations that the Abbasids inherited. They are also the dynasty that Islamic historiography loves to condemn.

Almost everything we know about the Umayyads was written under their successors. The Abbasid revolution of 750 presented itself as the restoration of the Prophet’s family and the defeat of impious kings, and the historians who served the new order described the old one in those terms. Some of the accusations are supported by earlier or independent evidence; others are literary topoi. The historian’s task is to separate the two.

This article follows the Umayyads from Mu‘awiya’s assumption of power in 661 to the fall of Marwan II in 750. It treats them neither as saints nor as villains, but as a ruling dynasty that solved some problems brilliantly, failed at others and was destroyed by the coalition it had excluded.

Key takeaways
  • Mu‘awiya ibn Abi Sufyan founded the dynasty in 661, moving the capital to Damascus and instituting hereditary succession.
  • The second fitna (680–692) followed the death of Mu‘awiya and included the killing of Husayn at Karbala and the counter-caliphate of Ibn al-Zubayr.
  • Abd al-Malik (685–705) rebuilt the state after the civil war, Arabised the administration and reformed the coinage.
  • The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem was completed under Abd al-Malik, one of the earliest surviving monuments of Islamic architecture.
  • Al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf governed Iraq with severity and is remembered both for repression and for administrative and Qur’anic reforms.
  • Umayyad armies conquered North Africa, crossed into Iberia in 711 and reached Sind in the same period; the conquests were often negotiated rather than purely military.
  • Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz (717–720) attempted tax and conversion reforms and is widely respected across the schools.
  • The dynasty fell in 750 to the Abbasid revolution, which drew on Khurasani, mawali and Shi‘i grievances.

1. Why the Umayyads Matter

The Umayyads matter because they turned a community into an empire with a professional state. They inherited the Rashidun’s conquests but not their consensus, and they had to govern millions of non-Muslims, a growing number of converts and a military aristocracy that expected rewards. Their solutions — a hereditary monarchy, a Syrian power base, a standing army and a sophisticated bureaucracy — shaped the Islamic state for centuries.

They also matter because the religious and political divisions of the modern Muslim world took shape under their rule. The killing of Husayn at Karbala in 680 became the founding martyrdom of Shi‘i devotion. The Kharijite revolts produced a tradition of radical piety and rebellion. The Sunni compromise of obedience to the ruler and patience under injustice was formulated partly in response to Umayyad power.

Finally, the Umayyads matter for the history of art, architecture and administration. The Dome of the Rock, the Great Mosque of Damascus, the desert palaces and the first Islamic coins are their creations. They borrowed from Byzantine and Sasanian models and transformed them into something new.

2. From Civil War to Dynasty

When Ali was killed in 661, Mu‘awiya was the strongest figure in the community. He had governed Syria for nearly twenty years, commanded a loyal army and controlled the revenues of a rich province. He received the bay‘ah in Kufa and moved the seat of government to Damascus. The capital shift was symbolic as well as practical: the centre of gravity of the empire had moved to the Mediterranean and the Fertile Crescent.

Mu‘awiya’s most consequential decision was to nominate his son Yazid as his successor. The nomination turned the caliphate into a dynasty and bypassed the tradition of consultation. It was accepted by many, resisted by others and condemned by later jurists who preferred the Rashidun model. The change was not simply personal ambition; it reflected a genuine fear that without a designated successor the community would fall into another civil war.

The fear was justified. When Mu‘awiya died in 680, Yazid’s succession was contested by Husayn ibn Ali, the Prophet’s grandson, and by Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr. Husayn was killed at Karbala in Iraq with a small group of family members and supporters. The event became the central tragedy of Shi‘i Islam and a moral indictment of dynastic power that Sunnis also remember with grief.

Source caution. The Karbala narratives were shaped by later devotion and polemic. The killing itself is established; the detailed speeches and the exact sequence of the days are preserved in sources written much later.

3. Mu‘awiya: The Pragmatic Founder

Mu‘awiya is one of the most misjudged figures in Islamic history. Later sources portray him as a cunning king who corrupted the caliphate, and there is truth in the charge that he replaced consultation with dynasty. But he was also a capable administrator who ended a civil war, restored unity and managed the empire’s rival factions with a combination of generosity and ruthlessness.

He made peace with Hasan ibn Ali, Ali’s son, who abdicated his claim in return for a pension and a quiet life. The agreement, often called the year of unity, was praised by many contemporaries as a mercy for the community. Mu‘awiya also developed the Syrian army, the navy and the frontier defences against Byzantium. He raided Byzantine territory and even besieged Constantinople, though the campaign failed.

Source caution. The portrait of Mu‘awiya as a “king” rather than a caliph comes largely from Abbasid-era historians. Some earlier reports are more favourable, and modern scholars have argued for a more balanced assessment.

4. Damascus and the Shape of the State

Damascus was a Byzantine city with a Greek-speaking bureaucracy, a Christian population and a sophisticated tax system. The Umayyads kept much of that machinery and adapted it. The diwan continued to be staffed by Greek and Coptic officials for decades, and the coinage initially imitated Byzantine models. Abd al-Malik’s reforms in the 690s replaced the imagery with Arabic inscriptions and a new gold dinar.

The Arabisation of the administration was gradual. It was driven by the need to integrate the empire and by the growing confidence of a generation that no longer saw itself as a provincial offshoot of Byzantium. The change did not happen overnight, and local languages continued in the provinces. But the long-term effect was a state whose official culture was Arabic and Islamic.

The Dome of the Rock, completed in the 690s, is the most visible monument of that confidence. Its inscriptions are the earliest dated Qur’anic quotations in architecture, and its design deliberately rivals the Christian shrines of Jerusalem. The Great Mosque of Damascus, built on the site of a church, made a similar claim: the new faith had arrived and would build in the language of empire.

Source caution. The dating and patronage of the Dome of the Rock are established by its inscriptions. The interpretation of its political meaning is debated among art historians.

5. The “Arab Kingdom” Thesis and Its Critics

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars described the Umayyad state as an “Arab kingdom”: a regime in which a Arab military aristocracy ruled over non-Arab converts and non-Muslim subjects, and in which conversion was discouraged because it reduced tax revenue. The thesis was developed by Julius Wellhausen and others and became a standard account.

Later scholarship complicated it. The administration was not uniformly hostile to converts, and the fiscal system was more flexible than the thesis suggested. Some governors encouraged conversion; others penalised it. The category of mawali — non-Arab clients — was not a single underclass but a varied group that included scholars, soldiers and administrators.

What remains true is that the Umayyad state was Arab in its ruling elite, its language of power and its military structure. Arabs received higher stipends, held the highest offices and dominated the court. Converts had to attach themselves to an Arab tribe as clients, and their status was inferior. That hierarchy generated resentment, and the resentment became a political resource for the Abbasids.

Source caution. The “Arab kingdom” model is a modern historiographical construct, not a contemporary description. It captures real inequalities but oversimplifies a diverse and changing administration.

6. The Second Fitna and the Counter-Caliphate

The second fitna began with Yazid’s accession in 680 and lasted until 692. It had three centres: Karbala, where Husayn was killed; Madinah, where the people revolted and were defeated at al-Harrah; and Makkah, where Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr declared himself caliph and was recognised by much of the Hijaz, Iraq, Egypt and Yemen.

Ibn al-Zubayr was a grandson of Abu Bakr and a son of al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam. He controlled the holy cities and presented himself as the defender of the Prophet’s family and the opponent of Umayyad impiety. The Umayyads besieged Makkah and damaged the Ka‘bah, an act that deepened their unpopularity. For a decade the empire had two competing caliphates.

The civil war ended with the Marwanid branch of the Umayyads, based in Syria, defeating Ibn al-Zubayr and killing him in 692. Abd al-Malik then rebuilt the state, reformed the coinage and restored unity. The cost was high: the sanctuary had been violated, the Prophet’s grandson had been killed and the community had been divided for more than a decade.

Source caution. The second fitna is reported in al-Tabari and al-Baladhuri. The chronology is difficult because different sources date the key events differently, and the article follows the most common sequence.

7. Al-Hajjaj and the Governance of Iraq

Al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf was appointed governor of Iraq in 694 and governed it for twenty years with a severity that became proverbial. He crushed the Kharijite revolts, suppressed Ibn al-Zubayr’s supporters and imposed order on a province that had been in turmoil for a generation. His methods included mass executions, collective punishment and the intimidation of the religious scholars.

He was also an administrator of consequence. He supervised the vowel markings of the Qur’anic codex, which made the text easier to read for non-Arabs. He founded the city of Wasit as a garrison and administrative centre. He reorganised the tax system and promoted public works. The sources that condemn his cruelty also record his effectiveness.

Al-Hajjaj illustrates a central problem of the Umayyad state: it depended on strongmen who could hold provinces together, and those strongmen often ruled by fear. When a strongman died or fell, the province reverted to factionalism. The dynasty never solved the problem of governing Iraq, and Iraq became the base of the revolution that destroyed it.

Source caution. The reports of al-Hajjaj’s cruelty are numerous and come from sources hostile to the Umayyads. Some are corroborated by independent evidence; others may be exaggerated. The article does not repeat the most lurid anecdotes as fact.

8. The Great Conquests: Africa, Iberia and Sind

The Umayyad period saw the furthest extension of the Arab conquests. In North Africa, Uqba ibn Nafi founded Qayrawan in 670 and raided as far as the Atlantic; the region was not fully subdued until the early eighth century. In 711 Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the strait that now bears his name and defeated the Visigothic kingdom in a campaign that within a few years brought most of Iberia under Muslim rule.

In the east, Muhammad ibn Qasim conquered Sind in 711–712, establishing a Muslim presence in the Indian subcontinent. In Transoxiana, Qutayba ibn Muslim extended Umayyad authority beyond the Oxus. The conquests were not a single coordinated wave; they were a series of campaigns led by governors with local resources and local agendas.

The terms varied. Some cities were taken by treaty, others by force. In Iberia, the sources describe a mixture of Visigothic collapse, local alliances and military victory. In Sind, Muhammad ibn Qasim is remembered for granting religious freedom to Hindus and Buddhists and for integrating local elites into the administration. The details are preserved in later sources and should be read with caution.

Source caution. The conquest of Iberia is reported in Ibn Abd al-Hakam and later Arabic sources, and in Latin chronicles written centuries later. The numbers and the details of the battles are unreliable; the broad outline is not.

9. Umar II and the Reform Impulse

Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz, known as Umar II, ruled from 717 to 720 and is the one Umayyad caliph whom almost every later tradition praises. He was a scholar and a pious man, and he attempted to reverse some of the policies that had made the dynasty unpopular. He withdrew the tax penalties on converts, restored confiscated property and reduced the luxury of the court.

His fiscal reforms were the most important. He ruled that converts should not be taxed as if they were still non-Muslims, and he tried to put the jizya on a consistent footing. The reforms reduced revenue in the short term and were resisted by governors. They also revealed a tension that the dynasty never resolved: the empire’s finances depended on a tax distinction between Muslims and non-Muslims, and mass conversion eroded that distinction.

Umar II died after less than three years in office. His brief reign became a symbol of what the Umayyads might have been, and later historians used him as a reproach to his successors. Whether his policies could have saved the dynasty is impossible to know, but they show that the Umayyad state was not monolithically oppressive.

Source caution. The reports of Umar II’s reforms are preserved in al-Tabari and the biographical works. The fiscal details are debated, and the article describes the direction of reform rather than a precise budget.

10. The Third Fitna and the Abbasid Revolution

The dynasty’s final crisis began in the 740s. The Berber revolt in North Africa, the Kharijite insurrections in Iraq and the rivalry within the Umayyad family weakened the state. In Khurasan, a movement led by Abu Muslim raised the black banners of the Abbasids and called for a ruler from the Prophet’s family. The slogan was deliberately ambiguous: it could mean the Alids or the Abbasids, and it attracted both.

The Abbasid army marched west, took Kufa in 749 and defeated Marwan II at the Battle of the Zab in 750. Marwan fled to Egypt and was killed. The new dynasty hunted down the surviving Umayyads, and one prince, Abd al-Rahman, escaped to Iberia, where he founded the Umayyad emirate of Córdoba. The revolution was not a simple change of dynasty; it was a coalition of the excluded — mawali, Khurasanis, Shi‘i sympathisers and provincial elites — that then had to be held together.

The Abbasids presented their victory as the restoration of justice and the end of kingly rule. In practice they built a more centralised and more elaborate monarchy than the Umayyads had ever done. The rhetoric of the revolution and the reality of the new state were different things, and the gap between them is one of the themes of Abbasid history.

Source caution. The revolution is reported in al-Tabari and in the Abbasid court histories. The role of Abu Muslim and the promises made to the Alids are debated, and the sources are shaped by the need to legitimise the new dynasty.

11. The Umayyads in Memory

The Umayyads are remembered as the dynasty that turned the caliphate into a monarchy. That charge is substantially true, but it is not the whole story. They also created the administrative and architectural foundations of Islamic civilisation, defended the frontiers, expanded the empire and produced the first great monuments of Islamic art. Their reputation suffered because their successors wrote the history.

Modern scholarship has rehabilitated parts of the Umayyad record without denying its violence. The dynasty’s treatment of the Prophet’s family, the killing of Husayn, the siege of Makkah and the repression of Iraq are not inventions of Abbasid propaganda. They are part of the record and must be named. The question is whether they define the dynasty entirely, and the answer is no.

12. Art, Architecture and the Desert Palaces

Umayyad architecture is the first distinctively Islamic architecture. The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, the Great Mosque of Damascus and the desert palaces of Syria and Jordan combine Byzantine, Sasanian and local traditions into a new visual language. The palaces, such as Qusayr Amra and Mshatta, preserve frescoes, mosaics and carved facades that show a court culture of considerable sophistication.

Source caution. The interpretation of Umayyad palace imagery is contested. The article describes the monuments and the range of scholarly readings without endorsing one.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

The Umayyad experience teaches three lessons. The first is that legitimacy is not only a matter of power: a ruler who wins the battle but loses the moral argument will be remembered badly, and the memory has political consequences. The second is that empire requires institutions, and the Umayyads built them even when their politics were unstable. The third is that exclusion is expensive: the dynasty’s treatment of converts, non-Arabs and the Prophet’s family created the coalition that destroyed it.

The limits of the sources are serious. The Abbasid historians had every reason to blacken the Umayyads, and the surviving Umayyad-friendly sources are few. Archaeology, inscriptions, coins and architecture provide an independent check, and they often show a more complex picture than the literary tradition. A responsible account uses both.

The best way to remember the Umayyads is neither to idealise them as the last Arab caliphs nor to reduce them to a dynasty of tyrants. They were a ruling family that inherited a revolution, built a state and lost it. Their monuments still stand, their coins still circulate in museums and their conflicts still shape the way Muslims argue about power.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the early chronicles, the Abbasid-era literary tradition, the material evidence and modern historiography. It does not present the Abbasid portrait of the Umayyads as neutral fact.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Who founded the Umayyad dynasty?

Mu‘awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, who became caliph in 661 after the death of Ali and moved the capital to Damascus. He had governed Syria for nearly twenty years and was the strongest figure in the community.

2. Why is Karbala important?

Husayn ibn Ali, the Prophet’s grandson, was killed at Karbala in 680 after refusing to give allegiance to Yazid. The event became the central martyrdom of Shi‘i Islam and a moral indictment of dynastic power that Sunnis also remember with grief.

3. What did Abd al-Malik accomplish?

He ended the second fitna, rebuilt the state, Arabised the administration, reformed the coinage and completed the Dome of the Rock. His reign is the turning point from a conquest state to a settled empire.

4. Was Umar II an Umayyad?

Yes. Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz was a member of the Umayyad family and ruled from 717 to 720. He is widely praised for his piety and for attempting tax and conversion reforms, and he is the one Umayyad caliph whom almost every later tradition honours.

5. How did the Umayyad dynasty end?

It fell in 750 to the Abbasid revolution, which drew on Khurasani, mawali and Shi‘i grievances. Marwan II was defeated at the Battle of the Zab, and one Umayyad prince, Abd al-Rahman, escaped to Iberia and founded the emirate of Córdoba.

15. Sources & Further Reading

This article cites the Qur'an by surah and verse, and identifies hadith by collection and, where verified, book or number. Where scholars differ, the difference is stated rather than resolved by assertion.

  • The Qur’an — especially 59:7 on the distribution of wealth and 4:59 on authority. Translations adapted from Saheeh International unless stated.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — the reports on the Prophet’s family and the warnings against sedition.
  • Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk — the second and third fitnas, the conquests and the revolution of 750.
  • Al-Baladhuri, Futuh al-Buldan — the provincial conquests, treaties and tax arrangements.
  • Ibn Abd al-Hakam, Futuh Misr wa Akhbaruha — North Africa and the conquest of Iberia.
  • Ibn al-Athir, al-Kamil fi al-Tarikh — a later synthesis with additional reports.
  • Ibn Kathir, al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah, and al-Dhahabi, Siyar A‘lam al-Nubala — biographical and grading material.
  • Hugh Kennedy, The Prophet and the Age of the Caliphates; Chase Robinson, Empire and Elites after the Muslim Conquest; Gerald Hawting, The First Dynasty of Islam — modern critical accounts.
  • Oleg Grabar, The Shape of the Holy — the Dome of the Rock and early Islamic Jerusalem.
