Islamic Inheritance (Mawarith): Rights, Shares and Practical Duties

NoorDeen Editorial · 12 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Islamic inheritance law (mawarith or fara'id) is unusual in the Qur'an: the shares are revealed in explicit numerical detail. That detail is a mercy and a responsibility. It protects the vulnerable from being written out by custom, and it prevents the estate from becoming a prize for the loudest relative.

This article explains the framework and the duties that surround it. It does not calculate a specific estate. Shares change with the exact combination of surviving heirs, debts, valid bequests and local law, and a wrong calculation can permanently harm a family. Use a qualified mufti or inheritance specialist and, where the state requires it, a lawyer.

Key takeaways
  • Inheritance shares are part of Quranic law, not merely local custom.
  • Funeral costs and genuine debts are settled before the will and the shares.
  • A valid will is limited to one-third of the estate unless the heirs consent.
  • Male and female heirs both receive shares; the common claim that women inherit nothing is false.
  • Orphans, minors and absent heirs have specific protections.
  • A qualified calculation is essential; do not rely on guesswork or family lore.

1. Why Inheritance Is Quranic Law

The Qur'an addresses inheritance directly:

“For men is a share of what the parents and close relatives leave, and for women is a share of what they leave, whether it is small or large — an obligatory share.”
Qur'an 4:7

This verse corrected a pre-Islamic custom in which only adult male warriors could inherit. Women, children and the vulnerable could be excluded. The Qur'an made a share obligatory for both men and women, and then specified the shares in 4:11–12 and 4:176.

The legal detail is striking. The Qur'an does not leave inheritance to the discretion of the dying person or the strongest relative. It sets limits on the will and fixed entitlements for close relatives. A person may dispose of part of the estate, but not at the expense of the heirs Allah has named.

Inheritance is therefore an act of worship and justice, not a technicality to be arranged after the funeral. Delaying distribution, hiding assets or pressuring heirs to sign away their shares are serious wrongs.

2. What Must Be Paid First

The estate is not simply divided among heirs. The Qur'an says shares are distributed “after any bequest he may have made or debt” (4:12). Classical jurists set the order:

  • Funeral and burial costs: reasonable and necessary expenses, without extravagance.
  • Genuine debts: including unpaid zakat, loans, rent, medical bills and lawful maintenance obligations.
  • Valid will (wasiyyah): up to one-third of what remains, to a non-heir or to charity, unless the heirs consent to more.
  • Fixed shares: distributed according to the Quranic scheme and the surviving heirs.
  • Residue: to the nearest male relatives (asabah) according to the school's rules, or dealt with as the law and scholar direct.

The Prophet ﷺ explained the limit of the will in the case of Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, who was ill and had only one daughter. He asked whether he should give away two-thirds or half of his wealth. The Prophet ﷺ said one-third, and added that one-third is a lot; leaving heirs rich is better than leaving them poor and begging (Sahih al-Bukhari 2742; Sahih Muslim 1628).

A debt is not cancelled by death, and an heir who hides an asset is taking what is not theirs. Families should keep written records of loans and obligations while the person is alive.

3. The Fixed Shares in Outline

The Quranic shares are not a single formula; they are a set of rules that interact. The most familiar features are:

  • Spouse: a husband receives one-half of his wife's estate if she has no child, or one-quarter if she does. A wife receives one-quarter of her husband's estate if he has no child, or one-eighth if he does.
  • Parents: each parent generally receives one-sixth where there is a child; the mother may receive one-third in some combinations without a child.
  • Children: a son receives twice the share of a daughter in the same class. Where there are no sons, daughters receive specified fractions, commonly one-half for one daughter and two-thirds shared by two or more, subject to other heirs.
  • Siblings and extended kin: their shares depend on whether there is a child, a parent, a spouse, and on the school's treatment of residuaries and blocked heirs.

The two-to-one ratio between son and daughter is often quoted without context. The classical rationale is that the male generally bears financial maintenance obligations while the female's property remains hers, and that a woman is not required to spend her inheritance on the household. Scholars also note that the ratio is not universal across all heirs; it applies to the specific sibling combination. The rule is not a statement that a woman's life or testimony is half a man's.

A daughter is never excluded merely because she is a daughter. A wife is never excluded merely because she is a wife. Where a family custom says otherwise, the custom is wrong.

Do not self-calculate: the presence of a grandchild, a predeceased son's children, a full or half sibling, a grandmother, or a surviving spouse can change the result. Use a qualified specialist and provide the full family tree, debts and will.

4. The Will and Its Limits

The Qur'an instructs those leaving wealth to make a fair bequest for parents and close relatives (2:180–182), and the Prophet ﷺ capped the bequest at one-third. A will is therefore useful for:

  • charity or a cause the person cares about;
  • a non-heir who is in need, such as a stepchild or a friend;
  • clarifying debts, funeral wishes and documents;
  • appointing an executor and a guardian for minor children.

A will cannot be used to disinherit a Quranic heir or to give a favoured child more than the law allows without the consent of the other heirs. The Qur'an warns against altering a bequest unjustly (2:181–182). Families sometimes pressure a dying person to sign a late will; that is coercion, not piety.

In countries where the state applies a different inheritance regime, a Muslim should take legal advice early. A will that is valid in civil law may still be unjust in religious terms, and a religiously correct plan may need a civil instrument to be enforceable. The two systems should be reconciled before death, not litigated after it.

5. Common Injustices

The same mistakes recur across communities:

  • “The eldest son takes everything.” This is custom, not Islamic law. It strips daughters, wives and younger siblings of their shares.
  • “Daughters do not inherit because they marry out.” The Qur'an explicitly gives women a share (4:7). Marriage does not erase it.
  • “The family home goes to the son who cared for the parents.” Care may deserve compensation or a gift during life, but it does not automatically cancel the shares of other heirs.
  • “We will sort it out later.” Delay allows assets to disappear, memories to fade and resentment to harden.
  • Oral promises and deathbed gifts. A gift made to avoid inheritance may be treated as a bequest or as invalid depending on the circumstances.
  • Joint accounts and hidden assets. Adding a child's name to an account for convenience can create a dispute about ownership after death.

The Qur'an commands that orphans be given their property and that it not be exchanged for inferior property (4:2). It warns those who consume an orphan's wealth unjustly of fire in their bellies (4:10). Those verses are not only about strangers; they apply to relatives who manage a minor heir's share.

A family meeting before death, with documents and a qualified adviser, can prevent most of these disputes. Avoiding the conversation is not trust in Allah; it is often a way of postponing injustice.

6. A Practical Process

A defensible process looks like this:

  • List every asset and every genuine debt, including informal loans and unpaid obligations.
  • Obtain the death certificate and the local legal authority's inheritance document where required.
  • Identify all heirs, including minors, absent relatives and any unborn child.
  • Have a qualified scholar or inheritance calculator produce the shares from the full facts.
  • Pay funeral costs, debts and the valid one-third bequest before distributing.
  • Document the distribution with signatures and bank records, and set up a trust or guardianship for minors where the law allows.
  • Keep the calculation and receipts; they protect the executor and the heirs.

An executor should not take a “fee” from the estate without a lawful basis or the heirs' informed consent. A guardian of a minor heir should keep the minor's property separate and account for it. These are trust obligations, not favours.

Where the estate is small or the family is cooperative, the process can still be simple. Simplicity is not an excuse to skip the shares.

7. Schools of Law and Local Law

The major Sunni schools agree on the Quranic fixed shares but differ on residuaries, blocked relatives, the treatment of certain grandparents and siblings, and the calculation of al-'awl and al-radd. A calculation that is correct in one school may differ in another. This is one reason a qualified specialist is needed.

Local law may impose compulsory shares, community property rules, or a civil probate process. In some countries, a Muslim can make a will that honours both the religious scheme and the legal minimum. In others, the civil law may override the religious plan for certain assets. A lawyer and a scholar should be consulted together where possible.

None of this authorises injustice. A civil loophole that lets one heir take everything is not a religious justification. The goal is a distribution that is both legally effective and morally defensible before Allah.

8. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Does a daughter inherit?

Yes. The Qur'an explicitly gives women a share (4:7) and specifies daughters' shares in 4:11. A daughter is not excluded merely because she is a daughter or because she is married. The exact share depends on the other surviving heirs.

2. Can I leave everything to one child in my will?

No. A valid will is limited to one-third of the estate, and the Quranic heirs have fixed shares. Giving more to one child requires the informed consent of the other heirs after the death, and even then coercion or concealment is unjust. Seek a scholar and a lawyer.

3. Why does a son receive twice a daughter's share?

That is the rule for the specific sibling combination in 4:11. Classical scholars explain it in light of the male's maintenance obligations and the fact that a woman's property remains hers. The ratio is not a universal statement about the value of women, and other heirs receive different shares.

4. Should debts be paid before inheritance?

Yes. Funeral costs and genuine debts are paid before the will and the fixed shares (4:12). Hiding a debt or an asset is a breach of trust and can wrong the creditors and the heirs.

5. Can I use an online inheritance calculator?

A calculator can help you understand the structure, but it is only as good as the facts you enter. Complex families, missing heirs, debts and local law require a qualified specialist. Use a calculator for learning, not as the final authority for a real estate.
