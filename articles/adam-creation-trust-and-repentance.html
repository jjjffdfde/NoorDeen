Adam: Creation, Trust and Repentance

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Adam is the first human being in the Qur’an and the first prophet in the Islamic account of history. His story is told in several surahs, and it is not a single continuous narrative: it is a set of scenes that the Qur’an returns to whenever it wants to explain human nature, the reality of temptation, the meaning of repentance and the dignity of the human being.

That is why the story matters beyond the question of origins. The Qur’an uses Adam to answer questions that every reader asks: why is there evil, why do we fail, what does it mean to be forgiven, and what kind of creature has God placed on the earth? This article follows the Qur’anic text closely, marks where the later storytellers added detail, and refuses to present popular embellishment as revelation.

Key takeaways
  • Adam is presented as a vicegerent (khalifah) on earth, created from clay and honoured by the command to the angels to prostrate.
  • Iblis refused out of pride, claiming superiority of substance, and was expelled; his request for respite was granted as a test.
  • The prohibition concerned a single tree, and the transgression was one act, not a permanent corruption of human nature.
  • Adam and Hawwa’ (Eve) both repented with the words of 7:23; the Qur’an does not blame the woman alone.
  • The Qur’an rejects inherited guilt: no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another (6:164; 53:38).
  • The descent to earth is not simply a punishment; it is the beginning of vicegerency, guidance and prophethood.
  • The details of the tree, the serpent, the location of the garden and Adam’s age come from later literature and are not in the Qur’an.

1. Why Adam Matters

The Qur’an mentions Adam by name in several places and alludes to his story in others. The longest continuous passages are 2:30–39 and 7:11–27, with further material in 15:26–44, 17:61–65, 18:50, 20:115–123 and 38:71–85. These are not duplicates. Each passage selects the details that serve its argument: the first teaches the dignity of knowledge and the reality of repentance, the second warns the children of Adam against the whispering of Satan, and the third answers those who doubted the resurrection by pointing to the creation of the first human being.

Reading the passages together produces a coherent account, but it also shows how the Qur’an teaches. It does not hand the reader a biography with dates and places. It gives scenes, dialogue and moral commentary. The reader is expected to think with the story rather than to extract a chronicle from it. That is the first interpretive principle this article follows.

The second principle is source discipline. The Qur’an is the primary text. The authentic hadith add a small number of details, and the classical commentaries preserve a much larger body of material drawn from Jewish and Christian traditions and from the imagination of later storytellers. Some of it is edifying; much of it is unverifiable, and this article keeps the layers separate.

2. The Qur’anic Account and Where It Is Found

The core sequence is this. Allah announces to the angels that He is placing a vicegerent on the earth. The angels ask whether He will place there one who causes corruption and sheds blood, while they glorify and sanctify Him. Allah replies that He knows what they do not know. He teaches Adam the names of all things and presents them to the angels, who cannot name them. Adam names them, and the angels are commanded to prostrate. All prostrate except Iblis, who refuses, is arrogant and is expelled. Adam and his wife are placed in a garden with one prohibition. Satan approaches them, they slip, their shame becomes apparent, and they repent. They are sent down to the earth with guidance, and the story becomes the story of every human being.

The passages differ in emphasis. Surah al-Baqarah frames the story as a lesson about knowledge, obedience and the mercy of repentance. Surah al-A‘raf frames it as a warning: “O children of Adam, let not Satan tempt you as he removed your parents from Paradise” (7:27). Surah Ta-Ha adds the note that Adam forgot and that “his Lord chose him and turned to him in forgiveness and guided him” (20:121–122). Surah al-Hijr and Surah Sad focus on the creation and on the refusal of Iblis.

None of these passages names the tree, the place of the garden, the time that passed or the manner of the descent. Those gaps are real, and they are not defects. The Qur’an is interested in the meaning of the event, and it says what it needs to say to make that meaning clear.

3. Creation from Clay and the Divine Announcement

The Qur’an describes the creation of Adam from clay, dust and moulded mud. “He created man from clay like [that of] pottery” (55:14); “We created man from an extract of clay” (23:12); “Indeed, We created them from sticky clay” (37:11). The variations are not contradictions. They describe the same origin with different images: dust, earth, clay, pottery, a substance shaped and then given life.

The creation is presented as a deliberate act, not an accident. Allah says to the angels, “I am going to create a human being from clay. So when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, then fall down to him in prostration” (38:71–72). The phrase “of My spirit” has been discussed by theologians for centuries. It does not mean that the human being shares divinity. Classical exegetes explained it as an attribution of honour, like the “House of Allah” or the “she-camel of Allah” — a creature singled out for a dignity that belongs to its Maker.

The angels are told that a vicegerent is coming. The word khalifah in 2:30 has been understood in two main ways: a successor to previous creatures on the earth, or a representative of divine authority. Both readings are found in the commentaries, and both capture something true. The human being is a steward, not an owner; the earth is entrusted, not possessed.

4. The Angels’ Question and the Knowledge of Names

The angels’ question is not a rebellion. They ask how a creature who causes corruption and sheds blood can be placed on the earth when they already worship. The answer is not a defence of human behaviour; it is a statement of a knowledge the angels do not possess. Then Allah teaches Adam the names and asks the angels to inform Him of them. They confess that they know only what He has taught them. Adam names them, and the point is made: this creature has a capacity that the angels lack.

What does “the names” mean? The commentaries offer several readings. Some take it literally: Adam was taught the names of all created things, and therefore the vocabulary of language. Others understand it as the knowledge of the essences and properties of things, or the capacity for conceptual thought. The Qur’an does not define it, and the ambiguity is productive. What is clear is that knowledge is the ground of human dignity in this passage, not power, wealth or lineage.

That emphasis has consequences. A community that honours Adam must honour learning. The first human being is not presented as a warrior or a king but as a student who is taught and who can name what he sees.

5. Iblis: Refusal, Pride and the Nature of Evil

The command to prostrate was a test of obedience, and Iblis failed it. The Qur’an is precise about why: “He refused and was arrogant and became of the disbelievers” (2:34). When asked what prevented him, he answered, “I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay” (7:12). The sin begins with a comparison and ends in disobedience.

Iblis is not presented as an equal opponent of God. He is a created being who was given respite and who uses it to mislead. “He said, ‘Reprieve me until the Day they are resurrected.’ Allah said, ‘Indeed, you are of those reprieved’” (15:36–37). His programme is stated plainly: he will approach human beings from every side, except those who are sincere (15:39–40). The Qur’an therefore treats evil as a real force, but never as an independent power.

The passage also identifies the anatomy of the first sin: pride, a sense of superiority based on substance, and the refusal to accept a command that elevated someone else. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said that no one who has an atom’s weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise, and that arrogance is rejecting the truth and despising people (Sahih Muslim).

6. The Garden, the Tree and the Single Prohibition

Adam and his wife are placed in a garden and given permission to eat freely, with one exception: “Do not approach this tree, lest you be among the wrongdoers” (2:35). The wording is significant. The prohibition is not “do not eat” but “do not approach”, a wider boundary that protects the person before the act. Islamic law would later use the same logic in the doctrine of sadd al-dhara’i‘, blocking the means to harm.

The tree is never identified. Wheat, grape, fig and other suggestions appear in the commentaries, but they are guesses, often borrowed from earlier traditions. The Qur’an withholds the detail, and there is no obligation to supply it. What matters is that the prohibition was clear, that it was one command among many permissions, and that the transgression was a single act rather than a rejection of God.

Satan’s approach is described as whispering (waswasa), and his argument is that the tree is forbidden only because it would make them angels or immortal (7:20). He also swears that he is a sincere adviser (7:21). The Qur’an comments that he deceived them by delusion. The mechanics of temptation are recognisable: a plausible reason, a flattering assurance, a minimisation of the command, and a gradual approach rather than a sudden leap.

7. The Slip, Blame and the Vocabulary of Repentance

The Qur’an uses the language of slipping (zalla), not of a fall from grace in the Christian theological sense. “And Adam and his wife ate from the tree, and their shame became apparent to them, and they began to cover themselves with the leaves of the garden” (7:22). The shame is real, but the story does not end there. “Then Adam received from his Lord [some] words, and He accepted his repentance” (2:37).

The words are given in Surah al-A‘raf: “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers” (7:23). This is the model of repentance in Islam: confession without excuse, an appeal to mercy without despair, and a resolve that belongs to the future rather than a bargaining over the past.

Both Adam and his wife say “we have wronged ourselves”. The Qur’an does not single out Hawwa’ (Eve) as the temptress, and it does not endorse the later story that she was created from a crooked rib in a way that made her inherently deceptive. The hadith that woman was created from a rib is authentic, but the Prophet ﷺ used it to command gentle treatment, not to establish female moral inferiority. The Qur’anic account places the responsibility on both partners and the mercy on both.

Source caution: the identification of the tree, the presence of a serpent, the location of the garden and the idea that Adam’s sin was sexual are not in the Qur’an. They enter through later narrative and commentary, and this article does not present them as revelation.

8. Descent to Earth and the Meaning of Vicegerency

The descent is stated in the plural: “We said, ‘Go down from it, all of you’” (2:38). It is not a solitary expulsion of a guilty man. It is the beginning of human history, with a promise attached: “And when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance — there will be no fear concerning them, nor will they grieve” (2:38).

The Qur’an does not treat earthly life as a punishment to be escaped. It calls the earth a place of settlement and provision (7:10; 2:29), and it calls human beings successors on the earth (6:165; 27:62; 35:39). The descent therefore turns the story into the frame of prophethood: guidance will come, and the test is whether people follow it.

9. Original Sin, Inherited Guilt and the Qur’anic Correction

The Qur’an does not teach that Adam’s descendants are born guilty of his act. The principle is stated repeatedly: “No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” (6:164; 35:18; 53:38). Every person is responsible for his or her own choices, and every person has access to repentance. The story of Adam is the story of a first mistake and a first forgiveness, not the origin of a permanent stain.

This does not mean that the Qur’an is optimistic about human behaviour. It describes the soul as inclined to evil unless shown mercy (12:53), and it commands constant seeking of forgiveness. The Prophet ﷺ said, “Every son of Adam sins, and the best of those who sin are those who repent” (al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah; graded hasan by several scholars). The difference from the doctrine of original sin is not the seriousness of sin but the universality of pardon.

The practical consequence is important. A person is not trapped by an inherited condition, a caste, a family curse or a national guilt. The Qur’an insists that the door of repentance remains open until death, and it warns only against delaying until the moment of dying (4:18). The Adam story therefore ends with hope: the first human beings failed, repented and were forgiven, and their descendants are invited to do the same.

10. The Covenant of the Descendants of Adam

One of the most striking Adam-related passages is 7:172: “And when your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants and made them testify of themselves, [saying], ‘Am I not your Lord?’ They said, ‘Yes, we have testified.’” The verse is offered as a refutation of the excuse that people did not know: “lest you should say on the Day of Resurrection, ‘Indeed, we were of this unaware.’”

Interpreters have differed sharply about what this event was. Some read it as a literal pre-creation gathering in which every human soul testified. Others read it as a statement about the innate recognition of God, the fitrah, which the Qur’an describes elsewhere: “So direct your face toward the religion, inclining to truth, [the] disposition of Allah upon which He has created [all] people” (30:30). Both readings are classical, and the verse is not a proof-text for a single metaphysical system.

The ethical force of the verse is clear either way. The Qur’an claims that there is a witness within the person, however muffled by habit, and that the prophetic message awakens it rather than inventing it.

11. Adam in Hadith and Later Literature

The authentic hadith add a small number of details. The Prophet ﷺ said that Allah created Adam on a Friday (Sahih Muslim), that Adam was sixty cubits tall (Sahih al-Bukhari), and that Adam and Musa debated: Musa said, “You are Adam who drove us out of Paradise,” and Adam replied, “You are Musa whom Allah chose for His speech; do you blame me for something Allah decreed before He created me?” The Prophet ﷺ said that Adam overcame Musa in the argument (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). The report is theological, not biological: it is about divine decree and human responsibility, and scholars have written extensively on how to hold both.

A second hadith, “Allah created Adam in His image” (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim), has been the subject of intense theological discussion. The literalists affirmed the attribute without asking how; the theologians interpreted “image” as qualities such as life, knowledge, will and power, without likeness. This article does not adjudicate the dispute, but it notes that the report is authentic and that its interpretation is contested.

The later literature is much larger. It supplies the name of Adam’s wife as Hawwa’, the name of the tree, the serpent that spoke, the number of years Adam lived and the place of his burial. Some of this is harmless storytelling; some contradicts the Qur’an; all of it should be labelled as tradition rather than revelation.

12. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for this article are:

  • The Qur’an — especially 2:30–39, 7:11–27, 15:26–44, 17:61–65, 18:50, 20:115–123, 38:71–85, 55:14 and 7:172.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — the creation on Friday, the sixty cubits, the Adam–Musa debate and the “image” report.
  • Al-Tabari, Jami‘ al-Bayan — the classical commentary, including the isnad criticism of narrative material.
  • Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur’an al-‘Azim and al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah — a later synthesis with explicit grading of reports.
  • Al-Qurtubi, al-Jami‘ li Ahkam al-Qur’an — legal and theological discussion of the passage.
  • Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah — the later narrative framework, used with caution.

The Qur’anic text is fixed and complete; the narrative detail is not. Where the commentaries disagree, this article reports the disagreement. Where a report has no chain or a weak chain, it is not used as evidence. The reader should be suspicious of any account of Adam that is more precise than the Qur’an and more confident than the hadith.

Editorial note: this is an educational synthesis, not a critical edition of the commentaries. Reports from al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir and the storytellers are identified as such. The article does not claim that every detail in the classical literature is authentic.

13. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Was Adam the first prophet?

Yes. The Qur’an presents Adam as the first human being and the first recipient of divine guidance, and the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said that Adam was a prophet. The hadith that Nuh was the first messenger sent to the people of the earth is consistent with this: Adam was a prophet to his own household, while Nuh was sent to a disobedient people.

2. Does Islam teach original sin?

No. The Qur’an states that no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another (6:164; 35:18; 53:38). Adam and Hawwa’ repented and were forgiven. Every person is born with a disposition to recognise God (30:30) and is responsible for his or her own choices.

3. Was Hawwa’ (Eve) blamed for the expulsion?

Not in the Qur’an. Both are addressed together, both eat, both feel shame, and both say “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves” (7:23). The later story that Eve alone tempted Adam comes from extra-Qur’anic literature.

4. What was the forbidden tree?

The Qur’an does not say. Wheat, grape, fig and other identifications appear in the commentaries and in earlier traditions, but none is established by revelation. The silence is deliberate, and the identity of the tree is not religiously important.

5. Did Adam live for 930 years?

The figure comes from later tradition, not from the Qur’an or an authentic hadith. The Qur’an gives no lifespan for Adam. The ages of the early prophets in the Bible and in Islamic narrative literature are not reliable chronology.
