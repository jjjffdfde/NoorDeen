Repentance, Dhikr and Spiritual Development

NoorDeen Editorial · 12 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Islamic spirituality is not a separate mystical layer added to law. It is the interior dimension of the same submission: a heart that knows Allah, a tongue that remembers Him, and a life that returns to Him after failure. This article explains tawbah (repentance) and dhikr (remembrance) with practical structure, avoiding both legalism and vague sentimentality.

It draws on the Qur'an, authentic hadith, and classical works such as al-Ghazali's Ihya' and Ibn al-Qayyim's al-Wabil al-Sayyib.

Key takeaways
  • Repentance is available to everyone, no matter how serious the sin.
  • Sincere tawbah has conditions: stopping, regretting, resolving, and making amends where possible.
  • Dhikr is a command, not an optional hobby; it shapes the heart and behaviour.
  • The best dhikr is what is authentic, consistent and understood.
  • Self-accounting and a daily routine are more effective than occasional intensity.
  • Spiritual development requires community, knowledge and patience with oneself.

1. The Human Capacity for Change

The Qur'an presents human beings as capable of both great good and serious error, and it consistently offers the path back. It does not teach that a person is permanently defined by their worst act.

“Say, "O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful."”
Qur'an 39:53

This verse is addressed to those who have "transgressed against themselves" — not to the already righteous. The door of return is open. Despair is itself a spiritual disease, because it doubts Allah's mercy.

At the same time, the Qur'an does not treat repentance as a cheap transaction. It demands sincerity, cessation and repair. The balance is mercy without complacency, and accountability without despair.

2. Tawbah: Conditions and Sincerity

The classical scholars list conditions for valid repentance:

  • Ceasing the sin immediately.
  • Regret for having done it.
  • Resolving not to return to it.
  • Restoring rights where the sin involved another person — returning property, apologising, or clearing a reputation.
  • Sincerity — repenting for Allah, not merely to avoid consequences or impress people.

If the sin is between the person and Allah, these conditions suffice. If it involves another person, their rights must be addressed. This is why repentance from theft requires returning what was stolen, and repentance from backbiting may require correcting the harm or seeking forgiveness, depending on the case.

“O you who have believed, repent to Allah with sincere repentance. Perhaps your Lord will remove from you your misdeeds and admit you into gardens beneath which rivers flow.”
Qur'an 66:8

The Prophet ﷺ said: “Allah is more joyful at the repentance of His servant than one of you who found his lost camel in the desert” (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). Repentance is not a humiliation; it is a homecoming.

3. The Danger of Procrastination

The most common trap is postponing repentance. "I will change after Ramadan", "after I finish this project", "when I am older." The Qur'an warns against dying in heedlessness, and the Prophet ﷺ advised taking advantage of five before five, including health before illness and life before death (narrated by al-Hakim and others).

Procrastination often hides a deeper issue: attachment to the sin, fear of social consequences, or a mistaken belief that Allah will not forgive. Each of these needs its own remedy.

A practical rule: do not delay the decision even if you cannot immediately fix every consequence. Turn to Allah now, then take the practical steps.

4. Dhikr: What It Is and Why It Matters

Dhikr means remembrance of Allah — with the tongue, the heart, or both. The Qur'an commands it repeatedly:

“O you who have believed, remember Allah with much remembrance, and exalt Him morning and afternoon.”
Qur'an 33:41–42

And it identifies the effect:

“Those who have believed and whose hearts are assured by the remembrance of Allah. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allah hearts are assured.”
Qur'an 13:28

Dhikr is not a technique for material success. It is an act of worship that reorients the heart toward its Creator. The Prophet ﷺ said: “The example of one who remembers his Lord and one who does not is like the living and the dead” (Sahih al-Bukhari).

The best dhikr combines authenticity, understanding and consistency. A small amount done daily is better than an intense session abandoned after a week.

5. Types of Dhikr

  • Qur'anic recitation: the highest form of remembrance. Even a few verses daily, with meaning, transforms the heart.
  • Tasbih: SubhanAllah (Glory to Allah), Alhamdulillah (Praise to Allah), Allahu Akbar (Allah is Greatest).
  • Tahlil: La ilaha illa Allah — the declaration of tawhid.
  • Istighfar: seeking forgiveness, especially Astaghfirullah and the sayyid al-istighfar.
  • Salawat: sending blessings upon the Prophet ﷺ.
  • Du'a: supplication, which is remembrance with request.
  • Reflection (tafakkur): contemplating creation, scripture and one's own state.

The Prophet ﷺ taught specific dhikr for specific times: after prayer, on waking, before sleep, when travelling, when distressed. The Hisn al-Muslim collection (available in NoorDeen's Duas library) organises these by occasion.

6. Morning and Evening Adhkar

The Qur'an commands remembrance "morning and afternoon" (33:42), and the Sunnah provides a structured set of morning and evening supplications. These are not magical incantations; they are declarations of dependence, protection and praise.

A minimal daily set includes:

  • Ayat al-Kursi (2:255) — recited in the morning and evening for protection.
  • Surah al-Ikhlas, al-Falaq and an-Nas — three times each.
  • Bismillahilladhi la yadurru ma'a ismihi shay'un fi al-ard wa la fi al-sama' — three times.
  • Hasbiyallahu la ilaha illa Huwa — seven times.
  • SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi — one hundred times, if able.
  • Astaghfirullah — at least seventy or one hundred times, as in authentic reports.

Consistency matters more than volume. Attach the adhkar to fixed anchors: after Fajr and after Asr. Use the Duas library to learn the Arabic, transliteration and meaning.

7. Muraqabah and Self-Accounting

Muraqabah is the awareness that Allah sees you. Muhasabah is self-accounting: reviewing your day before Allah. Umar ibn al-Khattab famously said: "Account yourselves before you are accounted."

A simple nightly practice:

  • What did I do today that pleased Allah? Thank Him for it.
  • What did I do that displeased Him? Seek forgiveness and plan to avoid it.
  • Whose rights did I neglect? Make a concrete plan to restore them.
  • What is one small improvement for tomorrow?

This is not morbid introspection. It is honest stewardship of the self. The goal is amendment, not self-hatred.

8. The Diseases of the Heart

Classical scholars identified diseases that corrupt the heart and block spiritual growth:

  • Arrogance (kibr): rejecting truth and looking down on people.
  • Envy (hasad): resenting Allah's gifts to others.
  • Riya' (ostentation): performing good deeds for praise.
  • Heedlessness (ghaflah): living as if Allah is absent.
  • Love of the world (hubb al-dunya): allowing material attachment to dominate the heart.
  • Anger and resentment: refusing to forgive and seeking revenge.

The remedy is not to deny the feeling but to redirect it: humility for arrogance, gratitude for envy, sincerity for ostentation, dhikr for heedlessness, contentment for worldly love, and forgiveness for resentment.

9. A Thirty-Day Plan

A realistic plan is better than an ambitious one abandoned. Here is a thirty-day structure:

  • Days 1–7: establish the five prayers on time; add one page of Qur'an with translation daily; say istighfar 100 times.
  • Days 8–14: add morning and evening adhkar; memorise one short du'a; review one name of Allah each day.
  • Days 15–21: add a nightly self-accounting journal; identify one recurring sin and plan a replacement habit.
  • Days 22–30: add a small act of charity or service; reconnect with a mosque or study circle; review progress without perfectionism.

If you miss a day, resume the next day. The Prophet ﷺ said that the deeds most beloved to Allah are those done consistently, even if small (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

10. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Do I need a spiritual guide (shaykh) to practise dhikr?

No. The basic dhikr taught in the Qur'an and Sunnah can be practised by any Muslim without a personal guide. A trustworthy teacher is helpful for learning, accountability and correcting misunderstandings, but it is not a condition for valid worship.

2. Is silent or audible dhikr better?

Both are established. Silent dhikr protects sincerity and is suitable in public; audible dhikr can strengthen focus and encourage others. The Prophet ﷺ practised both. Avoid ostentatious display.

3. Can I make up missed dhikr?

Voluntary dhikr has no fixed make-up requirement, but you may perform it later. Consistent daily practice is better than accumulating debts. Some specific Sunnah dhikr are tied to their times and are not made up in the same way.

4. What if I keep falling into the same sin?

Keep repenting and keep reducing the conditions that lead to it. Change your environment, avoid triggers, seek accountability, and do not despair. The Prophet ﷺ said that Allah loves the servant who repents, and He does not tire of forgiving until we tire of asking.

5. Is music permitted during dhikr?

Scholars differ on music generally, and the use of instrumental music in religious settings is contested. The safest practice is to recite dhikr without instruments, focusing on the words and their meaning. This is a difference of jurisprudence, not a pillar of faith.
