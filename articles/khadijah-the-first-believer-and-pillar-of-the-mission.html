Khadijah: The First Believer and the Pillar of the Early Mission

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Khadijah bint Khuwaylid is the first person to believe in the message of Muhammad ﷺ and the woman whose wealth, judgement and courage made the first years of the mission survivable. Before revelation, she was a successful merchant in Makkah who employed Muhammad ﷺ to trade on her behalf. After revelation, she became the first Muslim, the first confidante of the Prophet ﷺ, and the mother of most of his children.

Her story is often reduced to a single scene: the Prophet ﷺ returns from the cave of Hira, trembling, and she wraps him in a cloak and takes him to her cousin Waraqa ibn Nawfal. That scene is authentic and important, but it is not the whole of her life. She was a mature woman of business and standing, a widow before her marriage to the Prophet ﷺ, and a partner who spent her fortune on a mission that the powerful of Makkah were trying to crush.

This article follows the established sources and marks the line between what the Qur’an and the authentic hadith establish and what later biography, poetry and preaching have added. It does not claim that every detail of her age, her previous marriages or her funeral is settled. It does claim that her place in the story of Islam is not decorative. Without her, the first community might not have survived its first decade.

Key takeaways
  • Khadijah was a Qurayshi merchant of the Banu Asad, known for her trading caravans and her reputation for integrity.
  • She employed Muhammad ﷺ before revelation and later proposed marriage to him; the sources report her age as forty, though the figure belongs to later tradition.
  • She was the first person to accept Islam, before any man or child in the standard Sunni account.
  • She supported the Prophet ﷺ emotionally and financially through the boycott and the years of persecution.
  • She bore the Prophet ﷺ six children: Qasim, Abdullah, Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum and Fatima, with some variation in the reports about the sons.
  • The year of sorrow, around 619–620 CE, took both Abu Talib and Khadijah and left the Prophet ﷺ exposed to new dangers.
  • The authentic hadith praise her as one of the best women of the worlds and record the Prophet’s lasting love for her.
  • Later stories about her exact age, her previous husbands and the details of her funeral are not all equally attested.

1. Why Khadijah Matters

Khadijah matters because she is the first test of the new revelation. When the Prophet ﷺ came down from the cave of Hira, he did not go to a council of elders or to a wealthy patron. He went to his wife. She was the first to hear the account of the encounter, the first to believe it, and the first to help him interpret it. Her faith was not a formality; it was the human foundation on which the mission rested in its most fragile hours.

She also matters because her life complicates the caricature of pre-Islamic Makkah as a world in which women had no public role. Khadijah was a merchant who sent caravans, hired agents, negotiated contracts and maintained a household of considerable standing. The sources do not present her as an exception who had to fight for the right to trade; they present her as a successful participant in the commercial life of the city.

2. The Merchant of Makkah

Khadijah was born into the Banu Asad, a clan of Quraysh. Her father was Khuwaylid ibn Asad, and her mother is named in the sources as Fatima bint Za’idah. The family was not among the poorest of Quraysh, and Khadijah inherited wealth and a trading business. The sources describe her as a woman of beauty, intelligence and strong character, but they are more interested in her reputation than in her appearance.

She traded with Syria and Yemen, sending caravans under the management of trusted agents. The sources say that she hired Muhammad ﷺ because of his reputation for honesty and fair dealing. He travelled with her goods, returned with a profit, and impressed her with the way he handled the journey. The exact terms of the partnership are not preserved in detail, but the pattern is clear: she was the principal, he was the agent, and the relationship was commercial before it was personal.

Source caution. The sources differ on Khadijah’s previous marriages and on the number of children from those marriages. Some reports mention a marriage to Abu Halah al-Tamimi and another to Atiq ibn Abid; the details are not uniform. This article does not treat those reports as a settled family tree.

3. The Marriage to Muhammad ﷺ

The marriage of Khadijah and Muhammad ﷺ is one of the best-known events of the pre-revelation period. The sources report that she proposed to him, or that she sent a trusted woman to sound him out, and that he accepted. Her father is said to have opposed the match, while her uncle or another relative acted on her behalf. The accounts differ on who spoke at the wedding and what was said.

The age reports are also uneven. The most common figure in later biography is that Khadijah was forty and Muhammad ﷺ was twenty-five. Some early reports give her a lower age, and modern historians have questioned the round number. The Qur’an does not mention the marriage or her age. The hadith collections mention her virtues but do not provide a birth certificate. The honest position is that the marriage took place when Muhammad ﷺ was a young man, that Khadijah was older than him, and that the exact ages are later estimates.

What matters more than the numbers is the character of the marriage. It lasted about twenty-five years, until Khadijah’s death. The Prophet ﷺ did not marry another woman during her lifetime. The sources present the relationship as one of mutual trust, consultation and affection. Khadijah was not a passive wife who supported a man’s career; she was a partner who helped him discern the meaning of his experience.

Source caution. The figure “forty” is traditional, not Qur’anic, and not established by a sound hadith with a precise age. It should be presented as the most common later report, not as a measured fact.

4. The First Revelation and the Cave of Hira

The first revelation is described in Sahih al-Bukhari and other collections. The Prophet ﷺ used to retreat to the cave of Hira, on the mountain of light outside Makkah, to reflect and worship. One night the angel Jibril appeared to him and said, “Read.” He replied that he could not read. The angel pressed him, then recited the opening verses of Surah al-Alaq: “Read in the name of your Lord who created, created man from a clinging substance. Read, and your Lord is the most generous, who taught by the pen, taught man that which he knew not” (96:1–5).

The Prophet ﷺ returned home trembling and asked Khadijah to cover him. She wrapped him in a cloak and calmed him. When he told her what had happened, she did not dismiss him or accuse him of madness. She said that Allah would not disgrace him, because he maintained family ties, bore the burdens of others, gave to the poor and helped those in distress. Her words are preserved in the hadith literature and are one of the earliest summaries of his character.

Khadijah then took him to her cousin Waraqa ibn Nawfal, a Christian who had studied the scriptures and was advanced in years. Waraqa recognised the pattern of revelation and said that the angel who had come to Muhammad ﷺ was the same angel who had come to Musa. He warned that the Prophet’s people would oppose him and drive him out. The report is in Sahih al-Bukhari and is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for Khadijah’s role in the first days.

““Never, by Allah, will Allah disgrace you. You keep good relations with your kin, you bear the burdens of the weak, you give to the poor, you honour the guest and you help those who are afflicted.””
Khadijah’s words to the Prophet ﷺ, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari

5. The First Muslim

The standard Sunni account places Khadijah as the first person to accept Islam. Unlike the question of the first man, which is disputed between Abu Bakr, Ali and Zayd ibn Harithah, the question of the first woman is not seriously contested in the classical sources. She believed before the public call, before the mosque, before the community had a name. Her faith was the first fruit of the revelation.

This priority is not a competition for rank. It is a statement about the nature of her response. She heard the account of the cave and believed it because she knew the man who told it. Her faith was not credulity; it was the trust of a person who had tested his character over years of marriage and business. The Qur’an praises those who believe without demanding a sign, and Khadijah is the first example of that disposition in the life of the Prophet ﷺ.

6. The Household and the Children

Khadijah bore the Prophet ﷺ six children according to the most common reports: Qasim, Abdullah, Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum and Fatima. The sons died in infancy or early childhood. The daughters lived into adulthood, and three of them were married before the hijrah or shortly after. The sources differ on whether Abdullah was also called al-Tayyib or al-Tahir, and whether those were separate sons who died young.

The household in Makkah was not a wealthy palace. The sources describe a modest home, hard work and the pressures of the boycott. Khadijah’s wealth supported the family and the mission, but it did not buy immunity from persecution. Her daughters experienced the hostility of Quraysh, and the sources report that Ruqayyah and Umm Kulthum were divorced by the sons of Abu Lahab before their marriages to Uthman ibn Affan.

Source caution. The number and names of Khadijah’s children are reported with variation, especially the sons. The article follows the most common list without claiming that every name belongs to a separate child.

7. Support, Wealth and Sacrifice

Khadijah’s support was not only emotional. She spent her wealth on the mission, on the poor and on the persecuted. The sources report that she freed slaves, helped the weak and financed the early community. Her money gave the Prophet ﷺ a measure of independence from the powerful families of Makkah. It allowed him to refuse their bribes and to continue preaching when the boycott made ordinary life impossible.

The Prophet ﷺ later remembered her generosity in a hadith reported in Sahih Muslim and other collections: she believed in him when people disbelieved, she trusted him when people rejected him, and she supported him with her wealth when people withheld theirs. The wording varies, but the substance is consistent. He called her the best of women, and he compared her to Maryam, Asiya and Fatima in different reports.

““She believed in me when the people disbelieved, she trusted me when they rejected me, and she supported me with her wealth when they withheld theirs.””
The Prophet ﷺ on Khadijah, reported in Sahih Muslim and other collections

8. The Boycott and the Year of Sorrow

The Quraysh imposed a social and commercial boycott on the Banu Hashim and the Muslims, probably around 616–619 CE. The sources describe the boycott as a written agreement hung in the Ka‘bah, the cutting off of trade and marriage, and the suffering of the vulnerable. Khadijah’s wealth was spent supporting those trapped in the valley. The episode ended when the document was found eaten by insects, a detail that later sources preserve with dramatic embellishment.

Around 619–620 CE, two deaths changed the Prophet’s life. His uncle Abu Talib, who had protected him from the worst of Quraysh, died. Khadijah died shortly afterwards, in the same year according to the most common reports. The year is called the year of sorrow. The Prophet ﷺ lost both his political protector and his closest companion within a short period.

Source caution. The story of the boycott document being eaten by insects is a later biographical detail. The boycott itself is early and widely reported; the miraculous document is not in the Qur’an or the earliest hadith.

9. Her Rank in the Hadith

The authentic hadith give Khadijah a rank that no other wife of the Prophet ﷺ shares. In Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ says that the best of women are Maryam bint Imran, Khadijah bint Khuwaylid, Fatima bint Muhammad and Asiya bint Muzahim. The exact wording and the number of women vary, but Khadijah is consistently among them.

The Prophet ﷺ also said that he was given love for her, and that he never found a replacement for her. A’ishah reported that he used to remember Khadijah often and send gifts to her friends. When A’ishah asked why he remembered a woman who had died, he replied that she had been his wife when no one else was, and that she had believed in him when others rejected him. The report is in Sahih Muslim and is one of the most human passages in the literature.

Editorial note. The hadith that list “the best women of the worlds” vary in their wording and number. Khadijah’s inclusion is stable; the exact list is not. The article does not use the lists to diminish A’ishah or Fatima.

10. A’ishah’s Memory of Khadijah

A’ishah’s reports about Khadijah are among the most important sources for the Prophet’s domestic life. She never met Khadijah, but she lived in the house that Khadijah’s memory still filled. A’ishah said that she was not jealous of any of the Prophet’s wives as much as she was jealous of Khadijah, because he remembered her so often. The reports are in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim.

In one report, A’ishah said that she asked the Prophet ﷺ about Khadijah, and he praised her. A’ishah replied that she was an old woman and that Allah had given him better than her. The Prophet ﷺ became angry and said that he had not been given better than her: she had believed in him when people disbelieved, she had trusted him when people rejected him, and she had borne his children when others did not. A’ishah later said that she never spoke of Khadijah in that way again.

Source caution. The dialogue is reported with variations in wording. It should not be turned into a polemic against A’ishah, who is the narrator and whose honesty in preserving the correction is part of her own virtue.

11. Death and Burial

Khadijah died in Makkah, probably in 619 or 620 CE, and was buried at al-Hajun, the graveyard above the city. The sources report that the Prophet ﷺ buried her himself or supervised the burial, and that he grieved for her deeply. Her grave became a place of remembrance, though the exact location is not certain today.

She was about sixty-five years old according to the common reports, which is consistent with the figure of forty at the time of her marriage and twenty-five years of marriage. The numbers are later estimates, not contemporary records. The important fact is that she died before the hijrah, before the mosque in Madinah, before the community became a state. She never saw the public triumph of the mission she had supported in its private infancy.

The Prophet ﷺ named a year after her sorrow, remembered her friends, and defended her memory. The sources also report that he sent a cloak or a garment to her sister or to a woman who had been close to her. These small acts of remembrance are more revealing than the later legends. They show a man who did not forget the woman who had believed in him when no one else did.

12. The Later Legends and Their Limits

Khadijah’s story has attracted many later additions. The most common are the exact age of forty, the detailed account of her previous marriages, the story of the jinn or the monk who recognised the Prophet ﷺ before the marriage, and the elaborate descriptions of her funeral. Some of these details may preserve early memories; others are clearly later edification.

The age of forty is a round number that may reflect a symbolic rather than a documentary tradition. The previous marriages are reported in some biographical works but not in the earliest layers with consistent detail. The story of the monk Bahira, which is sometimes attached to the marriage narrative, belongs to a different cycle of reports about the Prophet’s youth and is not part of the Khadijah story in the earliest sources.

Source caution. The “forty years old” figure, the previous-marriage details and the funeral descriptions are later biographical traditions. They are not Qur’anic and are not uniformly attested in the sound hadith.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

Khadijah’s life offers four lessons. The first is the dignity of work: she was a merchant who managed capital, agents and risk, and her competence was part of her virtue. The second is the courage of belief: she accepted the revelation before it had a community, a mosque or a state. The third is the power of partnership: she supported the Prophet ﷺ not as a follower who obeyed without thought but as a confidante who helped him interpret his experience. The fourth is the honesty of grief: the Prophet ﷺ mourned her, remembered her and defended her memory without turning her into an idol.

The limits of the sources are also a lesson. We do not know her exact age, the exact date of her death or the full details of her business. The later biographies fill the gaps with edifying stories, and some of those stories are beautiful. But the historian’s discipline is to distinguish the beautiful from the established. Khadijah does not need the legends. Her place is secure in the Qur’an’s first audience, in the hadith, and in the memory of the community.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith, the early biographical reports and later narrative. It does not claim that every detail about Khadijah is historically established, and it does not use her rank to silence legitimate historical questions.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Was Khadijah the first Muslim?

Yes, in the standard Sunni account she was the first person to accept Islam, before any man or child. The question of the first man is disputed, but the first woman is not seriously contested in the classical sources.

2. How old was Khadijah when she married the Prophet ﷺ?

The most common later report says she was forty and he was twenty-five. Some early reports give a lower age, and modern historians have questioned the round number. The Qur’an and the sound hadith do not give a precise age, so the figure should be presented as a later tradition rather than a measured fact.

3. How many children did Khadijah have?

The most common list is six: Qasim, Abdullah, Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum and Fatima. The sources differ on the names of the sons and on whether Abdullah was also called al-Tayyib or al-Tahir. The daughters are consistently named.

4. Did the Prophet ﷺ marry anyone else during Khadijah’s lifetime?

No. The sources are consistent that he remained married to Khadijah alone until her death, after about twenty-five years of marriage. He married other women after her death.

5. What is the year of sorrow?

It is the year around 619–620 CE in which both Abu Talib, the Prophet’s uncle and protector, and Khadijah died. The Prophet ﷺ called it the year of sorrow because it removed both his political protection and his closest companion.
