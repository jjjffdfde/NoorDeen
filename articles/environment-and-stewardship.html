Environment and Stewardship: The Islamic Case for Caring for the Earth

NoorDeen Editorial · 11 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The environment is not a secular afterthought in Islam. The Qur'an presents the natural world as a sign of Allah, a trust, and a balanced order that humans are responsible for maintaining. This article gathers the scriptural foundations and turns them into practical habits, without pretending that every environmental policy is a religious obligation.

It is an educational reflection, not a political programme. Muslims may differ on policies while agreeing on the underlying duty of stewardship.

Key takeaways
  • Humans are stewards (khalifah) accountable for the earth, not owners with unlimited licence.
  • The Qur'an describes creation as balanced (mizan) and warns against corruption.
  • Waste is condemned; moderation is a Quranic principle.
  • Kindness to animals and planting trees are acts of reward.
  • Environmental harm is a moral issue, not only a technical one.
  • Practical action should be proportionate, informed and sustained.

1. Stewardship, Not Domination

The Qur'an says:

“And [mention] when your Lord said to the angels, “Indeed, I will make upon the earth a successive authority [khalifah].””
Qur'an 2:30

The word khalifah carries the sense of a successor or steward. Humans are placed on earth to manage it according to Allah's guidance, not to exploit it without limit. The Qur'an also says:

“It is He who has made you successors upon the earth and has raised some of you above others in degrees, that He may test you through what He has given you.”
Qur'an 6:165

The test is not only in wealth and power but in how we use the resources entrusted to us. The earth belongs to Allah:

“To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.”
Qur'an 2:255

This reframes ownership. A farmer, a company or a state may hold land, but the ultimate owner is Allah, and the holding carries duties to neighbours, future generations and other creatures.

2. Balance and Corruption

The Qur'an describes creation as measured and balanced:

“And the heaven He raised and imposed the balance, that you not transgress within the balance.”
Qur'an 55:7–8

It warns against spreading corruption on earth:

“And do not cause corruption on the earth after its reformation.”
Qur'an 7:56

And it links human actions to environmental disorder:

“Corruption has appeared throughout the land and sea by [reason of] what the hands of people have earned, so He may let them taste part of [the consequence of] what they have done that perhaps they will return.”
Qur'an 30:41

This verse is often read as a warning about moral and social corruption, but it also supports a broader ecological reading: human greed and excess damage the systems on which life depends. The Qur'an does not separate “spiritual” from “material” responsibility.

3. Water, Waste and Moderation

The Qur'an commands moderation in eating and drinking:

“Eat and drink, but be not excessive. Indeed, He likes not those who commit excess.”
Qur'an 7:31

The Prophet ﷺ was known for economy in water. A well-known report says he passed by Sa'd while he was performing ablution and said: “Do not waste water”. Sa'd asked, “Is there waste in ablution?” He replied: “Yes, even if you are on the bank of a flowing river” (narrated by Ibn Majah; graded hasan by some scholars).

Whether or not one accepts the exact grading, the principle is clear and repeated: waste is ingratitude. Practical applications:

  • Use only the water needed for wudu and ghusl; do not leave taps running.
  • Fix leaks and report communal waste.
  • Reduce food waste; plan meals and share surplus.
  • Avoid single-use plastic where a durable alternative is easy.
  • Repair rather than replace when repair is reasonable.

Moderation is not deprivation. The Qur'an praises those who spend without extravagance and without stinginess (25:67).

4. Kindness to Animals

The Prophet ﷺ taught that a woman entered Paradise for giving water to a thirsty dog, and that a woman was punished for starving a cat (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). He forbade branding animals on the face, separating a mother from her young unnecessarily, and overloading beasts.

The Qur'an says:

“And there is no creature on [or within] the earth or bird that flies with its wings except [that they are] communities like you.”
Qur'an 6:38

Animals are not mere machines. They have needs, fear and pain. Islamic slaughter rules themselves reflect mercy: a sharp blade, a swift cut, and no slaughter in front of other animals. Hunting for sport without need, animal fighting and neglect are contrary to this ethos.

Pets, livestock and wildlife all fall under the duty of care. If you cannot meet an animal's needs, do not take it on.

5. Planting and Long-Term Thinking

The Prophet ﷺ said: “If the Hour comes while one of you has a seedling in his hand, let him plant it” (narrated by Ahmad and others; graded hasan). He also said that whoever plants a tree and a bird, human or animal eats from it, it is charity for him (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).

This is a striking image of long-term responsibility: act for the future even when you may not see the benefit. It supports:

  • Planting and protecting trees and green spaces.
  • Caring for soil, water and biodiversity.
  • Supporting sustainable agriculture and avoiding needless destruction.
  • Teaching children to value creation, not only to consume it.

Environmental action is not a substitute for worship, and worship is not an excuse for negligence. The two belong together.

6. Practical Steps for Individuals and Communities

  • Audit your consumption: food, water, energy, clothing and electronics. Identify one area to improve this month.
  • Reduce, reuse, repair, recycle — in that order. Recycling is the last resort, not the first.
  • Eat locally and seasonally where possible, and waste less. Food waste is both an ethical and an economic failure.
  • Use energy wisely: switch off, insulate, choose efficient appliances, and avoid unnecessary travel.
  • Support community projects: mosque gardens, tree planting, clean-ups and water conservation.
  • Speak honestly: do not exaggerate scientific claims, and do not dismiss real problems for convenience.
  • Teach and model: children learn stewardship by watching adults, not by lectures.

No single person can fix global systems. But the Qur'an judges by intention and effort, not by whether the whole world changes. A consistent small habit is better than a dramatic gesture that ends in burnout.

7. Balancing Stewardship with Human Needs

Environmental ethics can become harsh or unrealistic. Islam recognises legitimate human needs: food, shelter, work, travel and comfort. The goal is not poverty or guilt but justice and moderation.

Principles for balance:

  • Prioritise harm reduction where trade-offs are unavoidable.
  • Do not impose burdens on the poor in the name of ecology while ignoring corporate and state responsibility.
  • Avoid both denial (“nothing is happening”) and despair (“nothing can be done”).
  • Seek knowledge from qualified experts and be honest about uncertainty.
  • Remember that the earth is a trust for future generations, not a final consumption.

The Prophet ﷺ lived simply, repaired his own sandals, and warned against extravagance. His example is a practical middle path: grateful use, not greedy waste.

8. The Mosque, the Neighbourhood and Collective Duty

Stewardship is not only a private lifestyle. In Madinah the Prophet ﷺ established a mosque and a protected area (harim) around the settlement, and classical jurists developed rules for the protection of wells, rivers, trees and common pasture because these belong to the community, not to whoever reaches them first.

That legacy suggests concrete communal duties:

  • Mosques and Islamic centres can audit their own energy and water use, plant shade trees, and reduce disposable waste at gatherings.
  • Communities can organise clean-ups, repair projects and shared tools rather than replacing items individually.
  • Charity can support water access, sanitation and reforestation, especially where the poorest bear the heaviest environmental burden.
  • Neighbours have rights in Islam; noise, smoke, rubbish and blocked drains are not only environmental nuisances but failures of neighbourliness.
  • Public advocacy should be truthful and proportionate, avoiding both alarmism and complacency.

The Prophet ﷺ described the believers as a single body, and the Qur'an commands cooperation in righteousness (5:2). Environmental care is one arena where that cooperation becomes visible: a shared well, a clean street, a tree that outlives the planter.

9. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Is environmentalism a religious duty?

Stewardship, avoiding waste, kindness to animals and moderation are clearly grounded in the Qur'an and Sunnah. Specific policies are matters of judgement on which Muslims may differ.

2. Does Islam forbid eating meat?

No. Meat is lawful, and the Prophet ﷺ ate it. Islam regulates slaughter and encourages moderation and kindness to animals; it does not require vegetarianism.

3. Is climate change a Muslim issue?

Caring for creation and avoiding harm are Muslim concerns. The scientific details and policy responses are matters for experts; the moral duty to reduce waste and protect the vulnerable is clear.

4. What is the ruling on wasting water in wudu?

Waste is condemned even with abundant water. Use what is needed and avoid extravagance. The exact amount is not fixed; the principle is moderation.

5. Can I keep a pet?

Yes, with proper care, food, water, space and veterinary attention. Neglect is a serious wrong. Some animals require specific permits or are unsuitable for domestic keeping; follow local law and animal welfare guidance.

10. Sources & Further Reading

This article cites the Qur'an by surah and verse, and identifies hadith by collection and, where verified, book or number. Where scholars differ, the difference is stated rather than resolved by assertion.

  • The Qur'an — especially 2:30, 6:38, 6:165, 7:31, 7:56, 25:67, 30:41 and 55:7–9.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — kindness to animals and planting trees.
  • Ibn Majah and Ahmad — reports on wasting water and planting (gradings noted in the text).
  • Al-Nawawi, Riyad al-Salihin — chapters on moderation and mercy.
  • Contemporary Islamic environmental ethics, read alongside peer-reviewed environmental science.
