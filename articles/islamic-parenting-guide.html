Raising Children in Islam: Parenting with Mercy, Structure and Purpose

NoorDeen Editorial · 13 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The Qur'an calls children both a trial and a comfort: “Your wealth and your children are but a trial, and Allah has with Him a great reward” (64:15), and the righteous pray, “Our Lord, grant us from among our spouses and offspring comfort to our eyes” (25:74). Both are true. Parenting is a trust that exposes a person's patience, ego and dependence on Allah.

This guide draws on the Qur'an, authenticated Prophetic reports and widely accepted principles of child welfare. It is not a manual for every family, and it does not replace a scholar, teacher, doctor or safeguarding professional when a child is in danger.

Key takeaways
  • Children are a trust, not a possession or a project for parental status.
  • The Prophetic model combines affection, patience and firm, proportionate boundaries.
  • Tawhid and prayer are taught gradually, by example and without coercion.
  • Corporal punishment is not the Prophetic default and is legally restricted in many countries.
  • Fairness, listening and emotional safety shape a child's image of God and of authority.
  • Parents remain responsible for guidance, but not for controlling an adult child's every choice.

1. Children as a Trust

The Qur'an describes wealth and children as fitnah, a test, not because children are bad but because they reveal what a person loves, fears and sacrifices. A child can become an idol, a status symbol, or a vessel for a parent's unmet ambitions. The believer is asked to love without worshipping the gift.

“O you who have believed, protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones.”
Qur'an 66:6

Protection here is not only physical. It includes teaching a child what is true, modelling justice, and creating an environment in which questions can be asked without fear. A home that punishes every doubt may produce outward conformity and inward distance.

The Prophet ﷺ treated children as full human beings. He greeted them, carried them during prayer, shortened the prayer when a child cried, and allowed them to play. The reports are not sentimental decoration; they establish a norm.

2. The Prophetic Model of Mercy

The Prophet ﷺ said, “He is not one of us who does not show mercy to our young or respect our old” (reported in al-Tirmidhi and Abu Dawud; scholars grade it hasan). He kissed his grandson al-Hasan in front of a man who said he had ten children and had never kissed one. The Prophet replied, “What can I do for you if Allah has removed mercy from your heart?” (Sahih al-Bukhari 5997).

Mercy is not permissiveness. The same Prophet ﷺ gave clear instructions about prayer, honesty and respect, and he corrected children when needed. The balance is captured in the report that he said, “The best of you are those who are best to their families” (reported in al-Tirmidhi).

For parents, this means distinguishing between the child and the behaviour. “You are a bad boy” attacks identity; “You hit your sister, and that is not allowed” addresses the act. The first produces shame and defensiveness; the second makes repair possible.

A note on violence: the Prophet ﷺ forbade striking the face (Sahih Muslim), and he never struck a child or a servant in the reports describing his household. Many contemporary scholars and child-protection bodies treat hitting as harmful and avoid it. Physical abuse is never justified by “discipline”.

3. Teaching Tawhid First

Luqman's advice to his son begins with the foundation:

“O my son, do not associate anything with Allah. Indeed, association with Him is a great injustice.”
Qur'an 31:13

The passage then moves through gratitude to parents, humility, prayer, enjoining good, patience, moderation in walking and speech, and avoiding arrogance. It is a curriculum in character, not a list of slogans.

Children learn tawhid less from lectures than from what they see: whether adults lie, whether they treat the vulnerable with dignity, whether they pray when no one is watching, whether they mention Allah with love or only with fear. A parent who explains “Allah sees us” while shouting at a cashier teaches two contradictory lessons.

Questions about God, suffering and other religions should be welcomed. “I don't know; let's find out together” is a better answer than a fabricated certainty. The Qur'an itself invites reflection on the heavens, the earth and the self.

4. Prayer, Routine and Discipline

The well-known report instructs parents to order children to pray at seven and to apply appropriate discipline at ten (reported by Abu Dawud; scholars grade it hasan). It describes a gradual process, not a switch. A seven-year-old is learning the movements and words; a ten-year-old is building consistency.

Practical habits that help:

  • Pray beside the child before asking the child to pray alone.
  • Keep the prayer space clean, simple and associated with calm rather than shouting.
  • Teach short surahs and duas first; add length as capacity grows.
  • Use reminders rather than humiliation, especially in front of siblings or guests.
  • Distinguish between a child who is learning and an adult who is deliberately abandoning an obligation.

Discipline should be predictable, proportionate and connected to the act. Removing a privilege for a stated period is different from hours of shouting or a punishment that the parent invents in anger. The child should know what the rule is before the consequence arrives.

The Prophet ﷺ said, “None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself” (Sahih al-Bukhari 13; Sahih Muslim 45). In a family, that includes loving for a child the same dignity, patience and privacy the parent expects for themselves.

5. Emotional Health and Listening

A child's emotional system is still developing. Repeated shaming, threats of abandonment and unpredictable anger can teach a child that love is conditional and that authority is dangerous. Those lessons often outlast the childhood.

Listening is a discipline. It means putting the phone down, asking open questions, and resisting the urge to correct every feeling. “You seem angry about school” is not agreeing with the anger; it is helping the child name it. Only after that can a parent guide.

The Qur'an describes the Prophet ﷺ as gentle with his companions: “It is by mercy from Allah that you were lenient with them. And if you had been rude and harsh-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you” (3:159). If this is how a leader was told to treat adults, a parent's case for harshness with a child is weak.

Sleep, food, movement and screen limits affect behaviour more than many parents expect. A child who is exhausted or hungry is not in a state to learn self-control. Address the body before diagnosing the character.

6. Fairness Between Children

Favouritism is an old problem. The Prophet ﷺ was told by Nu'man ibn Bashir's father that he had given one son a gift and not the others. He asked whether the other children were also given the same, and when the answer was no, he refused to witness the gift and said, “Fear Allah and be just between your children” (Sahih al-Bukhari 2587; Sahih Muslim 1623).

Fairness is not identical treatment. A sick child needs more time; a younger child needs more supervision; a child with a disability may need more resources. Fairness means that the reason for different treatment is the child's need, not the parent's preference, and that children are not left to guess whether they are loved less.

Gender favouritism is particularly damaging. The Qur'an condemns the pre-Islamic resentment of daughters (16:58–59) and promises the same moral accountability to both. A family that celebrates a son's achievement while dismissing a daughter's, or that expects a daughter to serve a brother, teaches injustice in the name of tradition.

7. Screens, School and Modern Pressures

Contemporary parents face pressures earlier generations did not: constant connectivity, sexualised media, online bullying, academic competition and algorithms designed to capture attention. Pretending these do not exist is not protective.

A workable approach includes:

  • Agree on device-free times and places, and apply them to adults too.
  • Keep younger children's devices in shared spaces and learn what the apps actually do.
  • Teach privacy and consent, including the rule that images are not shared without permission.
  • Distinguish between curiosity and harmful content; react to the first with conversation, not panic.
  • Protect sleep and offline friendship; both are stronger predictors of wellbeing than perfect grades.

The Qur'an's teaching on privacy in the home remains relevant: children should ask permission at sensitive times, and adults should not spy on one another (24:58–59; 49:12). A parent's authority does not erase a child's right to dignity.

School choices, extracurricular activities and career pressure should be discussed with the child's temperament in mind. Not every child is a doctor, and not every path that pleases relatives is good for the child.

8. Parenting Adult Children

The duty of guidance does not end, but the mode changes. Adult children make their own lawful decisions, including decisions a parent disagrees with. The Qur'an's principle in the case of religious disagreement is kindness without obedience to sin (31:15); the same spirit applies to marriage, career and lifestyle choices.

Parents often struggle most when they fear a child is making a mistake. Advice given once, with respect, is usually heard better than advice repeated daily. Ultimatums, financial blackmail and shunning may produce compliance or distance, but rarely conviction.

“There is no compulsion in religion” (2:256) is a foundational principle. It does not mean that parents should not teach; it means that faith cannot be coerced into existence. A parent can plant, water and pray, but cannot manufacture the harvest.

Grandparenting brings a new test: respecting the parents' rules even when the grandparent disagrees. Undermining a daughter-in-law or son-in-law in front of the children creates conflicts that outlast the visit.

9. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Is it permissible to hit children?

The Prophet ﷺ forbade striking the face and is not reported to have struck a child. Many contemporary scholars and child-protection bodies advise against hitting altogether, and it is illegal in many countries. Discipline should be proportionate, non-humiliating and connected to the behaviour. Physical abuse is never acceptable.

2. At what age should children start praying?

The well-known report mentions instructing children at seven and applying appropriate discipline at ten (Abu Dawud, graded hasan). The purpose is gradual formation. A child should first see prayer as normal, learn short surahs, and be encouraged without humiliation.

3. How do I handle a child who asks difficult questions about Islam?

Welcome the question and be honest about what you know. “I don't know, let's ask someone who does” is better than inventing an answer. Questions are not automatically rebellion; they are part of growing understanding.

4. Should I treat sons and daughters differently?

Needs may differ, but worth and dignity do not. The Qur'an condemns the pre-Islamic resentment of daughters and holds both accountable to the same moral standard. Favouritism, unequal education and using a daughter as a servant for a son are injustices, not Islamic norms.

5. What if my adult child leaves Islam?

The Qur'an prohibits compulsion in religion (2:256) and commands kind treatment even when parents are pressured toward shirk (31:15). Keep the relationship open, make dua, and avoid abusive or humiliating tactics. For specific family and legal questions, consult a qualified scholar and, where relevant, a counsellor.

10. Sources & Further Reading

This article cites the Qur'an by surah and verse, and identifies hadith by collection and, where verified, book or number. Where scholars differ, the difference is stated rather than resolved by assertion.

  • The Qur'an — especially 2:233, 2:256, 3:159, 16:58–59, 24:58–59, 25:74, 31:13–19, 49:11–13, 64:15 and 66:6. Translations adapted from Saheeh International unless stated.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari — mercy to children (5997), fairness between children (2587) and brotherhood (13).
  • Sahih Muslim — fairness between children (1623), the prohibition of striking the face, and the brotherhood hadith (45).
  • Sunan Abi Dawud — the report on instructing children to pray (495), graded hasan by scholars.
  • Jami' al-Tirmidhi — mercy to the young and respect for the old (1919), and the best of you to their families (3895).
  • Contemporary child-welfare and safeguarding guidance, read alongside qualified scholarly advice for case-specific questions.
