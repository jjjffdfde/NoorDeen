The Hijrah to Madinah: Planning, Trust and a New Society

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The Hijrah is the hinge of the Seerah. In 622 CE the Prophet ﷺ and the early Muslims left Makkah for Yathrib, a town later known as Madinat al-Nabi. The move was not an escape from responsibility; it was a carefully planned migration that created the conditions for a new society.

The Hijrah involved secret negotiations, a dangerous journey, a cave, a guide, a pursuer and a reception. It also involved a written agreement among the communities of Madinah, a brotherhood between the refugees and the helpers, and the construction of a mosque. This article follows the main stages and distinguishes the established reports from later embellishment.

Key takeaways
  • The pledges of Aqabah prepared the ground for the migration by securing protection and support in Yathrib.
  • The Prophet ﷺ planned the journey carefully: Ali stayed in his bed, Abu Bakr accompanied him, a guide was hired and a cave was used for concealment.
  • The Qur’an refers to the cave in 9:40: “Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us.”
  • The arrival at Quba and the first mosque marked the beginning of a new community.
  • The document of Madinah established mutual obligations among the Muslims, the Jews and the tribes of the town.
  • The brotherhood between the Muhajirun and the Ansar was a radical experiment in sharing wealth and loyalty.
  • The Hijri calendar was instituted later by Umar, not by the Prophet ﷺ, and it begins with the year of the migration.

1. A Turning Point After Thirteen Years

By the tenth year of prophethood, the Prophet ﷺ had lost Abu Talib and Khadijah, been rejected at Ta’if and seen the Makkan opposition harden. The community was still small and vulnerable. Then came the possibility of a new base.

Yathrib, an oasis town about 400 kilometres north of Makkah, was divided between the tribes of Aws and Khazraj and included Jewish communities. Its people had heard of the Prophet ﷺ through travellers and relatives. Some of them met him during the pilgrimage season and accepted Islam. These meetings led to the pledges of Aqabah.

The Hijrah was not a sudden decision. It was the result of years of preaching, relationship-building and careful assessment. The Prophet ﷺ did not flee in panic; he sent his companions ahead in small groups, arranged protection and waited for the right moment.

2. The Pledges of Aqabah

The first pledge of Aqabah took place around 621 CE, when twelve men from Yathrib met the Prophet ﷺ and pledged to worship Allah alone, avoid theft, fornication and the killing of children, obey the Prophet ﷺ in what is right and not disobey him. This is known as the “pledge of women” because it resembles the conditions mentioned in Surah al-Mumtahanah 60:12.

The second pledge, around 622 CE, involved seventy-three men and two women. They pledged to protect the Prophet ﷺ as they would protect their own families. The Prophet ﷺ then sent Mus’ab ibn Umayr to Yathrib to teach the Qur’an and Islam. Mus’ab’s work prepared the town for the migration.

The pledges are reported in the Sirah and in Sahih al-Bukhari. They show that the Hijrah was based on a mutual covenant, not merely on an invitation. The people of Yathrib committed themselves to protect the Prophet ﷺ and the Muslims, and the Prophet ﷺ committed himself to their community.

3. The Plot and the Departure

Quraysh realised that the Prophet ﷺ was preparing to leave. They met in Dar al-Nadwa and agreed to kill him, choosing a young man from each clan so that the blood would be spread among the tribes and Banu Hashim could not take revenge on one family. The Qur’an refers to their plotting: “And [remember] when those who disbelieved plotted against you to restrain you or kill you or evict you” (8:30).

The Prophet ﷺ learned of the plot and arranged for Ali to sleep in his bed, wrapped in his cloak. He told Ali that no harm would come to him. He then left with Abu Bakr, and they hid in the cave of Thawr, south of Makkah, for three days.

The choice of Thawr was strategic: it was in the opposite direction from Yathrib, so pursuers would look north while the two men waited south. Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr brought news, Asma bint Abi Bakr brought food, and Amir ibn Fuhayrah, Abu Bakr’s freed slave, grazed sheep to erase footprints. The plan combined human intelligence with trust in Allah.

4. The Cave of Thawr

The most famous Qur’anic reference to the Hijrah is in Surah al-Tawbah:

“If you do not aid him — Allah has already aided him when those who disbelieved had driven him out [of Makkah] as one of two, when they were in the cave and he said to his companion, “Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us.””
Qur’an 9:40

The verse names Abu Bakr as the companion of the cave and records the Prophet’s ﷺ reassurance. It is a powerful statement of trust: the two men were in physical danger, with pursuers nearby, and the Prophet ﷺ told his companion not to grieve because Allah was with them.

The Sirah adds that Quraysh offered a reward for their capture and that a tracker followed them to the cave, but found the entrance covered by a spider’s web and a dove’s nest. The spider and dove detail is a later report, not an authentic hadith. It is beloved in popular storytelling, but it should be labelled as a later addition rather than presented as certain history.

What is certain is the cave, the three days, the pursuers and the Qur’anic verse. The lesson does not depend on the spider.

5. The Journey to Yathrib

After three days, the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr set out with a guide, Abdullah ibn Urayqit, who was not yet a Muslim but knew the desert routes. They travelled along the Red Sea coast to avoid the main caravan road. The journey was difficult: heat, thirst, rough terrain and the fear of pursuit.

One of the pursuers, Suraqa ibn Malik, caught up with them. According to the Sirah, his horse stumbled, and he eventually asked for protection and promised not to reveal their location. He later became a Muslim. The story is preserved in the Sirah and later histories; it is not an authentic hadith, but it is part of the early narrative.

Another famous story is that of Umm Ma’bad, a woman who gave them milk from a weak sheep. The report is found in Ibn Hisham and later sources, not in the two Sahihs. It is a beautiful story of hospitality and blessing, but it should be described as a later report.

The Prophet ﷺ did not travel alone or rely on supernatural transport. He hired a guide, took provisions, planned a route and accepted help from non-Muslims. The Hijrah is a model of planning and trust, not of passivity.

6. Quba and the First Mosque

The Prophet ﷺ reached the outskirts of Yathrib, at Quba, around the beginning of Rabi’ al-Awwal. He stayed with the family of Amr ibn Awf and laid the foundation of a mosque there. The Qur’an refers to a mosque “founded upon piety from the first day” (9:108), which many exegetes identify with Quba.

The first Friday prayer was held in the valley of Ranuna on the way into Yathrib. The Prophet ﷺ delivered a sermon and led the prayer. The community then entered the town, and the Prophet ﷺ stayed with Abu Ayyub al-Ansari while the mosque and his houses were built.

Quba is significant because it shows that the first act of the new community was worship and construction, not conquest. The Prophet ﷺ built a mosque before he built a palace. The mosque was a place of prayer, teaching, consultation and refuge — the centre of the new society.

7. Madinah and the Document of the City

Yathrib was a plural society. It included the Muhajirun (the emigrants from Makkah), the Ansar (the helpers from Aws and Khazraj), Jewish tribes such as Banu Qaynuqa, Banu Nadir and Banu Qurayzah, and other groups with their own alliances. The Prophet ﷺ established a written agreement, often called the Constitution of Madinah, to regulate relations among them.

The document is preserved by Ibn Ishaq and Ibn Hisham. It declares that the Muslims and the Jews of Madinah are one community (ummah) alongside the believers, that each group retains its religion, that they cooperate against aggression, that disputes are referred to the Prophet ﷺ, and that the Jews share the costs of war with the Muslims. It also addresses the tribes of the town and their obligations.

The document is not a modern constitution, and it did not create a secular state. It was a covenant among communities with different religions and tribal loyalties, designed to prevent the kind of civil war that had torn Yathrib apart. It established a common framework of security and adjudication while recognising religious difference.

Its precise application was tested by later conflicts, and some Jewish tribes were eventually expelled or punished after breaches of the agreement. Historians debate the details and the extent of collective responsibility. What remains clear is that the Prophet ﷺ began with a plural agreement, not with forced conversion.

8. The Brotherhood of Muhajirun and Ansar

The emigrants had left their homes and property in Makkah. The helpers of Madinah opened their homes and shared their wealth. The Prophet ﷺ established a bond of brotherhood (mu’akhah) between individual Muhajirun and Ansar, a relationship stronger than a business partnership and more formal than friendship.

The Qur’an praises the Ansar: “And those who had settled in the home and [adopted] the faith before them love those who emigrated to them and find not any want in their breasts of what they were given but give [them] preference over themselves, even though they were in need” (59:9). It also describes the believers as brothers (49:10) and the Muhajirun and Ansar as allies of one another (8:72).

The brotherhood was not a permanent transfer of property. It was a system of support during the initial period of hardship. Some Ansar offered to divide their wealth; the Prophet ﷺ encouraged generosity but also organised work, trade and the distribution of the spoils of war. The goal was dignity, not dependency.

This experiment in sharing is one of the most striking social achievements of the Seerah. It was not based on ethnic kinship but on faith. It created a community that could absorb refugees and integrate them into a new society.

9. The Hijri Calendar

The Islamic lunar calendar begins with the year of the Hijrah. It was not instituted by the Prophet ﷺ himself; according to the reports, Umar ibn al-Khattab established it during his caliphate to give the growing state a common dating system. The first year is 1 AH, corresponding to 622 CE.

The calendar is lunar, with twelve months of twenty-nine or thirty days. The Qur’an says that the number of months with Allah is twelve (9:36) and that the new moons are markers of time for people and pilgrimage (2:189). The sighting of the crescent is the traditional method for beginning a month, though scholars and communities differ on whether astronomical calculation may be used.

The Hijri calendar is not a claim that the Prophet ﷺ commanded a particular numbering system. It is a collective memory: every year counts from the migration that made the community possible. For Muslims, it is a reminder that the Hijrah was not a single event but the beginning of a new phase in the life of the ummah.

10. Lessons from the Hijrah

The Hijrah offers lessons for individuals and communities:

  • Plan, then trust. The Prophet ﷺ arranged the route, the guide, the food and the hiding place, and he trusted Allah with the outcome.
  • Migration can be worship. Leaving a place of oppression for a place where faith can be practised is a prophetic strategy.
  • Build institutions. The first acts were a mosque, a covenant and a brotherhood, not a monument.
  • Pluralism requires justice. The document of Madinah protected different communities within a common framework.
  • Refugees need dignity. The Ansar shared their wealth, but the goal was to help the Muhajirun stand on their own feet.
  • Gratitude is political. The calendar begins with the Hijrah because the community remembered what Allah had done for it.

The Hijrah is often remembered as a flight, but it was also a founding. It created the space in which the Qur’an could be applied, the Prophet ﷺ could teach, and a community could learn to govern itself. The migration was not the end of the Makkan struggle; it was the beginning of the Madinan responsibility.

11. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for the Hijrah are:

  • The Qur’an — especially 8:30, 8:72, 9:40, 9:108, 59:9 and 60:12.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari — the pledges of Aqabah, the Hijrah and the Prophet’s ﷺ arrival in Madinah.
  • Sahih Muslim — parallel reports on the Hijrah and the brotherhood.
  • Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah — the most detailed early narrative, including the cave, the journey, Quba and the document of Madinah.
  • Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-Rusul wa al-Muluk — the chronology and the reports of the journey.
  • Ibn Kathir, al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah — the Hijrah and the early Madinan period.

The Sirah contains reports of varying reliability. The spider and dove, Umm Ma’bad and some details of Suraqa are later reports, not authentic hadith. The Qur’anic verses, the pledges, the cave, the arrival at Quba, the mosque and the document are established in the early sources.

Editorial note: the exact dates of the Hijrah are approximate. The conventional date is 622 CE, and the Hijri calendar begins with that year, but the month and day are reported differently. The article follows the standard chronology without claiming precision the sources do not provide.

12. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Why is the Hijrah so important?

It marks the transition from a persecuted minority in Makkah to a community with a base, institutions and the freedom to practise and teach Islam. The Hijri calendar begins with it because it reshaped the life of the ummah.

2. Did the Prophet ﷺ flee secretly?

He left carefully and quietly, with a planned route and a guide, because Quraysh had plotted to kill him. It was not a panic flight; it was a strategic migration.

3. What is the Constitution of Madinah?

A written agreement preserved by Ibn Ishaq and Ibn Hisham that regulated relations among the Muslims, the Jewish tribes and the other communities of Madinah. It established mutual obligations, religious freedom for the groups and a common framework of security and adjudication.

4. Who were the Ansar and the Muhajirun?

The Muhajirun were the emigrants from Makkah; the Ansar were the helpers of Madinah who received them. The Prophet ﷺ established a bond of brotherhood between them, and the Ansar shared their homes and wealth.

5. Did the Prophet ﷺ start the Hijri calendar?

The calendar begins with the Hijrah, but the reports attribute its formal institution to Umar ibn al-Khattab during his caliphate. It is a collective memory of the migration, not a direct command of the Prophet ﷺ.
