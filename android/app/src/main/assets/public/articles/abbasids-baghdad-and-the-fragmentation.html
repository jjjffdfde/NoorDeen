The Abbasids: Baghdad, the Translation Movement and the Fragmentation of the Caliphate

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

The Abbasids ruled the Muslim world for five centuries, longer than any other caliphate, and their name is attached to the most celebrated period of Islamic civilisation. Baghdad, founded in 762, became the largest city in the world outside China, a centre of trade, scholarship and administration. The translation of Greek, Persian and Indian learning into Arabic, the development of paper, the growth of a commercial economy and the flowering of literature all belong to their era.

The Abbasids are also the dynasty that lost control of its own empire. By the middle of the tenth century the caliph was a figurehead under the protection of the Buyids, and by the eleventh he was a client of the Seljuks. The caliphate survived as a symbol of religious authority long after it had ceased to govern. In 1258 the Mongols sacked Baghdad and killed the last caliph, and the institution never recovered its central place.

This article follows the Abbasids from the revolution of 750 to the catastrophe of 1258. It treats the “golden age” as a real achievement and as a selective memory: the same period produced the mihna, the enslavement of millions, sectarian violence and the fragmentation of political authority.

Key takeaways
  • The Abbasid revolution of 750 overthrew the Umayyads and established a new dynasty based in Iraq.
  • Baghdad was founded by al-Mansur in 762 as a round city and became the administrative and commercial capital of the empire.
  • Harun al-Rashid (786–809) is the most famous Abbasid caliph, but his reign ended in a civil war between his sons.
  • The translation movement rendered Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic and shaped the intellectual tradition of the Islamic world.
  • The mihna, a caliphal inquisition over the createdness of the Qur’an, was imposed by al-Ma’mun in 833 and ended under al-Mutawakkil.
  • Paper, credit and long-distance trade transformed the urban economy and made Baghdad a commercial hub.
  • Political power fragmented: the Buyids took Baghdad in 945, the Seljuks in 1055, and the caliph became a symbolic figure.
  • The Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258, killing the caliph and ending the Abbasid caliphate in its historic capital.

1. Why the Abbasids Matter

The Abbasids matter because they presided over the formation of classical Islamic civilisation. The schools of law, the science of hadith, the systematisation of theology, the codification of Arabic grammar and the great works of geography and history all took shape under their rule. The culture that later generations called Islamic was largely an Abbasid creation, even where its roots were older.

They also matter because their history exposes the gap between the ideal of the caliphate and the reality of politics. The Abbasids came to power promising to restore the Prophet’s family and to end kingly rule. Within a century they had built a court of immense wealth, a bureaucracy staffed by secretaries and a monarchy that resembled the empires they had replaced. The tension between the promise and the practice is the theme of their history.

Finally, the Abbasids matter for the history of knowledge. The translation movement, the paper revolution and the growth of libraries and madrasas created an intellectual infrastructure that outlasted the dynasty. The works produced in Baghdad, Kufa, Basra, Nishapur and Cairo were studied in Europe, Africa and Asia for centuries.

2. The Revolution of 750

The Abbasid revolution began in Khurasan, a province that had been a source of discontent for the Umayyads. Abu Muslim, a mysterious and gifted organiser, mobilised the local Arabs and Iranians with black banners and a message of justice. The movement claimed to restore the rights of the Prophet’s family, a slogan broad enough to attract Alids, mawali and provincial elites who wanted a change.

The Umayyad army was defeated at the Battle of the Zab in 750, and Marwan II was killed in Egypt. The new caliph, Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah, ordered the extermination of the Umayyad family. One prince, Abd al-Rahman ibn Mu‘awiya, escaped across North Africa to Iberia, where he founded a rival Umayyad state. The Abbasids moved the capital from Damascus to Iraq, first to Kufa and then to a new city.

The revolution did not deliver what all its supporters expected. The Alids, who had hoped for a caliph from their line, were sidelined. Abu Muslim, who had made the revolution possible, was killed by the second Abbasid caliph, al-Mansur. The new dynasty was more centralised and more bureaucratic than the old one, and it claimed a religious authority that the Umayyads had never asserted so explicitly.

Source caution. The revolution is reported in al-Tabari and in the Abbasid court histories, which were written to legitimise the new dynasty. The promises made to the Alids and the role of Abu Muslim are debated.

3. Baghdad and the Round City

Al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762 on the west bank of the Tigris, at a point where the river and the canals connected the Fertile Crescent with the Gulf and the Iranian plateau. The city was laid out as a circle, with the caliph’s palace and the great mosque at the centre, the administrative quarters around them and the residential districts outside. The design expressed a cosmology of power: the ruler at the centre, the world arranged around him.

Baghdad grew rapidly. Within a generation it was a metropolis of markets, workshops, libraries and immigrant communities. It attracted merchants from China, India, Central Asia, Byzantium and Africa, and it became the hub of a commercial network that stretched across the hemisphere. The city’s population is impossible to establish precisely, but it was certainly one of the largest in the world.

The Round City itself did not survive as a single unit; the city expanded into suburbs on both banks of the Tigris. But the idea of Baghdad as the centre of the world persisted in geography, literature and political imagination. It is the city of the Thousand and One Nights, of the House of Wisdom and of the caliphs who could summon the scholars of the age.

Source caution. The foundation narratives are preserved in al-Tabari and later geographers. The archaeological evidence is limited because the city has been continuously inhabited and rebuilt.

4. The Barmakids and the Court

The early Abbasid state was run by a small number of powerful families, and the most famous were the Barmakids, a Persian family of Buddhist origin that had converted to Islam. Khalid ibn Barmak and his sons served as viziers, governors and tutors to princes. They accumulated enormous wealth and influence, and they became the model of the courtier-administrator in Arabic literature.

The family’s fall in 803 is one of the great mysteries of Abbasid history. Harun al-Rashid ordered the execution of Ja‘far ibn Yahya and the imprisonment of his father and brothers. The sources offer several explanations: the family’s excessive power, their sympathy for the Alids, a romantic scandal involving Harun’s sister, or a combination of these. The truth is probably political: the Barmakids had become too powerful to be safe.

Source caution. The causes of the Barmakid fall are disputed. The romantic story of Ja‘far and the caliph’s sister is a literary embellishment and should not be treated as established fact.

5. Harun al-Rashid and the Image of the Golden Age

Harun al-Rashid ruled from 786 to 809 and became the most famous caliph in the world. His court was wealthy, his armies powerful and his empire vast. He corresponded with Charlemagne, exchanged gifts with Byzantium and patronised poets, jurists and musicians. The image of Harun in later literature is a symbol of the splendour of the Abbasid age.

The reality was more complicated. Harun’s reign ended in a civil war between his sons al-Amin and al-Ma’mun, a conflict that devastated Baghdad and weakened the dynasty. He also presided over a court whose luxury was condemned by pious critics, and his treatment of the Alids and the Barmakids was ruthless. The golden age was golden for some and dangerous for others.

Source caution. The Thousand and One Nights is a later literary compilation with many layers. It is not a source for Abbasid history, and its depiction of Harun is fictional.

6. The Translation Movement

The Abbasid era produced the largest translation project in pre-modern history. Greek works of philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy were translated into Arabic, along with Persian and Indian texts. The movement was not confined to one institution or one reign; it was a broad, century-long effort sponsored by caliphs, viziers, courtiers and scholars.

Hunayn ibn Ishaq, a Christian physician and translator of the ninth century, is the most famous figure of the movement. His translations of Galen and Hippocrates became standard texts in both the Islamic world and, later, in Latin Europe. The mathematicians of Baghdad absorbed Indian numerals and Greek geometry, and the result was the algebra of al-Khwarizmi and the trigonometry of later scholars.

The “House of Wisdom” is often described as a research academy founded by al-Ma’mun. Modern scholarship suggests that it was more likely a palace library and translation bureau, and that the story of a single institution of higher learning is a later simplification. The translation movement was real; the bureaucratic details are not.

Source caution. The nature of the Bayt al-Hikma is debated. Dimitri Gutas and other scholars have argued that it was a library rather than a research academy. The article follows that more cautious reading.

7. Paper, Commerce and the Urban Economy

Paper reached the Islamic world from China, probably through Central Asia after the Battle of Talas in 751. By the end of the eighth century there were paper mills in Baghdad, and by the tenth century paper had largely replaced papyrus and parchment for administrative and scholarly use. The change was as important as the printing press would later be in Europe: it made books cheaper and knowledge more portable.

The Abbasid economy was commercial and urban. Basra and Baghdad were connected to the Indian Ocean trade, the Silk Road and the Mediterranean. Merchants used credit, letters of exchange and partnerships to conduct business across vast distances. The word “cheque” derives from the Arabic sakk, and the commercial vocabulary of the period survives in many languages.

The wealth of the cities coexisted with poverty, slavery and inequality. The Abbasid economy depended on enslaved labour in agriculture, domestic service and the army, and the Zanj rebellion of the ninth century was a massive revolt of enslaved East Africans in southern Iraq. The golden age was built on a social order that was not golden for everyone.

Source caution. The scale of the Abbasid economy is difficult to quantify. The article describes the structures and the direction of trade rather than offering precise figures.

8. The Mihna and the Limits of Caliphal Power

The mihna was an inquisition instituted by al-Ma’mun in 833. The caliph adopted the theological position that the Qur’an was created rather than uncreated, and he required judges and scholars to affirm it. Those who refused were dismissed, imprisoned or flogged. The most famous resister was Ahmad ibn Hanbal, the founder of the Hanbali school, who was beaten and imprisoned for his refusal.

The mihna was not primarily a philosophical dispute. It was a test of whether the caliph had the authority to define doctrine and to impose his definition on the scholars. The scholars resisted, and the resistance established a principle that became central to Sunni Islam: the caliph may enforce the law, but he does not define creed.

The policy was reversed by al-Mutawakkil in the 840s, and the episode became a cautionary tale about the dangers of state interference in theology. It also shaped the relationship between rulers and scholars for centuries. The ulama emerged from the mihna with greater moral authority than the caliph, and that authority would outlast the dynasty.

Source caution. The mihna is reported in al-Tabari and in the biographical works on Ibn Hanbal. The theological positions involved are complex, and the article simplifies them deliberately.

9. Fragmentation: Samanids, Buyids and Fatimids

The Abbasid empire began to fragment in the ninth century. The Tahirids in Khurasan, the Samanids in Transoxiana, the Tulunids in Egypt and the Hamdanids in Syria became effectively independent. The caliph retained symbolic authority and the revenues of Iraq, but he could no longer command the provinces.

In 945 the Buyids, a Shi‘i dynasty from the Caspian region, took Baghdad and placed the caliph under their protection. They kept him as a figurehead and ruled in his name. In 969 the Fatimids, an Isma‘ili dynasty based in Tunisia, conquered Egypt and founded Cairo as a rival caliphate. For a century there were three caliphs: the Abbasid in Baghdad, the Fatimid in Cairo and the Umayyad in Córdoba.

The fragmentation was not simply a political failure. It produced a remarkable cultural flourishing in the regional courts. The Samanids patronised Persian literature, the Buyids supported philosophy and the Fatimids built Cairo and patronised the sciences. The Abbasid caliphate lost power, but the Islamic world gained a plurality of centres.

Source caution. The term “caliphate” in this period covers very different institutions. The article uses it for the office and distinguishes the rival claims where necessary.

10. The Seljuks, the Nizamiyya and the Sunni Revival

In 1055 the Seljuk Turks entered Baghdad and ended Buyid rule. The Seljuk sultan Tughril Beg was a Sunni Muslim who presented himself as the protector of the caliph. The relationship was unequal: the sultan held the army and the caliph held the legitimacy. The arrangement became the model for later Turkish and Persian states.

The Seljuks patronised the madrasa, a new institution of higher learning that spread across the Islamic world. The most famous was the Nizamiyya in Baghdad, founded by the vizier Nizam al-Mulk in 1065. Its teachers included al-Ghazali, whose works on theology, law and Sufism became foundational for Sunni Islam.

The Sunni revival was not only an intellectual movement. It was also a political programme: the Seljuks and their successors supported Sunni scholarship, built madrasas and suppressed Isma‘ili and Shi‘i movements where they could. The result was a more institutionalised and more uniform Sunni orthodoxy than had existed before.

Source caution. The history of the madrasa is complex, and the Nizamiyya was not the first institution of its kind. The article presents it as the most famous example of a broader movement.

11. The Late Caliphate and the Sack of Baghdad

For two centuries after the Seljuk conquest, the Abbasid caliph was a symbolic figure. He conferred legitimacy on sultans, appointed judges and presided over court ceremonies. The caliphate had become an institution of religious authority without political power, and its survival depended on the goodwill of the military rulers around it.

In 1258 the Mongol army of Hulagu Khan besieged and sacked Baghdad. The caliph al-Musta‘sim was killed, the city was plundered and burned, and the population was massacred. The sack is one of the most traumatic events in Islamic history, and it is often described as the end of the Abbasid golden age.

The dynasty did not disappear entirely. A surviving Abbasid prince was installed as a figurehead caliph in Cairo under the Mamluks, and the line continued until the Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517. But the caliphate of Baghdad, the centre of the classical Islamic world, was gone. The memory of 1258 shaped later Muslim perceptions of the Mongols and of the fragility of civilisation.

Source caution. The figures for the dead in 1258 are unreliable and vary wildly in the sources. The destruction was real; the numbers are not.

12. What the “Golden Age” Obscures

The phrase “golden age” is useful as a shorthand and misleading as a description. The Abbasid period produced extraordinary achievements, but it was also a time of civil war, sectarian violence, slavery, famine and epidemic. The wealth of Baghdad was built on a social order that excluded most of its inhabitants from its benefits.

The golden age was also not confined to the Abbasids. The Umayyads of Córdoba, the Fatimids of Cairo, the Samanids of Bukhara and the Ghaznavids of Afghanistan all contributed to the same civilisation. The achievements of the period were the product of a network of courts, markets and scholars, not of a single dynasty.

Remembering the Abbasids honestly means holding both truths: they presided over one of the most creative periods in human history, and they presided over a state that was often unjust and eventually collapsed. The two are not contradictory. They are the normal condition of empires.

Editorial note: this article uses “golden age” as a conventional label while insisting that the period was not uniformly prosperous or peaceful.

13. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

The Abbasid experience teaches three lessons. The first is that knowledge requires institutions: libraries, paper, patronage and a commercial economy made the translation movement possible. The second is that political power and religious authority are different things, and confusing them is dangerous, as the mihna showed. The third is that fragmentation is not the same as decline: the regional courts of the tenth and eleventh centuries produced some of the finest works of Islamic civilisation.

The limits of the sources are considerable. The Abbasid court histories were written by officials and clients of the dynasty, and they magnify its achievements and justify its decisions. The biographical dictionaries preserve the scholars’ perspective, which is often critical of the rulers. Archaeology and material culture provide a third perspective, but Baghdad’s continuous habitation has destroyed much of the evidence.

The best way to remember the Abbasids is to read their scholars, not only their caliphs. The mathematicians, physicians, jurists, poets and historians of the period created works that outlived the dynasty and shaped the world. Their achievement is more durable than the empire that sponsored them.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the court histories, the biographical tradition, the material evidence and modern scholarship. It does not treat the “golden age” as a period without conflict.

14. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. Who founded Baghdad?

The Abbasid caliph al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762 on the Tigris. It was laid out as a round city with the caliph’s palace and the great mosque at the centre, and it became the administrative and commercial capital of the empire.

2. What was the translation movement?

It was a century-long effort to translate Greek, Persian and Indian works of philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy into Arabic. It was sponsored by caliphs, viziers and scholars, and it produced the foundations of classical Islamic science.

3. What was the mihna?

The mihna was an inquisition instituted by al-Ma’mun in 833 over the createdness of the Qur’an. Scholars who refused to affirm the caliph’s position were punished. Ahmad ibn Hanbal resisted, and the episode established the principle that the caliph does not define creed.

4. When did the Abbasid caliphate end?

The Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258 and killed the caliph al-Musta‘sim. A figurehead Abbasid caliphate continued in Cairo under the Mamluks until 1517, but the caliphate of Baghdad never recovered.

5. Was the Abbasid period a golden age?

It was a period of extraordinary achievement in science, philosophy, literature and administration, but it was also marked by civil war, sectarian violence, slavery and political fragmentation. The label is useful as shorthand and misleading as a full description.

  • Ibn Khaldun, al-Muqaddima — the theory of dynasties and the analysis of urban civilisation.
  • Dimitri Gutas, Greek Thought, Arabic Culture; Jonathan Berkey, The Formation of Islam; Hugh Kennedy, When Baghdad Ruled the Muslim World — modern critical accounts.
  • Michael Cooperson, Classical Arabic Biography — the literary construction of the caliphs and scholars.
