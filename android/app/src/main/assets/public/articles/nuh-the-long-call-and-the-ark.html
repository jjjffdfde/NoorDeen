Nuh: The Long Call, the Ark and the Flood

NoorDeen Editorial · 14 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Nuh is the prophet of patience in the Qur’an. He calls his people for what the text describes as a thousand years minus fifty, is mocked, is rejected by the powerful, and is finally commanded to build a vessel on dry land. The flood that follows is one of the Qur’an’s great scenes of judgement, and the survival of the believers in the ark is one of its great scenes of mercy.

The story is also a test of honesty. Popular retellings add a global flood, a specific mountain, the exact size of the ark and a chronology of centuries. Some of those details are later tradition; some are modern speculation dressed as archaeology. This article follows the Qur’an, uses the authentic hadith sparingly, and says clearly where the evidence stops.

Key takeaways
  • Nuh is described in the Qur’an as remaining among his people “a thousand years minus fifty” (29:14).
  • He called openly and secretly, by night and day, and was rejected by the chiefs while the weak believed.
  • The ark was built by divine command and under divine observation, and Nuh was mocked while building it.
  • The flood destroyed the deniers; the Qur’an does not specify its geographical extent.
  • Nuh’s son refused to board and was drowned; the Qur’an denies that family ties save a person who rejects faith.
  • Nuh’s wife is cited in 66:10 as an example of a believer’s relative who disbelieved.
  • Ark-hunting claims, the “Durupinar” formation and the Black Sea flood hypothesis are not evidence for the Qur’anic narrative.

1. Why Nuh Matters

Nuh is one of the five prophets described in the hadith as “the messengers of strong will” (ulu al-‘azm), together with Ibrahim, Musa, Isa and Muhammad ﷺ. The Qur’an mentions him in more than forty places and devotes a whole surah to his preaching (Surah Nuh, 71). He is the first messenger sent to a people who had turned to idolatry, and his story sets the pattern for every later confrontation between a prophet and a hostile community.

For Muslims, Nuh is also a figure of warning and hope at once. The warning is that a community can become so settled in its error that it mistakes the prophet for a fool. The hope is that a small, weak, mocked group can be saved while the powerful are not. The story is retold in the Qur’an precisely because those dynamics recur.

The narrative is spread across Surahs Hud (11:25–49), al-Mu’minun (23:23–30), al-A‘raf (7:59–64), al-Shu‘ara’ (26:105–122), al-Qamar (54:9–17), al-Anbiya’ (21:76–77) and Nuh (71:1–28). Each passage selects different scenes: the preaching, the ark, the son, the flood, the covenant.

2. The Qur’anic Dossier

The Qur’an presents Nuh as a messenger sent to his own people. “We sent Nuh to his people, [saying], ‘Warn your people before there comes to them a painful punishment’” (71:1). His message is the message of every prophet: worship Allah alone, fear Him and obey the messenger (71:3; 23:23). The Qur’an does not give the name of his town, the number of his followers or the date of the flood.

The narrative is remarkably consistent across the surahs. The people reject him, the chiefs accuse him of being a mere man who wants superiority, and they demand signs. Nuh refuses to drive away the poor believers to please the elite. He insists that he asks no wage. The flood comes by divine command, the believers are saved, and the deniers are destroyed.

The Qur’an also uses Nuh as a historical reference point. “The people of Nuh denied before them” (51:46), and the destruction of earlier nations is cited as evidence that God’s judgement is real (9:70; 14:9; 22:42; 25:37). The story is thus both narrative and argument: it tells what happened and it draws the lesson for the present.

3. The Call: Day and Night, Secret and Public

Surah Nuh preserves the content of the preaching with unusual intimacy. Nuh says: “My Lord, indeed I invited my people [to truth] night and day. But my invitation increased them not except in flight. And indeed, every time I invited them that You may forgive them, they put their fingers in their ears, covered themselves with their garments, persisted, and were arrogant with [great] arrogance” (71:5–7).

He then appeals to their self-interest without reducing the message to it: “Ask forgiveness of your Lord. Indeed, He is ever a Perpetual Forgiver. He will send [rain from] the sky upon you in [continuing] showers and give you increase in wealth and children and provide for you gardens and rivers” (71:10–12). The Qur’an presents repentance as the door to both spiritual and material well-being, not as a transaction but as a consequence of restored order.

The call is also theological. Nuh asks, “What is [the matter] with you that you do not attribute to Allah [due] grandeur, while He has created you in stages?” (71:13–14). He points to the seven heavens, the moon as a light, the sun as a lamp, and the earth as a spread carpet with wide paths (71:15–20). The argument is from creation to Creator, the same argument the Qur’an uses throughout.

4. The Response: Mockery, Elites and the Poor

The chiefs of the people answer with a familiar combination of social contempt and theological denial: “This is not but a man like you, desiring to make himself superior to you” (23:24); “we see you in clear error” (7:60); “we do not see you but as a man like ourselves, and we do not see you followed except by those who are the lowest of us” (11:27). The objection is not really about evidence. It is about status.

Nuh’s reply is one of the most important passages in the Qur’an on the social character of prophethood: “And I do not say to you that I have the treasures of Allah, nor do I know the unseen, nor do I say that I am an angel, nor do I say of those whom your eyes look down upon that Allah will not give them any good. Allah is most knowing of what is within their souls” (11:31). He refuses to trade the poor for the powerful.

The Qur’an also records the demand for a sign and the offer of a compromise: “They said, ‘O Nuh, you have disputed with us and increased our dispute. Bring us what you threaten us with, if you are of the truthful’” (11:32). Nuh answers that only Allah can bring it, and that no advice of his will benefit them if Allah intends to leave them in error (11:34). The dialogue ends not with a debate victory but with a judgement.

5. The 950 Years and the Problem of Chronology

The Qur’an says: “And We certainly sent Nuh to his people, and he remained among them a thousand years minus fifty years” (29:14). The number is stated in the text, and the classical commentators took it literally. Some modern readers have suggested that the “year” here is not a solar year, or that the figure is symbolic of endurance rather than a precise chronology. Neither suggestion is required by the text, and neither is established.

What the number does establish is the scale of the patience being described. The Qur’an is not offering a date for the flood; it is telling the reader that the call lasted an extraordinarily long time and that the rejection was not a momentary misunderstanding. The rhetorical force of the figure is at least as important as its arithmetic.

The verse also raises a question the Qur’an does not answer: how could a single preacher address one people for so long? The text does not describe generations, successors or the structure of the community. It compresses the period into a single continuous mission, and the reader is meant to feel the weight of it rather than to reconstruct a timeline.

6. The Ark: Command, Construction and Mockery

The ark is built by revelation, not by human engineering alone: “And construct the ship under Our observation and Our inspiration, and do not address Me concerning those who have wronged — indeed, they are to be drowned” (11:37). The same command appears in 23:27. The Qur’an does not describe the wood, the dimensions, the number of decks or the materials. Those details come from later tradition and from the Bible.

While he builds, his people mock him: “And he was building the ship, and every time a company of his people passed by him, they ridiculed him. He said, ‘If you ridicule us, then we will ridicule you just as you ridicule’” (11:38). The scene is a study in the loneliness of obedience. The man is doing something that looks absurd to everyone around him, and he is doing it because he was commanded.

The ark is also described as carrying “a pair of every kind” (11:40) and the believers, and the Qur’an says that Nuh was commanded to take his family except those against whom the word had already passed (11:40). The emphasis is on salvation by divine instruction rather than on the vessel as a technical achievement.

7. The Flood: Text, Scope and the Question of Universality

The flood begins when “Our command came and the oven overflowed” (11:40), an image that has puzzled commentators. Water comes from the sky and bursts from the earth: “So We opened the gates of the heaven with rain pouring down and caused the earth to burst with springs, and the waters met for a matter already predestined” (54:11–12). The ark sails “amid waves like mountains” (11:42).

The Qur’an describes the destruction of “the people of Nuh” and says that “they were drowned because of their sins” (71:25). It does not say that every human being on earth was drowned. The classical commentators often assumed a universal flood, and the Bible describes one, but the Qur’anic text is not explicit about geography or extent. The theological point is the judgement of a rejecting people, not a census of the planet.

The ark settles on “al-Judi” (11:44), a name the Qur’an gives without locating it. Later tradition places it in northern Mesopotamia or on a mountain in the region of Ararat. The Qur’an does not. The verse that follows — “And it was said, ‘Away with the wrongdoing people’” (11:44) — closes the scene with a verdict rather than a map.

8. The Son, the Wife and the Limits of Lineage

One of the most painful scenes in the Qur’an is the son who refuses to board. Nuh calls to him, “O my son, come aboard with us and be not with the disbelievers.” He replies, “I will take refuge on a mountain to protect me from the water.” Nuh says, “There is no protector today from the decree of Allah, except for whom He gives mercy.” And the wave comes between them, and he is among the drowned (11:42–43).

Then Nuh calls to his Lord: “My Lord, indeed my son is of my family, and indeed Your promise is true, and You are the most just of judges.” The answer is one of the most important verses in the Qur’an about belonging: “O Nuh, indeed he is not of your family; indeed, he is [one whose] work was other than righteous, so do not ask Me about that of which you have no knowledge” (11:45–46). Nuh repents of the question and seeks forgiveness (11:47).

The Qur’an reinforces the point with the wives of Nuh and Lut: “They were under two of Our righteous servants but they betrayed them, so they [the husbands] availed them nothing against Allah” (66:10). The verse is a warning to the believers that a relationship with a prophet is not a substitute for faith. It is also a correction of the idea that lineage, tribe or family name can secure salvation.

9. The Covenant and the “Second Adam”

After the flood, the Qur’an speaks of blessing and a new beginning: “It was said, ‘O Nuh, descend with peace from Us and blessings upon you and upon nations [to be created] from those with you’” (11:48). The verse is the Qur’anic equivalent of a new humanity, and it is why later writers called Nuh the second father of humanity.

That title is a later epithet, not a Qur’anic term. The Qur’an does say that Allah chose Nuh: “Indeed, Allah chose Adam and Nuh and the family of Ibrahim and the family of Imran over the worlds” (3:33). It also lists Nuh among the prophets with whom Allah made a covenant: “And [mention, O Muhammad], when We took from the prophets their covenant and from you and from Nuh and Ibrahim and Musa and Isa, son of Maryam” (33:7).

The covenant is the framework of the whole prophetic story. Nuh is not an isolated wonder; he is one link in a chain that runs from Adam through Ibrahim, Musa and Isa to Muhammad ﷺ. The Qur’an uses the earlier prophets to establish the continuity of the message and the unity of the Messenger who sent them.

10. Nuh in Hadith and the Intercession Narrative

The most important hadith reference to Nuh is the long report of intercession. On the Day of Resurrection, people go to Adam, then to Nuh, saying, “O Nuh, you are the first messenger to the people of the earth, and Allah called you a grateful servant” (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). The report identifies Nuh as the first messenger sent to a people, which is consistent with Adam being a prophet within his own household.

The Qur’an itself calls Nuh “a grateful servant” (17:3), and the hadith picks up the phrase. The report is also a reminder that the prophets are not worshipped; they are intercessors by God’s permission, and the chain of intercession ends with the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ.

The hadith also mentions that Nuh fasted often, and some reports describe him as one who never ate his fill. These reports are not at the highest level of authenticity, and they are not needed to establish his status. The Qur’an’s portrait of patient, persistent preaching is sufficient.

11. Archaeology, Geology and the Limits of Claim

The search for the ark has produced a large literature and a larger industry. Claims have been made for Mount Ararat in Turkey, for the “Durupinar” formation near Dogubayazit, for Mount Judi in southeastern Turkey and for sites in Iran. None has produced a vessel that can be dated, excavated and identified as the ark. The formations that are photographed are geological structures, and the photographs circulated online are often presented without the excavation reports that would test the claim.

The “Black Sea flood hypothesis” is a scientific proposal about a rapid rise in the Black Sea around 5600 BCE. It is debated among geologists, and even if it were correct it would not establish the Qur’anic narrative. A natural event and a prophetic judgement are not the same kind of claim, and the Qur’an does not invite the reader to date the flood.

This matters for honesty. The Qur’an does not need archaeological confirmation, and faith is not strengthened by a false report. When a Muslim repeats an unverified ark claim as fact, he puts the credibility of the narrative at the mercy of the next expedition. The better position is the one the Qur’an itself takes: the story is true, the lesson is clear, and the physical details are not the point.

Source caution: the Qur’an does not specify the size of the ark, the number of animals, the extent of the flood or the modern location of al-Judi. Claims to have found the ark are not established.

12. Sources and Their Limits

The main sources for this article are:

  • The Qur’an — especially 11:25–49, 23:23–30, 71:1–28, 7:59–64, 26:105–122, 54:9–17, 29:14, 3:33 and 33:7.
  • Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim — the intercession narrative and the identification of Nuh as the first messenger.
  • Al-Tabari, Jami‘ al-Bayan — the classical commentary and its isnad discussions.
  • Ibn Kathir, Tafsir and al-Bidayah wa al-Nihayah — the later synthesis with grading.
  • Al-Qurtubi, al-Jami‘ li Ahkam al-Qur’an — the legal and theological discussion.
  • Modern studies of the ancient Near East and of flood traditions — used only for context, not as proof of the Qur’anic account.

The Qur’anic narrative is consistent across its passages. The later literature adds the dimensions of the ark, the names of the sons, the number of believers, the exact mountain and the date. Those additions are not established and should not be preached as revelation.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Qur’anic text, the authentic hadith and later narrative. It does not endorse ark-hunting claims or treat scientific hypotheses as confirmation of revelation.

13. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. How long did Nuh preach?

The Qur’an says he remained among his people “a thousand years minus fifty years” (29:14), which is 950 years. The classical commentators took the figure literally. The text does not explain how the period was counted, and the number’s main force is to convey the scale of his patience.

2. Was the flood global?

The Qur’an does not say. It describes the destruction of the people of Nuh and the salvation of the believers in the ark, but it does not specify the geographical extent. Many classical commentators assumed a universal flood, but the text is not explicit, and the theological point is the judgement of a rejecting people.

3. Did Nuh’s son drown?

Yes. The Qur’an describes Nuh calling his son to board, the son refusing and seeking refuge on a mountain, and the wave coming between them (11:42–43). The Qur’an then says that the son was “not of your family” in the sense of faith (11:46).

4. Where did the ark land?

The Qur’an names “al-Judi” (11:44) without locating it. Later tradition places it in northern Mesopotamia or near Ararat. The exact site is not established, and claims to have found the ark are unverified.

5. Was Nuh the first prophet?

Adam was the first prophet, but Nuh was the first messenger sent to a disobedient people. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have called Nuh “the first messenger to the people of the earth” in the intercession narrative (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim).
