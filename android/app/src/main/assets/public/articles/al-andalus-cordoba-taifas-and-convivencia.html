Al-Andalus: Córdoba, the Taifas and the Memory of Convivencia

NoorDeen Editorial · 15 min read · Reviewed against the sources listed below

Al-Andalus was the Muslim presence in the Iberian peninsula that lasted from 711 to 1492, and for much of that time it was the most sophisticated society in western Europe. Córdoba under the Umayyad caliphs was a city of libraries, gardens, mosques and scholars, connected to the wider Islamic world and to Latin Christendom. Its legacy is claimed by Muslims, Spaniards, Jews and Europeans, often for contradictory purposes.

The story is not a simple romance. Al-Andalus was conquered by force, governed by a Muslim minority, divided by ethnic and tribal rivalries and eventually destroyed by Christian conquest and the expulsion of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. The idea of convivencia — the peaceful coexistence of Muslims, Christians and Jews — captures something real about the period, but it has been romanticised and, in recent decades, sharply criticised.

This article follows al-Andalus from the conquest to 1492 and beyond, and it treats the memory of the period as part of its history. It does not claim that medieval Iberia was a paradise of tolerance, and it does not accept the counter-claim that it was a uniform tyranny. The evidence supports a more interesting and more human picture.

Key takeaways
  • Muslim armies crossed into Iberia in 711 under Tariq ibn Ziyad and defeated the Visigothic kingdom within a few years.
  • Abd al-Rahman I founded the Umayyad emirate of Córdoba in 756 after escaping the Abbasid revolution.
  • Abd al-Rahman III declared himself caliph in 929 and built Madinat al-Zahra as a new palace-city.
  • Córdoba became a centre of scholarship, with libraries, poets, physicians, philosophers and jurists.
  • The caliphate collapsed in 1031 and was replaced by the taifa kingdoms, which fought each other and paid tribute to Christian rulers.
  • The Almoravids (1086) and Almohads (1147) imposed stricter rule and persecuted religious minorities.
  • The Reconquista reduced Muslim rule to Granada, which fell in 1492; the Jews were expelled the same year and the Muslims were forced to convert or leave.
  • The idea of convivencia is debated: it describes real cultural exchange but obscures inequality, violence and the dhimma system.

1. Why Al-Andalus Matters

Al-Andalus matters because it is the place where Islamic civilisation met Latin Christendom most directly. The translation of Arabic philosophy and science into Latin, the transmission of Greek learning through Arabic, the poetry of Ibn Zaydun and al-Mu‘tamid, the philosophy of Ibn Rushd and the law of Ibn Hazm all belong to its history. Without al-Andalus, the intellectual history of medieval Europe would be different.

It also matters because it is a test case for how Muslims, Christians and Jews can live together. The dhimma system gave non-Muslims a protected but subordinate status. It was not equality, and it was not genocide. It was a hierarchical arrangement that worked for long periods and broke down in others. The modern debate about convivencia is really a debate about what that arrangement meant.

Finally, al-Andalus matters because its memory is contested. Spanish nationalism, Arab nationalism, Jewish memory, Islamic revivalism and European romanticism have all claimed it. The historian’s task is to recover the period’s complexity against the simplifications of all these claims.

2. Conquest and the Making of a Province

In 711 Tariq ibn Ziyad, a Berber commander serving the Umayyad governor of North Africa, crossed the strait and defeated the Visigothic king Roderic at the Battle of Guadalete. The Visigothic kingdom was already weakened by dynastic conflict, and the Muslim armies exploited the divisions. Within a few years most of the peninsula was under Muslim control.

The conquest was not a single campaign. Tariq and Musa ibn Nusayr, the governor, operated with separate armies and sometimes in competition. Local lords negotiated treaties that allowed them to keep their lands and religion in return for tribute. Some cities resisted and were taken by force; others capitulated. The result was a patchwork of arrangements rather than a uniform conquest.

Al-Andalus became a province of the Umayyad caliphate, dependent on Damascus and then on Qayrawan. The Muslim settlers were a minority, divided between Arabs and Berbers, and their rivalries shaped the politics of the province. The Christian population remained large, and the Jewish communities continued to exist under the dhimma system.

Source caution. The conquest is reported in Ibn Abd al-Hakam and later Arabic sources, and in Latin chronicles written centuries later. The numbers and the details of the battles are unreliable; the broad outline is not.

3. The Emirate of Córdoba

In 756 Abd al-Rahman I, the last surviving Umayyad prince, landed in Iberia and made himself emir of Córdoba. He had escaped the Abbasid revolution and spent years in North Africa before crossing the strait. His arrival created a Umayyad state independent of Baghdad, and it gave al-Andalus a dynasty that would last for nearly three centuries.

Abd al-Rahman built the Great Mosque of Córdoba, one of the masterpieces of Islamic architecture, and he fortified the city. His successors expanded the mosque, developed the irrigation system and patronised poets and scholars. The emirate was not peaceful: it faced rebellions by Berbers, Arabs and converts, and the frontier with the Christian kingdoms was a zone of raids and negotiations.

The emirate’s independence was cultural as well as political. Córdoba developed its own legal and literary traditions, and its scholars travelled to the East to study and returned with books and ideas. The city became a bridge between the Islamic East and the Latin West.

Source caution. The early emirate is known from Arabic chronicles and from the archaeology of Córdoba. The literary anecdotes about the emirs are later compositions and should not be read as verbatim records.

4. The Caliphate of Córdoba

In 929 Abd al-Rahman III declared himself caliph, breaking the last symbolic tie to Baghdad. The declaration was a response to the Fatimid caliphate in North Africa, which claimed the same title and threatened the Umayyad sphere. It was also a statement of confidence: al-Andalus was rich, powerful and culturally self-sufficient.

Abd al-Rahman III built Madinat al-Zahra, a palace-city outside Córdoba, and made it the centre of a court of extraordinary splendour. He received ambassadors from Byzantium, the Holy Roman Empire and the Fatimids. His armies campaigned against the Christian kingdoms of the north and against the Fatimids in North Africa. His reign is often considered the high point of Muslim rule in Iberia.

His son al-Hakam II continued the patronage of learning and expanded the library of Córdoba. The caliphate lasted until 1031, when a series of civil wars and succession disputes destroyed it. The collapse was sudden and complete: the greatest state in western Europe fragmented into a mosaic of small kingdoms.

Source caution. The size of the Córdoba library is often exaggerated in later sources. The article describes its importance without repeating unverifiable figures.

5. Scholars, Libraries and the Transmission of Knowledge

Al-Andalus produced scholars of the first rank. Ibn Hazm (994–1064) was a jurist, theologian, logician and poet, famous for his literalist approach to law and his treatise on love. Ibn Rushd (1126–1198) was a philosopher, physician and jurist whose commentaries on Aristotle shaped Latin scholasticism. Ibn Arabi (1165–1240), though born in Murcia and buried in Damascus, belongs to the same Andalusian tradition of mystical thought.

The Jewish communities of al-Andalus produced figures such as Maimonides (1138–1204), the philosopher and physician who left Córdoba as a young man and eventually settled in Egypt. His work was written in Arabic and Hebrew and became central to Jewish philosophy. The presence of Jewish and Christian scholars in the same intellectual world is one of the strongest arguments for the reality of convivencia.

The transmission of knowledge to Latin Europe happened largely through translation. Toledo, conquered by Castile in 1085, became a centre where Arabic texts were translated into Latin, often with the help of Jewish and Muslim collaborators. The works of Ibn Rushd, Ibn Sina, al-Khwarizmi and others entered the European universities through this route.

Source caution. The translation movement in Toledo is well documented, but the popular image of a single “school of translators” oversimplifies a long and decentralised process.

6. Convivencia: The Debate

The term convivencia was popularised by the Spanish philologist Américo Castro and later by María Rosa Menocal, whose book The Ornament of the World presented al-Andalus as a model of tolerance and cultural exchange. The image is attractive, and it has been used to promote interfaith dialogue and to criticise modern intolerance.

Critics have argued that the image is romanticised. The dhimma system was hierarchical: non-Muslims paid a special tax, could not bear arms, had to distinguish themselves in public and were excluded from the highest offices. There were periods of persecution, including the Almohad forced conversions and the massacre of the Jews of Granada in 1066. Coexistence was real, but it was not equality.

The scholarly consensus today is more nuanced than either the romantic or the hostile account. Al-Andalus was a society of cultural exchange, translation and interdependence, and it was also a society of inequality, religious hierarchy and violence. Both facts are true, and the historian’s task is to hold them together.

Source caution. The debate over convivencia is a modern historiographical controversy. The article presents the main positions without adopting a political agenda.

7. The Taifa Kingdoms and the Fragmentation

After the collapse of the caliphate in 1031, al-Andalus fragmented into more than twenty small kingdoms, known as the taifas. The most important were Seville, Toledo, Zaragoza, Badajoz, Granada and Valencia. They competed with each other, patronised poets and scholars and paid tribute to the Christian kingdoms of the north.

The taifas were culturally brilliant and politically weak. The courts of Seville and Zaragoza produced some of the finest poetry and philosophy of the period, and the rulers competed to attract scholars. But their divisions made them vulnerable. In 1085 Alfonso VI of Castile took Toledo, and the taifa kings called for help from the Almoravids, a Berber dynasty from North Africa.

The Almoravids defeated Alfonso at the Battle of Sagrajas in 1086 and then annexed the taifas one by one. They were followed by the Almohads in the twelfth century. The period of the taifas is often seen as a golden age of Andalusian culture, but it was also a time of political fragmentation and foreign intervention.

Source caution. The taifa period is documented in Arabic chronicles and in the poetry of the courts. The political narrative is complicated by the shifting alliances of the period.

8. The Almoravids and the Almohads

The Almoravids were a Berber movement from the Sahara that had built an empire in North Africa. They entered Iberia in 1086 to defend the taifas against the Christian advance, and they soon took control of the peninsula. Their rule was stricter than that of the taifas, and they patronised a more austere form of Maliki law.

The Almohads, who replaced them in the twelfth century, were a more radical movement. They claimed to restore the pure monotheism of the early community and they persecuted those they considered heretics. The Jewish community suffered severely, and Maimonides’ family left Córdoba during this period. The Almohads also built the great mosque of Seville and the Giralda, one of the masterpieces of Andalusian architecture.

The Almohad period was not simply a time of intolerance. It was also a period of philosophical brilliance: Ibn Rushd served as a judge and physician under the Almohads, and his commentaries on Aristotle were written in their service. The relationship between political power and intellectual freedom was complicated, as it always is.

Source caution. The Almohad persecutions are reported in Jewish and Muslim sources. The article describes them without reducing the whole period to persecution.

9. The Reconquista and 1492

The Christian reconquest advanced slowly over centuries. Toledo fell in 1085, Zaragoza in 1118, Córdoba in 1236 and Seville in 1248. By the end of the thirteenth century only Granada remained, a small kingdom in the south that survived for two and a half centuries by paying tribute to Castile and navigating the rivalries between the Christian kingdoms.

Granada fell in January 1492. The treaty promised the Muslims their lives, property, religion and laws. Within a few years the promises were broken: the Muslim population was pressured to convert, and in 1502 Castile formally required conversion or exile. The Jews had been expelled in 1492 by the Alhambra Decree. The last Muslims, the Moriscos, were expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614.

The year 1492 is often treated as the end of convivencia. It was the end of a political order, and it was followed by the destruction of the religious diversity that had made al-Andalus distinctive. The memory of 1492 became central to both Spanish national identity and Muslim nostalgia.

Source caution. The Treaty of Granada and its violation are documented in Castilian and Arabic sources. The article follows the mainstream account of the expulsions.

10. The Architecture of Memory

The monuments of al-Andalus are among the most visited in the world. The Great Mosque of Córdoba, with its forest of columns and double arches, is one of the great buildings of Islamic architecture. The Alhambra in Granada, with its courtyards, muqarnas and inscriptions, is a masterpiece of Nasrid art. Madinat al-Zahra, the palace-city of Abd al-Rahman III, is an archaeological site that has been partially reconstructed.

These buildings are not neutral. They were built by a Muslim minority to express power, piety and cultural confidence, and they were later appropriated by Christian rulers who converted mosques into cathedrals. The Mosque of Córdoba became a cathedral in 1236, and its Christian additions are part of the building’s history.

The architecture of al-Andalus is also a reminder of the interconnectedness of Mediterranean culture. The horseshoe arch, the courtyard garden and the muqarnas are shared across Islamic, Christian and Jewish buildings. The visual language of the period is a language of exchange, even when the politics were violent.

Source caution. The interpretation of the Alhambra’s inscriptions and symbolism is a specialised field. The article describes the monuments without claiming to decode every inscription.

11. Al-Andalus in Modern Debate

Al-Andalus is a battleground of modern identities. Spanish nationalism has sometimes treated it as a foreign occupation and sometimes as a national heritage. Arab nationalism has claimed it as proof of the superiority of Islamic civilisation. Jewish memory has celebrated the Golden Age of Spain while also remembering the persecutions. European romanticism turned it into a fantasy of exotic sensuality.

The Islamic revivalist imagination often presents al-Andalus as a lost paradise and a warning about the dangers of division. The fall of Granada is invoked in sermons and poetry as a symbol of Muslim weakness and divine punishment. The historical record is more complicated: al-Andalus was lost through a combination of internal fragmentation, external pressure and the logic of frontier warfare.

The most honest way to remember al-Andalus is to resist both nostalgia and denigration. It was a society of extraordinary cultural achievement, built on a hierarchical and sometimes violent order, destroyed by conquest and expulsion. Its legacy belongs to everyone who studies it, and no one community owns it.

Editorial note: this article presents the modern uses of al-Andalus as part of its history and does not adopt a nationalist or romantic position.

12. Lessons and the Limits of the Sources

Al-Andalus teaches three lessons. The first is that cultural exchange does not require equality: some of the greatest achievements of the period were produced by a society that was deeply unequal. The second is that political fragmentation is dangerous: the taifas were culturally brilliant and militarily weak, and their divisions invited conquest. The third is that memory is selective: every community remembers the parts of al-Andalus that serve its present needs.

The limits of the sources are significant. The Arabic chronicles were written by courtiers and scholars, and they reflect the perspectives of the elite. The Latin and Hebrew sources provide a different view but are often polemical. Archaeology has transformed our understanding of daily life, agriculture and urbanism, but it cannot recover the inner lives of the people who lived there.

The best way to remember al-Andalus is to read its poetry, its philosophy and its law, and to walk its monuments with an awareness of who built them and who was excluded from them. The result is not a simple story, but it is a true one.

Editorial note: this article distinguishes the Arabic chronicles, the Latin and Hebrew sources, the material evidence and modern historiography. It does not present convivencia as either a paradise or a myth.

13. Common Questions (FAQ)

1. When did Muslims conquer Iberia?

Muslim armies crossed the strait in 711 under Tariq ibn Ziyad and defeated the Visigothic king Roderic. Within a few years most of the peninsula was under Muslim control, though the conquest was a patchwork of treaties and campaigns rather than a single event.

2. What was the caliphate of Córdoba?

It was the period from 929, when Abd al-Rahman III declared himself caliph, to 1031, when the caliphate collapsed. It was the political and cultural high point of Muslim rule in Iberia, with Córdoba as one of the great cities of the world.

3. What is convivencia?

Convivencia is the modern term for the coexistence of Muslims, Christians and Jews in medieval Iberia. It describes real cultural exchange and interdependence, but it should not be confused with equality: non-Muslims were subordinate under the dhimma system.

4. When did al-Andalus end?

The kingdom of Granada fell in January 1492. The treaty promised religious rights, but within years the Muslims were pressured to convert, and the Jews had been expelled in 1492. The last Muslims, the Moriscos, were expelled between 1609 and 1614.

5. Was al-Andalus a golden age of tolerance?

It was a period of remarkable cultural achievement and real coexistence, but it was also hierarchical and sometimes violent. The Almohads persecuted minorities, and the dhimma system denied non-Muslims equality. The honest answer is more complicated than the slogan.
