CHAPTER 1: Pre-Islamic Arabia & Makkan Sanctuary (Pre-570 CE) الجزيرة العربية قبل البعثة والحرم المكي الشريف

Arabia Before Revelation: Jahiliyyah, Makkan Sanctuary, and the Abrahamic Legacy

⏱️ 5 min read

In the sixth century of the Common Era, the Arabian Peninsula occupied a formidable geographic and cultural position at the crossroads of three continents, flanked by two warring imperial superpowers: the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire to the northwest and the Sasanian Persian Empire to the northeast. While these imperial giants exhausted their treasuries and legions in protracted geopolitical warfare, the arid interior of the Arabian desert—protected by its harsh climate and natural topography—remained sovereign and largely untamed by foreign monarchs. Yet, despite this political autonomy, the social, spiritual, and moral fabric of pre-Islamic Arabia was engulfed in profound darkness, an era historically designated as Jahiliyyah (the Age of Ignorant Pre-Islamic Barbarism).

The bedrock of Jahili society was tribalism and unrestrained tribal chauvinism ('asabiyyah), wherein an individual's sole legal protection, honor, and survival were tied to the collective muscle of their patrilineal clan. Justice was defined not by universal moral ethics, but by tribal vengeance: clans fought devastating multi-decade blood feuds over minor trespasses, such as the famous War of Basus and the War of Dahis and Ghabra. In the absence of a centralized judiciary, might was right. Economically, wealthy mercantile elites in urban trade hubs like Makkah and Ta'if exploited the vulnerable through predatory compound usury (riba al-fahish), plunging indebted families into generational servitude. Women were deprived of basic inheritance rights, treated as chattel in matrimonial contracts, and in severe instances, female infanticide (wa'd al-banat) was practiced by impoverished or prideful fathers who buried newborn daughters alive in fear of poverty or social stigma.

Spiritually, the primordial monotheism (Hanifiyyah) instituted by Prophet Ibrahim (Abraham) and his son Prophet Isma'il (Ishmael) when they laid the foundations of the sacred Ka'bah had been grievously corrupted. Centuries prior to the Prophet's birth, 'Amr ibn Luhayy al-Khuza'i traveled to the Levant, observed idol worship, and imported stone idols back to Makkah, erecting Hubal at the Ka'bah and inviting Arabian tribes to place their tribal effigies around the sanctuary. By the sixth century, approximately 360 idols encircled the House of Allah, including major deities like Al-Lat, Al-'Uzza, and Manat. The pilgrimage rites were perverted into polytheistic superstitious displays, where worshippers clapped, whistled, and circumambulated naked, believing that clothes worn while sinning were unsuitable for approaching the sanctuary.

Yet, amidst this spiritual decay, Makkah remained uniquely revered as an inviolable sanctuary (Haram Amīn). Divine providence safeguarded the sanctuary, most spectacularly in approximately 570 CE, known as the Year of the Elephant ('Am al-Fil). The Christian viceroy of Yemen, Abraha al-Ashram, constructed a magnificent cathedral (Al-Qullays) in Sana'a intending to divert the Arabian pilgrimage away from Makkah. When an Arab desecrated the cathedral, Abraha marched north with an overwhelming military host spearheaded by formidable war elephants to demolish the Ka'bah. Abdul Muttalib, chief of Banu Hashim and grandfather of the Prophet ﷺ, famously confronted Abraha demanding the return of his captured camels, stating: "I am the lord of the camels, and the House has a Lord who will defend it." Allah destroyed Abraha's invading army by sending flocks of birds (Ababil) casting baked stones of clay, leaving the proud army like chewed straw, an event immortalized in Surah Al-Fil.

Furthermore, a small intellectual and spiritual minority known as the Hunafa (the Upright Monotheists)—among them Zayd ibn 'Amr ibn Nufayl and Waraqah ibn Nawfal—rejected idolatry, refused to consume animals slaughtered on pagan altars, and condemned female infanticide. They wandered across the lands seeking the pristine Abrahamic faith, praying to the Lord of Ibrahim for guidance. It was into this exact historical crucible—a world trapped between collapsing empires and moral desolation, yet anchored by the sacred Ka'bah and the promise of Abrahamic renewal—that the final messenger of God was destined to arrive.

CHAPTER 2: Blessed Lineage & Desert Infancy (570 CE – 576 CE) النسب الشريف والمولد النبوي المبارك ورضاع بني سعد

Blessed Lineage, Birth in the Year of the Elephant, and Desert Infancy

⏱️ 5 min read

Prophet Muhammad ﷺ descended from the most noble, honorable, and unblemished lineage known in the Arabian Peninsula. His patrilineal ancestry traced directly back through the Adnanite branch of the Arabs to Prophet Isma'il (Ishmael) and his father Prophet Ibrahim (Abraham), the friend of God. The Prophet himself confirmed this immaculate ancestry, stating in Sahih Muslim: "Indeed, Allah chose Isma'il from the sons of Ibrahim, chose Banu Kinanah from the sons of Isma'il, chose Quraysh from Banu Kinanah, chose Banu Hashim from Quraysh, and chose me from Banu Hashim." His father was Abdullah, the most beloved son of Abdul Muttalib, whose life had earlier been ransomed with one hundred camels in fulfillment of a sacred vow. His mother was Aminah bint Wahb, the preeminent noblewoman of the Banu Zuhrah clan.

Before the child drew his first breath, tragedy touched his young household: his father Abdullah fell ill during a trade expedition to the Levant and passed away in Yathrib (Madinah), being buried among his maternal uncles of the Banu Najjar. Muhammad ﷺ was thus born into the world as an orphan, a reality that the Holy Quran memorializes with tender intimacy in Surah Ad-Duha: "Did He not find you an orphan and give you refuge?" (93:6). He was born on a Monday—a day the Prophet later sanctified by fasting, saying: "That is the day on which I was born and on which revelation was sent down to me" (Sahih Muslim)—in the month of Rabi al-Awwal in the Year of the Elephant (approximately 570 CE). Upon his delivery, celestial lights were witnessed by his mother illuminating the castles of Busra in Syria.

His grandfather Abdul Muttalib celebrated the birth with profound joy, carrying the infant into the sacred precincts of the Ka'bah, thanking Allah, and giving him the unique name "Muhammad" (The Praised One), an uncommon appellation in Arabia at the time. When asked why he did not name the child after his ancestors, Abdul Muttalib replied: "I wished that Allah would praise him in the heavens and that His creation would praise him on earth." The infant was briefly nursed by Thuwaybah, the freed slave of his uncle Abu Lahab, who had also nursed his uncle Hamzah and Abu Salamah, establishing a sacred maternal bond of foster-brotherhood.

In accordance with the refined aristocratic custom of the Makkan nobility, the infant was entrusted to a wet nurse from the nomadic Bedouin tribes of the desert. The open desert was prized for its pure dry air, healthy climate, and unpolluted classical Arabic dialect, free from the colloquial linguistic corruptions of foreign mercantile cities. The woman blessed with this honor was Halimah bint Abi Dhu'ayb as-Sa'diyyah of the Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr. Halimah had arrived in Makkah during a season of severe drought, riding an emaciated donkey with an infant whose hunger kept her awake. Other wet nurses rejected the young orphan Muhammad because an orphan promised meager remuneration. Yet Halimah, reluctant to return home empty-handed, took the blessed child into her care.

Instantly upon receiving Muhammad ﷺ, extraordinary blessings (barakah) enveloped Halimah's household: her breasts overflowed with abundant milk for both infants, her exhausted she-camel gave copious milk, her donkey outpaced the entire caravan on the return journey, and her meager sheep returned from grazing fat and full. During his four years among the Banu Sa'd, Muhammad grew with remarkable vigor, developing the purest vernacular Arabic and a deep intimacy with the desert wilderness. It was during this period that the miraculous purification of his heart (Shaqq as-Sadr) occurred. Archangel Jibreel descended while Muhammad was playing with his foster brother, gently laid him down, opened his chest, removed a black clot (representing the innate inclination toward worldly deception), and washed his heart in a golden basin with Zamzam water before sealing it with divine tranquility and wisdom. Fearing for his safety, Halimah lovingly returned the child to his mother Aminah in Makkah. At age six, his mother took him to Yathrib to visit his father's grave and maternal kin; on the return journey, she fell severely ill and passed away at Al-Abwa, leaving the young boy entirely in the care of his grandfather Abdul Muttalib.

CHAPTER 3: Childhood & Youth (576 CE – 590 CE) كفالة أبي طالب ورعاية الغنم وحفظ الله تعالى لنبيه

Childhood, Guardianship of Abu Talib, Youth, and Prophetic Shepherding

⏱️ 5 min read

Following the heartbreaking passing of his mother Aminah at Al-Abwa, the six-year-old Muhammad ﷺ was brought back to Makkah by his faithful servant Umm Ayman (Barakah). His grandfather Abdul Muttalib, now an venerable elder nearing eighty years of age, enveloped the young orphan with tender personal affection. Abdul Muttalib possessed a leather couch placed in the shade of the Ka'bah where no one, not even his own senior sons, dared to sit out of deep reverence. Yet whenever the young Muhammad arrived, Abdul Muttalib would joyfully seat the boy beside him, stroke his back with love, and tell his sons: "Leave my boy alone, for by Allah, he has a great future ahead of him."

This comforting chapter was short-lived: just two years later, when Muhammad ﷺ was eight years old, Abdul Muttalib passed away. On his deathbed, he explicitly entrusted the guardianship of his grandson to his full brother, Abu Talib (son of Fatimah bint Amr). Abu Talib was a man of immense nobility, respected by all Quraysh, though financially impoverished with a large household. Abu Talib took Muhammad into his home and treated him with even greater devotion than his biological children, never dining unless Muhammad was present, for he noticed that whenever the boy ate with them, the food miraculously multiplied and satisfied everyone, whereas without him they remained hungry.

At approximately twelve years of age, Muhammad accompanied Abu Talib on a commercial caravan to Busra in southern Syria. Upon reaching Busra, the caravan camped near the cell of a Christian hermit monk named Bahira (identified in classical sources as George). Bahira had spent decades studying ancient biblical manuscripts that foretold the advent of the final prophet. Uncharacteristically, Bahira invited the entire caravan to a feast, noticing from his cell that a solitary cloud hovered persistently over the caravan, shading young Muhammad, and that the branches of a tree lowered their shade to shelter him. After closely observing the young boy's physical features and questioning him about his habits and dreams, Bahira inspected his back and discovered the Seal of Prophethood (Khatam an-Nubuwwah) between his shoulder blades—a raised fleshy mark the size of a pigeon's egg matching the biblical descriptions. Bahira urgently warned Abu Talib: "Take your nephew back to his land and guard him against ill-wishers, for if they see in him what I have seen, they will seek to harm him. Great indeed is the destiny awaiting your brother's son." Abu Talib promptly concluded his trade and returned Muhammad safely to Makkah.

During his adolescent years, the young Muhammad earned his livelihood as a shepherd, tending the flocks of sheep and goats for the residents of Makkah on the hills of Ajyad and Qarayt. Years later, as the Messenger of Allah, he reflected on this formative occupation with his companions, remarking in Sahih al-Bukhari: "Allah never sent a prophet except that he tended sheep." His companions asked: "Even you, O Messenger of Allah?" He replied: "Yes, I used to tend them for the people of Makkah for a few carats (qirat)." Islamic scholars have noted the immense divine wisdom in shepherding as training for prophetic leadership: it instills profound patience, gentle vigilance, humility, solitude for contemplation, and the capacity to guide and protect a diverse and vulnerable flock without harshness.

Throughout his youth, divine providence enveloped Muhammad ﷺ with absolute moral and spiritual immunity ('Ismah), shielding him from the rampant vices of Makkan society. While young men in pre-Islamic Arabia indulged in wine, gambling, nocturnal revelry, and idolatrous sacrifices, Muhammad harbored an instinctive, innate revulsion toward pagan rituals and immoral conduct. In his own words recorded by Ibn Kathir and Al-Bayhaqi, he attempted only twice in his entire youth to attend nocturnal musical gatherings of the youth of Makkah; on both occasions, Allah caused deep sleep to overpower him until sunrise, awakening him only by the warmth of the sun, thoroughly preserving his purity until his prophetic call.

CHAPTER 4: Hilf al-Fudul & Al-Amin (590 CE – 595 CE) حلف الفضول وتجارة الشام ولقب الصادق الأمين

Hilf al-Fudul (The League of the Virtuous), Syro-Arab Trade, and the Title Al-Amin

⏱️ 5 min read

In his early twenties, Muhammad ﷺ bore witness to two major civic and military developments that deeply illuminated his ethical stance in pre-Islamic Arabia: the Sacrilegious Wars (Harb al-Fijar) and the historic civic alliance known as Hilf al-Fudul (The League of the Virtuous). The Fijar Wars erupted between the Quraysh (and their allies Kinanah) against the Hawazin tribe, so named because hostilities breached the sacred inviolability of the holy months and the Makkan Haram. Muhammad ﷺ participated alongside his uncles, primarily by gathering arrows shot by the enemy and passing them to his uncles, witnessing firsthand the destructive, senseless futility of tribal warfare devoid of moral restraint.

Following the cessation of the Fijar hostilities, an egregious incident catalyzed a moral reckoning among the Makkan nobility. A merchant from the Yemeni tribe of Zabid arrived in Makkah with merchandise, which was purchased by Al-'As ibn Wa'il as-Sahmi (a prominent Qurayshi oligarch). Al-'As withheld the payment and arrogantly refused to return the goods or compensate the merchant. Being a foreign traveler with no tribal allies in Makkah, the Yemeni merchant appealed to various clans of Quraysh, but they dismissed him out of deference to Al-'As. Desperate, the merchant climbed Mount Abu Qubays at sunrise and recited impassioned poetry appealing to the sacred honor of Makkah against injustice.

Deeply moved by this cry for justice, Az-Zubayr ibn Abdul Muttalib (the Prophet's uncle) rallied the conscience of Makkah, calling an assembly at the residence of Abdullah ibn Jud'an, a wealthy and respected chieftain of Banu Taym. Representatives from Banu Hashim, Banu al-Muttalib, Banu Asad, Banu Zuhrah, and Banu Taym gathered, washed their hands in Zamzam, and entered into a solemn covenant: they swore by Allah that they would stand as one unified body alongside any oppressed person in Makkah—whether a native citizen or a foreign stranger—against their oppressor, until their stolen rights were restored. They marched to Al-'As ibn Wa'il and forcibly extracted the Yemeni merchant's full payment.

Muhammad ﷺ was an active attendee and signatory of Hilf al-Fudul. Decades later, while ruling the Islamic state in Madinah, he remembered this pact with profound pride and endorsement, declaring in Musnad Ahmad: "I witnessed a pact in the house of Abdullah ibn Jud'an which I would not trade for a herd of red camels; and if I were summoned to it in Islam, I would respond to it." This statement stands as an enduring theological milestone: Islam affirms and champions universal human rights, civic justice, and the defense of the vulnerable, even when formulated through pre-Islamic or secular civic frameworks.

Simultaneously, Muhammad's reputation for superlative commercial integrity, meticulous accounting, and truthfulness spread throughout the Hijaz. In a society where merchants routinely practiced fraud, oaths of deceit, and extortion, Muhammad ﷺ conducted himself with unimpeachable transparency. Former trade partners like Sa'ib ibn Abi Sa'ib later recalled that Muhammad was the best business associate, never arguing, disputing, or concealing defects. His universal honesty earned him the twin honorary titles by which all Makkah knew him: Al-Sadiq (The Truthful) and Al-Amin (The Trustworthy). Whenever a Makkan citizen sought to travel, deposit valuable goods, or resolve an intricate commercial dispute, they turned unhesitatingly to Muhammad ﷺ.

CHAPTER 5: Marriage to Khadijah (RA) (595 CE – 605 CE) الزواج المبارك من السيدة خديجة رضي الله عنها والحياة الأسرية

Blessed Matrimony with Sayyidah Khadijah (RA) and Domestic Life

⏱️ 5 min read

Sayyidah Khadijah bint Khuwaylid ibn Asad of Banu Asad was universally celebrated as the most eminent noblewoman of Quraysh. Possessing remarkable business acumen, extensive wealth, and noble lineage, she was honored in pre-Islamic Makkah with the title Al-Tahirah (The Pure One) because of her piety, modesty, and unimpeachable chastity. As a successful merchant, she hired men to conduct trade caravans to Syria and Yemen on a profit-sharing basis (mudarabah). Having heard of Muhammad's superlative honesty, truthful tongue, and trustworthiness (Al-Amin), she offered him the leadership of her commercial caravan to Busra in Syria, offering him double the customary compensation and sending her trusted servant Maysarah to accompany and assist him.

Muhammad ﷺ accepted the commission, leading the caravan with consummate diligence and strategic commercial skill. Throughout the journey, Maysarah observed extraordinary events: during the midday desert heat, he observed two angels shading Muhammad from the scorching sun; at Busra, a Christian monk named Nastura remarked that none had rested under a particular tree since the prophets of old except Muhammad. In the marketplaces, Muhammad conducted business with exceptional grace, refusing to swear false oaths by the idols Al-Lat and Al-'Uzza, and securing unprecedented profits. Upon returning to Makkah, Maysarah reported to Khadijah not only the doubled financial returns, but an intimate, glowing account of Muhammad's transcendent character, gentleness, honesty, and spiritual dignity.

Khadijah, who had repeatedly declined marriage proposals from the wealthiest and most powerful chieftains of Quraysh, recognized that Muhammad ﷺ was an unparalleled soul. Through her intimate friend Nafisah bint Munabbih, Khadijah initiated a discrete proposal of marriage. When Nafisah approached Muhammad, asking why he had not married, he replied that he lacked the financial means. Nafisah asked: "What if you were invited to nobility, beauty, wealth, and honor?" He asked: "Who is she?" She replied: "Khadijah." Muhammad rejoiced and consulted his uncles, Abu Talib and Hamzah, who joyfully accompanied him to Khadijah's uncle Amr ibn Asad to formalize the engagement.

At the wedding banquet, Abu Talib delivered a memorable nuptial khutbah, extolling Muhammad's virtue: "Praise be to Allah Who made us from the lineage of Ibrahim and the seed of Isma'il... As for my nephew Muhammad ibn Abdullah, no man of Quraysh can be compared to him except that he outweighs him in honor, nobility, virtue, and intellect. Even though his worldly wealth is modest, wealth is merely a fleeting shadow and a loan to be returned." Muhammad was twenty-five years old, and Khadijah was forty (classical sources also record twenty-eight). Their union began twenty-five years of monogamous devotion, mutual veneration, and deep marital harmony until her passing in the tenth year of Prophethood. The Prophet never took another wife while Khadijah lived.

Their blessed household became a sanctuary of tranquility, compassion, and generosity. Allah blessed their union with six children: two sons, Al-Qasim (from whom the Prophet took his patronymic Kunya Abu al-Qasim) and Abdullah (also called At-Tahir and At-Tayyib), both of whom passed away in early childhood before Islam; and four noble daughters who grew to adulthood and accepted Islam: Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum, and Fatimah az-Zahra. During this period, the Prophet also welcomed young Ali ibn Abi Talib into his home to alleviate financial pressure on Abu Talib during a famine, raising Ali like his own son. He also emancipated and formally adopted Zayd ibn Harithah, who chose to remain in the Prophet's service rather than return with his biological father, illustrating the magnetic warmth and boundless paternal compassion of Muhammad's household.

CHAPTER 6: Ka'bah Reconstruction (605 CE) بناء الكعبة المشرفة والتحكيم العادل في وضع الحجر الأسود

Reconstruction of the Ka'bah and the Black Stone Arbitration

⏱️ 5 min read

When Muhammad ﷺ reached thirty-five years of age (approximately 605 CE), an event occurred that demonstrated his peerless wisdom, civic stature, and conflict-resolution genius to all of Arabia: the reconstruction of the Holy Ka'bah. For generations, the Ka'bah had consisted of dry stone walls standing only slightly taller than the height of a man, with no ceiling, leaving its sacred interior exposed. Torrential flash floods cascading down the Makkan valleys had severely cracked and weakened its foundations, while an earlier fire had charred its walls. Fearing catastrophic structural collapse, the clans of Quraysh gathered and unanimously resolved to demolish and completely rebuild the sacred House of Ibrahim.

Significantly, the chieftains of Quraysh made a solemn ethical pact: they stipulated that the Ka'bah must be rebuilt exclusively using pristine, halal wealth (al-mal al-halal al-tayyib). They explicitly prohibited the use of any money acquired through prostitution (kasb al-baghi), predatory usury (riba), theft, or extortion. This historic resolution provides profound historical insight: even in the depth of Jahiliyyah, the Quraysh recognized in their hearts that God is pure and accepts only what is pure. However, because halal funds were limited in a usury-driven economy, they were forced to reduce the original Abrahamic footprint on the northern side, enclosing the excluded semicircular space with a low wall, known as the Hijr Ismail or Al-Hateem.

The clans divided the demolition and reconstruction responsibilities equitably: Banu Abd Manaf and Banu Zuhrah rebuilt the front facade; Banu Sahm and Banu Jumah worked on the rear; Banu Makhzum took the western wall; and Banu Asad and Banu Taym took the eastern side. A Byzantine Christian craftsman named Baqum, whose ship had wrecked off the Red Sea port of Shu'aybah, was hired as master carpenter and mason. Muhammad ﷺ participated alongside the men of Quraysh, carrying heavy stones upon his shoulders, protected by divine modesty whenever worldly customs threatened exposure.

When the walls reached the height required to reposition the sacred Black Stone (Al-Hajar al-Aswad), a perilous impasse arose. Every tribal clan desired the supreme historical honor of lifting and placing the Black Stone into the eastern corner. Arguments escalated fiercely over four days, weapons were drawn, and the Banu Abd ad-Dar brought a bowl filled with blood into which they and Banu Adi dipped their hands, swearing an oath to the death (Hilf al-Dam). Civil war appeared inevitable inside the sacred sanctuary.

At this explosive moment, the eldest statesman of Quraysh, Abu Umayyah ibn al-Mughirah al-Makhzumi, stood and proposed a compromise: "O Quraysh! Appoint as your arbitrator the very next person who enters through the Gate of As-Safa (Bab al-Masjid)." They eagerly agreed. Moments later, the figure who entered through the gate was none other than Muhammad ibn Abdullah ﷺ. When the assembled chieftains saw him, a collective sigh of relief echoed across the sanctuary, and they joyfully proclaimed: "This is Al-Amin (The Trustworthy)! We accept his judgment! This is Muhammad!"

Demonstrating profound sociopolitical wisdom, Muhammad ﷺ avoided favoring any single tribe. He took off his outer cloak, spread it flat on the ground, and placed the Black Stone in its center with his own hands. He then commanded: "Let the chieftain of every clan grasp an edge of this cloak!" The leaders of Banu Hashim, Banu Umayyah, Banu Makhzum, and all competing factions stepped forward, collectively grasping the cloak and lifting the sacred relic together as equal partners. When they reached the designated corner, Muhammad ﷺ lifted the Black Stone with his blessed hands and set it firmly into its architectural socket. Through this brilliant, inclusive diplomacy, he unified the divided tribes, extinguished the sparks of a devastating civil war, and cemented his standing as the undisputed moral conscience of Makkah five years prior to receiving divine revelation.

CHAPTER 7: Dawn of Revelation (Ramadan 610 CE) الخلوة في غار حراء وبدء الوحي الشريف ونزول اقرأ

Spiritual Solitude in Cave Hira and the Dawn of Revelation

⏱️ 6 min read

As Muhammad ﷺ approached the age of forty, an intense spiritual longing for contemplative solitude began to envelop his soul. The moral bankruptcy, pagan idol-worship, and hedonistic pursuits of Makkan society distressed him deeply. Seeking purity of thought, he began retreating for days and weeks at a time to the Cave of Hira (Ghar Hira), situated atop Jabal an-Nur (The Mountain of Light), approximately two miles northeast of Makkah. The cave was small—scarcely large enough for one man to lie down—yet it offered a breathtaking panoramic vista of the arid valley and the distant House of Allah (the Ka'bah). There, in profound seclusion, he engaged in Tahannuth—devotional worship according to the monotheistic remnant of Ibrahim's faith—subsisting on dried meat and barley provisions lovingly prepared by his wife Sayyidah Khadijah (RA).

The prelude to prophethood manifested through true, righteous dreams (Ar-Ru'ya as-Sadiqah). For six consecutive months prior to revelation, every vision the Prophet beheld in his sleep came to pass with the crystal clarity of the morning dawn (Falaq as-Subh). Trees and stones along the desert paths were heard saluting him with: "As-Salamu 'Alayka Ya Rasul Allah" (Peace be upon you, O Messenger of Allah), though when he turned around, he saw only solitary rocks and hills. This period prepared his human consciousness for the immense spiritual weight of receiving the uncreated Speech of the Creator.

In the blessed month of Ramadan—traditionally identified on Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Decree) in August 610 CE—the celestial veil parted. Archangel Jibreel (Gabriel), the majestic Spirit of Revelation, descended into the cave in radiant splendor. Jibreel stood before Muhammad ﷺ and issued a divine command: "Iqra'!" (Read! / Recite!). Overwhelmed by fear and unlettered humility, Muhammad replied: "Ma ana bi-qari'" (I cannot read / I do not know how to recite). Jibreel seized him, embracing and squeezing him with overwhelming heavenly force until he reached the absolute limit of his physical endurance, then released him and commanded a second time: "Iqra'!" Again Muhammad answered: "I cannot read." Jibreel seized him a second time until exhaustion overtook him, released him, and said: "Iqra'!" For a third time Muhammad answered: "What shall I read?"

Then Archangel Jibreel transmitted the inaugural five verses of the Holy Quran from Surah Al-'Alaq: "Recite in the name of your Lord who created—created man from a clinging substance. Recite, and your Lord is the most Generous—Who taught by the pen—taught man that which he knew not." (96:1–5). The words were etched permanently into the Prophet's heart, marking the end of prophetic silence on earth and the inauguration of the final message to humanity.

Terrified by the majesty of the celestial encounter, his heart trembling violently (yarjufu fu'aduh), Muhammad ﷺ descended the mountain and rushed home to Sayyidah Khadijah. Shivering, he cried: "Zammiluni! Zammiluni!" (Cover me! Wrap me up!). Khadijah wrapped him in warm blankets, gently comforted him, and listened intently as he recounted the terrifying event, expressing fear for his own life. At this epochal moment, Khadijah delivered her immortal words of unwavering faith and psychological solace: "Never! By Allah, Allah will never disgrace you! For you maintain family ties, you bear the burdens of the weak, you assist the impoverished, you host guests generously, and you stand up for those afflicted by calamity."

Recognizing the divine nature of the event, Khadijah immediately took the Prophet to her paternal cousin, Waraqah ibn Nawfal ibn Asad. Waraqah was an aged Christian scholar who had abandoned idolatry, mastered Hebrew and Aramaic, and studied the Torah and Gospel until he had gone blind from old age. When Muhammad described the being in the cave, Waraqah wept with reverence and declared: "This is the Namus (the heavenly trustee of divine secrets, Jibreel) whom Allah sent down to Musa (Moses)! Would that I were a young man! Would that I could be alive when your people banish you from your city!" Surprised, Muhammad asked: "Will they really banish me?" Waraqah answered: "Never has a man brought what you have brought except that he was met with fierce enmity. If I live to see your day, I will support you with all my might." A short time later, Waraqah passed away, leaving the Prophet fortified for the monumental mission ahead.

CHAPTER 8: Secret Call & Dar al-Arqam (610 CE – 613 CE) الدعوة السرية ودار الأرقم بن أبي الأرقم رضي الله عنه

The Early Secret Call and the Sanctuary of Dar al-Arqam

⏱️ 5 min read

Following the inaugural revelation in Cave Hira, an agonizing period of temporary cessation of revelation occurred, historically known as Fatrah al-Wahy. Classical scholars debate its exact duration, ranging from several days to several months. During this hiatus, the Prophet ﷺ experienced intense grief, longing to hear the celestial voice once more. One day, while walking in Makkah, he heard a majestic voice calling from the sky. Looking up, he beheld the same angel who had visited him at Hira, seated upon a throne suspended between heaven and earth. Overawed, he returned home and called again: "Daththiruni! Daththiruni!" (Wrap me up!).

Immediately, Allah revealed the opening verses of Surah Al-Muddaththir: "O you who covers himself [with a garment], arise and warn! And your Lord magnify, and your clothing purify, and uncleanliness avoid!" (74:1–5). This revelation marked the transition from receiving personal prophethood (Nubuwwah) to actively undertaking divine apostolic messengership (Risalah). He was now under divine mandate to summon human hearts away from polytheistic degradation toward the worship of the One Creator.

Recognizing the deeply entrenched idolatry, economic oligarchy, and fierce tribal volatility of Makkah, the Prophet wisely adopted a strategy of discrete, private invitation (Ad-Da'wah as-Sirriyyah) for three years. He did not initially preach in public squares or near the idols of the Ka'bah; instead, he approached individuals of recognized moral rectitude, sound intellect, and spiritual integrity within his immediate circle. The first human being to embrace Islam unconditionally was his beloved wife Sayyidah Khadijah (RA), who had witnessed his character intimately for fifteen years and needed no convincing. She was followed by ten-year-old Ali ibn Abi Talib, his cousin who lived under his roof, becoming the first youth to accept Islam; then Zayd ibn Harithah, his freed and adopted son, becoming the first freedman; and Abu Bakr ibn Abi Quhafah (As-Siddiq), his lifelong intimate companion and respected merchant, becoming the first free adult male to believe.

Abu Bakr proved to be a powerhouse of proactive da'wah. Possessing gentle charisma, extensive genealogical knowledge, and unimpeachable business honor, Abu Bakr immediately reached out to fellow noblemen of Quraysh. Through his personal efforts, an elite core of early believers embraced Islam within days: Uthman ibn Affan, Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, Talhah ibn Ubaydullah, and Az-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam. These men, known as As-Sabiqun al-Awwalun (The Foremost Pioneers), would form the core pillar of Islamic leadership for decades to come.

As the number of secret believers expanded to approximately forty men and women—spanning both noble chieftains and vulnerable slaves like Bilal ibn Rabah, Ammar ibn Yasir, and Abdullah ibn Mas'ud—the Prophet established a secure headquarters. He selected the secluded residence of young Al-Arqam ibn Abi al-Arqam al-Makhzumi (Dar al-Arqam), strategically nestled on the quiet slope of Mount Safa. Dar al-Arqam served as the world's first Islamic institution: a clandestine spiritual sanctuary where believers gathered under the cover of night to hear newly revealed Quranic verses, learn ritual prayer (Salah), purify their hearts through spiritual companionship, and forge an unbreakable bond of faith that superseded tribal allegiance.

CHAPTER 9: Open Call at Mount Safa (613 CE – 615 CE) الصدع بالحق على جبل الصفا والإنذار القرآني المبين

The Open Call at Mount Safa and the Onset of Persecution

⏱️ 5 min read

After three years of discreet spiritual formation within the sanctuary of Dar al-Arqam, the divine directive arrived commanding an end to secrecy. Allah revealed two decisive commands: "And warn, [O Muhammad], your closest kin" (Surah Ash-Shu'ara 26:214), followed by: "Then declare openly what you are commanded and turn away from the polytheists" (Surah Al-Hijr 15:94). The era of clandestine meetings was over; the truth was to be proclaimed unapologetically in the public square.

The Prophet ﷺ initiated this public phase by inviting the clans of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib to a banquet at his home. After feeding them, he addressed his relatives with utmost sincerity: "O sons of Abdul Muttalib! By Allah, I know of no young man among the Arabs who has brought his people anything better than what I have brought you. I have brought you the good of this worldly life and the Hereafter. Who among you will stand by me in this mission?" His paternal uncle Abu Lahab violently interrupted him, mocking his message, while Abu Talib, though not embracing Islam, swore to protect his nephew from bodily harm as long as he lived. Young Ali ibn Abi Talib, barely entering his teens, boldly stood up and declared: "I will stand by you, O Messenger of Allah!"

Shortly thereafter, the Prophet climbed to the rocky summit of Mount Safa, near the Ka'bah, and shouted the traditional alarm call of Arabia used only for existential emergency: "Wa-Sabahah! Wa-Sabahah!" (Beware of a morning attack!). In Bedouin society, this shout brought every citizen rushing to arms. The clans of Quraysh gathered hurriedly at the foot of the mount; even chieftains who could not come sent deputies to see what calamity threatened Makkah.

Standing before the expectant crowd, the Prophet began with a profound rhetorical premise built on his forty-year reputation: "O assembly of Quraysh! If I were to inform you that enemy horsemen were emerging from the foot of this mountain about to raid you, would you believe me?" With one unified voice, the assembled chieftains replied: "Yes! We have never experienced from you anything but absolute truthfulness!" Having secured their unanimous confession of his trustworthiness (Al-Amin), he delivered the divine summons: "Then know that I am a warner sent to you before the descent of a severe punishment! O Banu Abd Shams! O Banu Ka'b ibn Lu'ayy! Save yourselves from the Fire! O Fatimah bint Muhammad, save yourself from the Fire, for I possess no power to protect you from Allah except that I have ties of kinship which I will maintain!"

A stunned silence gripped the valley. Then his uncle Abu Lahab stepped forward, his face contorted in rage, picked up a stone, and hurled it toward the Prophet, screaming: "Tabbal laka sa'ir al-yawm! A-li-hadha jama'tana?" (May you perish for the rest of this day! Is it for this that you have gathered us?). In immediate defense of His Messenger, Allah revealed Surah Al-Masad: "May the hands of Abu Lahab be ruined, and ruined is he! His wealth will not avail him or that which he gained..." (111:1–5). This open declaration shattered the social peace of Makkah. Quraysh recognized that Islam was not merely a private devotional philosophy, but an uncompromising theological and moral revolution that declared their stone idols powerless, abolished class privilege, condemned their ancestors' idolatry, and demanded absolute accountability before the Sovereign Judge of the Universe.

CHAPTER 10: Persecution & Abyssinian Migrations (615 CE – 616 CE) اضطهاد المستضعفين والهجرة إلى أرض الحبشة الأولى والثانية

Persecution of the Vulnerable and the Migrations to Abyssinia

⏱️ 6 min read

Furious that the public call of Islam was gaining adherents, the council of Quraysh chieftains—spearheaded by Abu Jahl (Amr ibn Hisham), Abu Lahab, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, and Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah—convened at Dar an-Nadwah to coordinate systematic retaliation. Because tribal custom protected individuals affiliated with strong clans, the brunt of early pagan brutality fell upon the mustad'afun: vulnerable slaves, freedmen, women, and foreign strangers without tribal protectors.

Among the most legendary examples of unyielding fortitude was Bilal ibn Rabah (RA), an Ethiopian slave owned by Umayyah ibn Khalaf. Umayyah would drag Bilal into the scorching midday desert sun, pin him flat on the burning sand, and place an immense, heated boulder upon his chest, threatening him with death unless he renounced Muhammad and worshipped Al-Lat and Al-'Uzza. Gasping for breath beneath the crushing weight, Bilal repeated his immortal defiance: "Ahad! Ahad!" (Allah is One! Allah is One!). Seeing his torment, Abu Bakr as-Siddiq purchased Bilal at an exorbitant price and immediately emancipated him for the sake of Allah.

Similarly, the family of Yasir—Yasir, his wife Sumayyah bint Khayyat, and their son Ammar—were subjected to gruesome torture in the blazing desert. The Prophet ﷺ passed by them, weeping with compassion yet powerless militarily to intervene, calling out to them: "Sabran ya ala Yasir, fa-inna maw'idakum al-Jannah!" (Patience, O family of Yasir, for your promised appointment is Paradise!). Abu Jahl murdered Sumayyah by piercing her with a spear, making her the first martyr (Shahidah) in the history of Islam, followed shortly by her husband Yasir. Khabbab ibn al-Aratt, a blacksmith, was dragged onto glowing hot coals that were extinguished only by the melting fat of his charred back, yet his faith remained unshakeable.

Recognizing that the torment was escalating beyond human endurance and desiring to preserve the fledgling community from annihilation, the Prophet ﷺ instructed his companions in Rajab of the fifth year of Prophethood (615 CE) to seek asylum across the Red Sea: "If you were to disperse into the land of Abyssinia (Al-Habashah), for in it reigns a Christian king (Ashamah an-Najashi / Negus) in whose realm no one is wronged, until Allah grants you relief from your plight." This marked the First Migration in Islam (Al-Hijrah al-Ula). A vanguard of twelve men and four women—including Uthman ibn Affan and his wife Ruqayyah, daughter of the Prophet—slipped away in the dead of night, took boat from the port of Shu'aybah, and found peaceful sanctuary in Aksum.

A few months later, as Makkan hostility redoubled, a second, much larger migration departed for Abyssinia, comprising eighty-three men and nineteen women led by the Prophet's cousin, Ja'far ibn Abi Talib. Terrified that Islam was establishing an international base, Quraysh dispatched two astute diplomatic envoys—Amr ibn al-'As and Abdullah ibn Abi Rabi'ah—laden with lavish gifts of cured leather for the Negus and his Christian bishops, demanding the extradition of the "foolish youths who deserted their ancestral religion."

In a dramatic royal audience before the Negus and his assembly of bishops holding their holy scriptures, the Negus summoned the Muslims to explain their faith. Ja'far ibn Abi Talib stepped forward as the spokesman of Islam and delivered one of the most magnificent diplomatic orations in history: "O King! We were an ignorant people worshiping stones, eating carrion, committing immoralities, severing family ties, mistreating neighbors, and the strong among us devoured the weak. We remained thus until Allah raised among us a Messenger from our own midst whose lineage, truthfulness, trustworthiness, and chastity we knew well. He summoned us to Allah to declare His Oneness and worship Him alone... He commanded us to speak truth, honor trusts, uphold family ties, be good neighbors, and refrain from shedding blood and committing crimes..."

When the Negus asked if Ja'far had any revelation brought by his Prophet, Ja'far recited the opening verses of Surah Maryam, narrating the miraculous conception of Prophet Isa (Jesus) and the immaculate chastity of Sayyidah Maryam (Mary). The Negus and his bishops wept so copiously that their beards were soaked with tears. The Negus declared: "By God, this message and that which Jesus brought emanate from the very same lamp of divine light!" Turning to the Makkan envoys, the Negus returned their gifts and proclaimed: "Depart, for by God, I will never surrender them to you!" The Muslims lived in peace and security in Abyssinia under royal protection, marking the first international victory for the Islamic faith.

CHAPTER 11: Hamzah & Umar Conversions & Boycott (616 CE – 619 CE) إسلام حمزة وعمر بن الخطاب وحصار شعب أبي طالب الجائر

Conversions of Hamzah and Umar and the Boycott in Shi'b Abi Talib

⏱️ 6 min read

In the sixth year of Prophethood (616 CE), within the span of a single week, the fledgling Muslim community was fortified by the dramatic conversion of two of Makkah's most formidable warrior aristocrats: Hamzah ibn Abdul Muttalib and Umar ibn al-Khattab. Hamzah, the Prophet's paternal uncle and foster brother, was a passionate hunter feared for his martial prowess. One afternoon, upon returning from hunting with his bow slung over his shoulder, a freedwoman informed him that Abu Jahl had severely insulted, cursed, and reviled his nephew Muhammad near Mount Safa while the Prophet remained silent. Incensed by tribal honor, Hamzah marched directly into the sanctuary of the Ka'bah, found Abu Jahl sitting among the Quraysh council, drew his heavy hunting bow, and struck Abu Jahl across the skull, fracturing it and shouting: "Do you dare insult him when I follow his religion and proclaim what he proclaims? Strike me back if you dare!" When Banu Makhzum rose to defend Abu Jahl, the humiliated chieftain backed down, admitting: "Leave Abu Umarah alone, for by God, I reviled his nephew excessively." Hamzah returned home, experienced deep spiritual reflection, and embraced Islam with sincere heart, becoming known as Asadullah (The Lion of Allah).

Days later, the Prophet made a heartfelt supplication recorded in Jami' at-Tirmidhi: "O Allah! Fortify Islam through whichever of these two men is more beloved to You: Abu Jahl ibn Hisham or Umar ibn al-Khattab." The divine answer chose Umar. Umar was a fierce, imposing champion of Qurayshi traditions who had actively persecuted Muslims. Driven to desperation by the division of Makkah, Umar buckled on his sword with the murderous resolve to assassinate Muhammad ﷺ. On his way, he encountered Nu'aym ibn Abdullah, a secret Muslim of Banu Adi, who diverted him by saying: "Why do you deceive yourself, O Umar? Do you think Banu Hashim will let you walk the earth if you kill Muhammad? Why do you not look to your own household first? Your sister Fatimah and her husband Sa'id ibn Zayd have embraced Islam!"

Raging with fury, Umar redirected his steps to his sister's house. As he approached, he heard the melodious recitation of the Quran taught by Khabbab ibn al-Aratt. Umar burst inside, struck his brother-in-law to the floor, and when his sister Fatimah rushed to protect her husband, Umar struck her across the face, drawing blood. Seeing the blood on his sister's face, Umar was seized with immediate shame. Fatimah defiantly declared: "Yes, O Umar! We have submitted to Allah and His Messenger! Do whatever you will!" Umar saw the parchment they were reading and asked to see it. Fatimah told him: "You are impure, and none touches it except the purified. Go and wash." Umar performed ablution, returned, and read the opening verses of Surah Taha: "Ta-Ha. We have not sent down to you the Quran that you be distressed, but only as a reminder for those who fear..." (20:1–4). The celestial beauty, majesty, and linguistic sublimity of the words shattered his pride. He wept and exclaimed: "How noble and sublime are these words! Guide me to Muhammad!"

Khabbab emerged from hiding, crying: "Rejoice, O Umar! For I hope that Allah has answered the supplication of His Prophet!" Umar proceeded to Dar al-Arqam with his sword still sheathed. When the Prophet opened the door, he seized Umar by his cloak, saying: "What brings you, O son of Khattab? Will you not desist until Allah sends upon you calamity?" Umar replied: "O Messenger of Allah, I have come to believe in Allah, His Messenger, and what has been revealed from Allah!" The Prophet uttered a mighty Takbir ("Allahu Akbar!") that resonated across the valley. With Umar and Hamzah in their ranks, the Muslims no longer hid: Umar demanded that they march openly in two ranks to the Ka'bah to pray, led by Hamzah on one flank and Umar on the other, while Quraysh looked on in stunned disbelief. Abdullah ibn Mas'ud later attested: "We were never able to pray at the Ka'bah openly until Umar accepted Islam."

Stunned by these conversions and the royal protection granted to the Abyssinian refugees, Quraysh realized that direct violence could not extinguish the faith. In the seventh year of Prophethood (617 CE), all clans of Quraysh entered into an atrocious, comprehensive economic and social boycott against Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib (both Muslims and non-Muslims who refused to surrender Muhammad to be killed). They drafted a formal charter forbidding intermarriage, commercial trade, social contact, or food sales with the boycotted clans, hanging the parchment inside the ceiling of the Ka'bah to impart sacred solemnity.

For three harrowing years (617–619 CE), the Prophet, his family, and their loyal protectors were confined to the barren mountain ravine known as Shi'b Abi Talib. The siege was brutal: food caravans entering Makkah were monopolized by Qurayshi oligarchs who inflated prices tenfold to prevent the besieged from purchasing grain. Believers were reduced to boiling dried animal skins and eating the bitter leaves of desert talh trees to survive. The cries of starving infants could be heard throughout the surrounding hills. Yet the Prophet remained unshakable, continuing to invite pilgrims to monotheism during the sacred months of truce.

In 619 CE, a coalition of virtuous Qurayshi elders whose consciences were tormented by the cruelty—led by Hisham ibn Amr, Zuhayr ibn Abi Umayyah, and Al-Mut'im ibn Adi—mobilized public opinion to tear down the unjust charter. Concurrently, the Prophet informed his uncle Abu Talib that Allah had revealed to him that tiny termites had consumed the parchment hung inside the Ka'bah, devouring every unjust clause, oath of enmity, and boycott term, leaving intact only the words: "Bi-ismika Allahumma" (In Your Name, O Allah). When Abu Talib challenged Quraysh to inspect the sealed parchment, they discovered the miracle precisely as described. Overcome with embarrassment and facing public outcry, the elders tore down the remnants of the parchment, bringing the three-year siege to an end.

CHAPTER 12: Year of Sorrow & Ta'if Mercy (619 CE) عام الحزن ووفاة خديجة وأبي طالب ورحلة الطائف وموقف الرحمة

The Year of Sorrow (Am al-Huzn) and the Mission of Mercy to Ta'if

⏱️ 6 min read

The tenth year of Prophethood (619 CE) brought overwhelming personal grief and geopolitical vulnerability to Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, a year historically remembered across Islamic chronicles as 'Am al-Huzn (The Year of Sorrow). Shortly after the lifting of the three-year boycott of Shi'b Abi Talib, his protective uncle and guardian Abu Talib fell terminally ill. For over forty years, Abu Talib had served as the Prophet's unyielding shield against Qurayshi violence, sacrificing tribal wealth and standing out of deep love for his nephew. On his deathbed, the Prophet tenderly pleaded with him: "O uncle! Say: La ilaha illa Allah—a phrase through which I will plead for you before Allah!" Yet surrounded by Abu Jahl and Abdullah ibn Abi Umayyah urging him not to abandon the ancestral faith of Abdul Muttalib, Abu Talib passed away without uttering the testimony, leaving the Prophet with profound heartbreak.

Scarcely two months later, a second, even more devastating blow struck: his beloved wife, comforter, and first confidante, Sayyidah Khadijah bint Khuwaylid (RA), passed away at the age of sixty-five. For twenty-five years, Khadijah had been the serene sanctuary of his domestic life, the first to believe in him when all humanity rejected him, spending all her wealth in support of his mission, and comforting his trembling heart with absolute faith. Her demise left a void that no human could fill; the Prophet wept bitterly and remembered her noble loyalty with radiant gratitude throughout the rest of his life.

With both Abu Talib and Khadijah departed, the Qurayshi aristocracy unleashed uninhibited physical abuse against the Prophet. Ruffians threw entrails of sheep and dust upon his head while he was praying near the Ka'bah; his daughters wept as they washed the filth from his hair, while he comforted them: "Do not cry, my little daughters, for Allah will surely protect your father." Realizing that Makkah had closed its doors to the call, the Prophet looked outward to the fertile, mountainous city of Ta'if, situated sixty miles southeast of Makkah, home to the proud and influential tribe of Thaqif. He walked the entire distance on foot accompanied only by his freed son Zayd ibn Harithah, hoping Thaqif would provide sanctuary, embrace Islam, or at least grant a safe haven for the oppressed believers.

In Ta'if, the Prophet sought out the three brothers who ruled Thaqif: Abd Yalil, Mas'ud, and Habib, sons of Amr ibn Umayyah. He sat with them and invited them to Islam and monotheism. Their response was dripping with venomous arrogance and mockery. One said: "Is Allah unable to send anyone except you?" Another remarked: "I would tear down the cloth of the Ka'bah if God sent you!" The third scoffed: "By God, I will not speak to you; if you are truly a Messenger, you are too dangerous to talk to, and if you are a liar, you do not deserve a word!" Not satisfied with rejecting him, they ordered the city's street ruffians, youths, and slaves to form two long lines along his departure path.

As the Prophet ﷺ and Zayd walked through the gauntlet, the mob pelted them with stones, shouting curses and mockery. Zayd attempted to shield the Prophet with his own body, suffering multiple head wounds. The barrage of rocks bruised the Prophet's legs until blood flowed down his shins, soaking his leather sandals and congealing inside them. Whenever he sat down from agony, the mob forced him to stand up so they could resume their barrage, pursuing him for several miles until he collapsed exhausted in an orchard belonging to Utbah and Shaybah ibn Rabi'ah (two Makkan noblemen who were visiting their estate).

Bleeding, bruised, and abandoned by all worldly power, the Prophet ﷺ lifted his blessed hands toward the heavens and uttered what classical scholars recognize as the most sublime, humble, and poignant supplication ever spoken: "O Allah! To You alone I complain of my weakness, the scarcity of my resources, and my humiliation before mankind. O Most Merciful of the merciful! You are the Lord of the oppressed, and You are my Lord. To whom do You entrust me? To a distant stranger who scowls at me? Or to an enemy whom You have given power over me? If You are not angry with me, then I do not care, for Your divine protection is broad enough for me. I seek refuge in the light of Your countenance, which illuminates all darkness and sets right all affairs of this world and the Hereafter, lest Your wrath descend upon me or Your displeasure alight upon me. To You belongs all submission until You are pleased, and there is no power nor might except through You."

Seeing his plight, the orchard owners felt a twinge of tribal pity and sent their young Christian slave, Addas from Nineveh (modern Mosul), with a plate of grapes. The Prophet took a grape and said: "Bismillah" (In the Name of God). Astonished, Addas said: "These are words the people of this land do not speak." The Prophet asked: "Where are you from, and what is your religion?" Addas replied: "I am a Christian from Nineveh." The Prophet remarked: "From the city of the righteous man Yunus ibn Matta (Jonah son of Amittai)?" Addas was stunned: "How do you know Yunus?" The Prophet replied: "He is my brother; he was a prophet, and I am a prophet." Addas fell to his knees, kissing the Prophet's head, hands, and bleeding feet.

Shortly after leaving the orchard, at the pass of Qarn al-Manazil, a cloud shaded the Prophet. Looking up, he saw Archangel Jibreel, who called out: "Allah has heard what your people said to you, and He has sent you the Angel of the Mountains (Malak al-Jibal) to command him as you wish." The Angel of the Mountains greeted the Prophet and said: "O Muhammad! Command me as you will. If you wish, I will bring together the two mountains of Makkah (Al-Akhshabayn: Mount Abu Qubays and Jabal Qu'ayqi'an) upon them, crushing them completely!" At this moment of supreme capability for absolute vengeance against his abusers, the Prophet's transcendent, universal mercy shone across history. He replied: "Nay! Rather, I hope that Allah will bring forth from their loins descendants who will worship Allah alone, associating nothing with Him." This sublime refusal to seek retribution stands as the definitive practical embodiment of the Quranic verse: "And We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds" (21:107).

CHAPTER 13: Al-Isra wal-Mi'raj (620 CE – 621 CE) الإسراء والمعراج الشريف وفرض الصلوات الخمس في سدرة المنتهى

Al-Isra wal-Mi'raj: The Miraculous Night Journey and Heavenly Ascension

⏱️ 6 min read

Following the profound sorrows of Makkah and the physical trauma of Ta'if, Allah Almighty bestowed upon His beloved Messenger ﷺ the greatest spiritual honor and consolation ever granted to a created being: Al-Isra wal-Mi'raj (The Miraculous Night Journey and Celestial Ascension). Occurring approximately sixteen to eighteen months prior to the Hijrah (in the month of Rajab, 621 CE), this physical and bodily journey transported the Prophet beyond the earthly dimensions of time and space, demonstrating that while the chieftains of earth had rejected him, the celestial heavens welcomed him with boundless joy.

The journey commenced while the Prophet was resting near the Ka'bah (in the Hatim). Archangel Jibreel descended, opened his chest once more, washed his heart with Zamzam water, and filled it with faith and wisdom. Jibreel presented Al-Buraq, a celestial white steed smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey, whose single hoofstep reached the furthest horizon of its gaze. The Prophet mounted Al-Buraq and was transported instantaneously from Al-Masjid al-Haram in Makkah to Al-Masjid al-Aqsa in Bayt al-Maqdis (Jerusalem). This terrestrial leg constitutes Al-Isra (The Night Journey). Upon entering the sacred compound of Al-Aqsa, the Prophet tied Al-Buraq to the historic ring where the previous prophets had tied their mounts. Inside the sanctuary, an assembly of all past prophets and messengers of Allah—from Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, and Isa to Dawud and Sulayman—were resurrected. Archangel Jibreel took the Prophet by the hand and led him forward to the prayer niche (Mihrab), where Muhammad ﷺ led all the prophets in congregational prayer as the undisputed Imam of the Prophets (Imam al-Anbiya).

Thereafter commenced the celestial ascension (Al-Mi'raj) through the cosmic dimensions. Accompanied by Jibreel, the Prophet traversed the seven heavens, each heaven guarded by angelic sentinels who asked: "Who is it?" Jibreel replied: "Jibreel." They asked: "Who is with you?" He answered: "Muhammad." They asked: "Has his mission been summoned?" He replied: "Yes." The gates opened, and in each realm, he was embraced by the prophets: in the first heaven, Adam, father of humanity; in the second, the maternal cousins Yahya (John the Baptist) and Isa (Jesus); in the third, Yusuf, who was given half of all human beauty; in the fourth, Idris; in the fifth, Harun; in the sixth, Musa; and in the seventh heaven, Ibrahim, resting his back against Al-Bayt al-Ma'mur (The Frequented House), where seventy thousand angels enter daily to worship and never return due to their vast numbers.

From the seventh heaven, the Prophet was elevated beyond all created realms to Sidrat al-Muntaha (The Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary), whose leaves were like elephant ears and fruits like large clay jugs, cloaked in divine splendors that defy human description. At this absolute frontier, Archangel Jibreel halted, stating: "Here I must stop. Were I to step forward even the width of a fingertip, I would be consumed by the divine light." The Prophet ﷺ proceeded alone into the immediate divine presence, crossing seventy thousand veils of celestial light, and received direct divine audience with Allah, Lord of the Worlds.

In that sacred direct presence, the supreme pillar of practical Islamic worship was ordained: the five daily prayers (Salah). Initially, Allah prescribed fifty prayers per day upon the Ummah. As the Prophet descended, he passed Prophet Musa in the sixth heaven, who questioned him about the divine decree. Musa advised: "Return to your Lord and ask for an alleviation, for your Ummah is weak and will not be able to bear fifty prayers; I tested the Children of Israel with less and they failed." The Prophet looked to Jibreel, who nodded in assent. The Prophet returned repeatedly between the divine presence and Musa, pleading for reductions—from fifty to forty, thirty, twenty, ten, and finally five daily prayers. When Musa urged him to return yet again to ask for further reduction, the Prophet said: "I have returned to my Lord until I feel shy; rather, I submit and accept." Then a divine voice proclaimed: "My obligation has been enacted and My servants' burden eased: they are five in action, yet fifty in the heavenly reward; My word does not change."

Returning to Makkah before the morning dawn, the Prophet described the miraculous journey to the people. Quraysh burst into laughter, ridiculing him and demanding that he describe the architecture of Jerusalem, knowing he had never visited it before. Allah lifted the veil and projected a vision of Al-Masjid al-Aqsa directly before his eyes, allowing him to describe its gates and pillars detail by detail. He also accurately foretold the arrival of a specific caravan from Syria, identifying its lead camel and missing water jug. When skeptics rushed to Abu Bakr, taunting him: "Do you believe your companion claims he went to Jerusalem and the heavens in a single night?" Abu Bakr replied without a heartbeat of hesitation: "If he said it, then it is the absolute truth! I believe him in news far greater than that—I believe the revelation that descends upon him from heaven in an instant of the morning or evening!" From that day forward, Abu Bakr was bestowed with his eternal title: As-Siddiq (The Confirming Champion of Truth).

CHAPTER 14: Pledges of Al-Aqabah (620 CE – 622 CE) بيعة العقبة الأولى والثانية وعهود يثرب التاريخية

The Pledges of Al-Aqabah and the Yathrib Covenant

⏱️ 6 min read

Following the rejection of Ta'if, the Prophet ﷺ adopted a strategic policy of presenting his message to external Arabian tribes during the annual pilgrimage fairs and commercial markets of Ukaz, Majannah, and Dhu al-Majaz. Abu Lahab followed behind him, shouting to the crowds: "Do not listen to him, for he is a liar and an apostate!" Most tribes, fearing Qurayshi retaliation, dismissed his appeals. However, in the pilgrimage season of the eleventh year of Prophethood (summer 620 CE), divine destiny orchestrated a breakthrough that would alter the trajectory of human history.

At Al-Aqabah (a narrow pass between Mina and Makkah), the Prophet encountered a small group of six men from the Banu Khazraj tribe of Yathrib (later known as Madinah): As'ad ibn Zurarah, Awf ibn al-Harith, Rafi' ibn Malik, Qutbah ibn Amir, Uqbah ibn Amir, and Jabir ibn Abdullah ibn Ri'ab. For generations, the Arab tribes of Yathrib (Aws and Khazraj) had been locked in bloody, exhausting civil strife, culminated in the catastrophic Battle of Bu'ath. Yathrib was also home to learned Jewish tribes (Banu Qaynuqa, Banu an-Nadir, Banu Qurayzah), who frequently warned the pagan Arabs: "A prophet will soon arise, and we will follow him and wipe you out as Aad and Iram were wiped out!"

When the Prophet sat with the six Khazrajites, recited the Quran, and invited them to monotheism, they looked at one another and said: "By God! You know this is the very Prophet the Jews have been threatening us with. Do not let them reach him before you!" Furthermore, they recognized in his message the divine cure for the civil discord tearing Yathrib apart. They embraced Islam immediately, promising to convey the message to their brethren upon returning home.

The following year (621 CE, 12th year of Prophethood), twelve men from Yathrib (ten from Khazraj and two from Aws) arrived for pilgrimage and met the Prophet secretly at Al-Aqabah, concluding the First Pledge of Al-Aqabah (Bay'at al-Aqabah al-Ula). They pledged allegiance upon the moral principles outlined in Surah Al-Mumtahanah: to worship Allah alone without associating partners, not to steal, not to commit adultery, not to kill their children, not to slander, and not to disobey the Prophet in that which is right. Because this pledge contained no military or defensive clauses, it was historically termed Bay'at an-Nisa (The Pledge of the Righteous).

To nurture this fledgling community, the Prophet dispatched Mus'ab ibn Umayr (RA)—a young, refined nobleman of Quraysh known for his eloquence, gentle temperament, and mastery of the Quran—as the first ambassador (Muqri') and teacher of Islam in Yathrib. Mus'ab resided with As'ad ibn Zurarah and taught people with sublime intellectual grace. When Sa'd ibn Mu'adh and Usayd ibn Hudayr, the formidable chieftains of the Banu Abd al-Ashhal clan, approached Mus'ab with spears drawn to expel him, Mus'ab gently said: "Will you not sit and listen? If you are pleased with our words, accept them; if you dislike them, we will spare you what you detest." They sat, heard the Quran recited, and their hearts melted; both chieftains embraced Islam on the spot. Sa'd returned to his clan and declared: "Speaking to your men and women is unlawful for me until you believe in Allah and His Messenger!" By sunset, every single man and woman of Banu Abd al-Ashhal had entered Islam. Within one year, Mus'ab's gentle da'wah reached every household in Yathrib.

In the pilgrimage of 622 CE (13th year of Prophethood), Mus'ab returned to Makkah accompanying a secret delegation of seventy-three men and two women (Nusaybah bint Ka'b and Asma bint Amr) from Yathrib. In the dead of night during the Tashriq days, they slipped away from their caravan in small groups to meet the Prophet at Al-Aqabah. The Prophet was accompanied by his paternal uncle Al-Abbas ibn Abdul Muttalib (who was still a non-Muslim at the time, but fiercely protective of his nephew). Al-Abbas addressed the Yathribites: "O people of Khazraj! Muhammad is protected among his people. If you believe you can honor what you invite him to and defend him against his enemies, then proceed; but if you will surrender him after taking him away, then leave him now!"

Ka'b ibn Malik and Al-Bara' ibn Ma'rur responded: "We have heard what you said. Speak, O Messenger of Allah, and take for yourself and your Lord whatever terms you desire!" The Prophet recited Quran, called them to Allah, and stipulated the terms of the Second Pledge of Al-Aqabah (Bay'at al-Aqabah ath-Thaniyah): to listen and obey in ease and hardship, to spend in prosperity and poverty, to enjoin good and forbid evil, to speak the truth for Allah without fearing blame, and to defend him as they defended their own wives and children. Al-Bara' took his hand and swore: "Yes, by the One Who sent you with the truth, we will defend you with the same shields we defend our families!" Sa'd ibn Ubadah and As'ad ibn Zurarah stepped forward to ensure everyone understood the gravity of the pact: it meant war against the entire world. Unflinchingly, all seventy-five delegates pledged their lives. The Prophet appointed twelve chieftains (Nuqaba)—nine from Khazraj and three from Aws—to represent their clans, declaring: "You are the guardians of your people, and I am the guardian of my people." With this historic covenant sealed, the green light for the Great Migration (Hijrah) was illuminated.

CHAPTER 15: The Great Hijrah (Rabi al-Awwal 1 AH / 622 CE) الهجرة النبوية المباركة وغار ثور وتأسيس مسجد قباء والمدينة المنورة

The Great Migration (Hijrah) and Arrival at Quba and Madinah

⏱️ 6 min read

Once the covenant of Al-Aqabah was sealed, the Prophet ﷺ permitted the oppressed believers of Makkah to emigrate to Yathrib in small, clandestine groups. Families sacrificed ancestral homes, properties, and businesses, slipping into the desert under cover of darkness. Soon, scarcely any Muslims remained in Makkah save the Prophet ﷺ, Abu Bakr as-Siddiq, young Ali ibn Abi Talib, and those held forcibly captive by their families. Realizing that Islam had established a sovereign fortified sanctuary north along their vital Syrian trade route, the chieftains of Quraysh convened an emergency parliament at Dar an-Nadwah in Safar 1 AH (September 622 CE).

At Dar an-Nadwah, Abu Jahl proposed a sinister, foolproof assassination conspiracy: each of the major Qurayshi clans would nominate a strong, noble youth armed with a razor-sharp sword. The squad would surround Muhammad's house and strike him simultaneously with a single combined blow. By dispersing the guilt across all clans, the blood responsibility (dam) would be impossible for Banu Hashim to avenge through tribal retaliation, forcing them to accept monetary blood money (diyah). Archangel Jibreel descended upon the Prophet, conveying the divine plot and instructing him: "Do not sleep tonight in the bed you usually sleep in, and depart Makkah."

On the designated night, the assassination squad encircled the Prophet's house, waiting for him to emerge for morning prayer. Demonstrating staggering courage, twenty-three-year-old Ali ibn Abi Talib volunteered to lie in the Prophet's bed, wrapping himself in the Prophet's distinctive green Hadrami cloak. This stratagem deceived the assassins peering through the shutters while allowing Ali to remain behind to return all commercial deposits and trusts (Amanat)—ironically entrusted to Al-Amin even by his persecutors—to their rightful owners. The Prophet slipped out the front door, cast a handful of dust toward the assassins while reciting the opening verses of Surah Ya-Sin: "And We have put before them a barrier and behind them a barrier and covered them, so they do not see" (36:9). A miraculous slumber overtook the assassins, and the Prophet walked past them unseen.

The Prophet joined Abu Bakr, who had prepared two swift she-camels fed on acacia leaves for months. To evade Qurayshi tracking parties that naturally searched northward along the road to Madinah, the Prophet enacted a masterstroke of tactical evasion: they rode southward into the rugged mountains and concealed themselves inside Cave Thawr (Ghar Thawr) atop Mount Thawr for three perilous days and nights. Quraysh posted an astronomical bounty of one hundred red camels for the capture or death of either man, dead or alive. Makkan desert trackers, reading faint footprints, climbed to the very mouth of the cave. Looking at their feet just inches away, Abu Bakr wept in terror for the Prophet's life, whispering: "O Messenger of Allah, if any one of them merely looks down at his feet, he will surely see us!" The Prophet calmly smiled and delivered his immortal theological reply: "O Abu Bakr, what do you think of two people, when Allah is their Third? Grieve not, for Allah is with us!" (Surah At-Tawbah 9:40). Allah blinded the pursuers' eyes; classical historians record that spiders spun webs across the cave opening and doves nested at the entrance, convincing the trackers that the cave had long been abandoned.

The logistical operation of the Hijrah was executed with consummate operational genius: Abu Bakr's son Abdullah gathered daytime intelligence in Makkah and reported to the cave at dusk; his daughter Asma bint Abi Bakr carried food provisions tied with her torn waist belt (earning the title Dhat an-Nitaqayn); Abu Bakr's freedman 'Amir ibn Fuhayrah grazed sheep over their tracks to erase footprints; and a skilled non-Muslim desert navigator named Abdullah ibn Urayqit was hired to guide them along the treacherous, unfrequented coastal route. Along the journey, a famous bounty hunter named Suraqah ibn Malik tracked them; as Suraqah charged with drawn spear, his stallion's hooves sank into the hard desert rock as if into quicksand. Terrified, Suraqah begged for mercy and was granted a written safe conduct, with the Prophet astonishingly prophesying: "How will you feel, O Suraqah, when you wear the golden bangles and crown of Chosroes (the Emperor of Persia)?"—a prophecy fulfilled twenty-two years later during Umar's caliphate.

On Monday, 8th of Rabi al-Awwal 1 AH (September 23, 622 CE), the Prophet arrived at Quba on the southern outskirts of Yathrib, where he was greeted with overwhelming jubilation by hundreds of waiting believers. In Quba, the Prophet resided for four days, laying the foundation stones of Masjid Quba—the very first house of worship founded in Islam upon pure piety (Taqwa). Ali ibn Abi Talib joined them on foot from Makkah, his feet bleeding from the 280-mile trek. On Friday, the Prophet rode into the heart of Yathrib, leading the first Friday congregational prayer (Salat al-Jumu'ah) in the valley of Ranuna. As his camel Al-Qaswa entered the city, women and children climbed rooftops singing the immortal anthem: "Tala'a al-badru 'alayna, min thaniyyatil-wada'..." Chieftains grasped his camel's reins, each pleading for the honor of hosting him. The Prophet gently replied: "Leave her, for she is divinely guided." The camel knelt at a date-drying yard belonging to two orphan boys, Sahl and Suhayl, opposite the house of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, where the Prophet took up residence. The city of Yathrib was rechristened Madinat ar-Rasul (The City of the Messenger), or simply Al-Madinah al-Munawwarah (The Illuminated City).

CHAPTER 16: Masjid, Muwakhat & Madinah Charter (1–2 AH) بناء المسجد النبوي الشريف والمؤاخاة العظمى ووثيقة المدينة الدستورية

Building the Prophetic Mosque, Muwakhat, and the Charter of Madinah

⏱️ 6 min read

Upon establishing his presence in Madinah, the Prophet ﷺ did not focus on personal comfort or authoritarian dominance; rather, he immediately enacted a revolutionary triad of state-building measures that laid the sociopolitical, spiritual, and legal foundation of the first Islamic commonwealth: the construction of Al-Masjid an-Nabawi, the institutionalization of the Great Brotherhood (Al-Muwakhat), and the drafting of the historic constitutional treaty known as Sahifat al-Madinah (The Charter of Madinah).

First, the Prophet purchased the land where his camel had knelt from the two orphan boys, Sahl and Suhayl, insisting on paying its full market price despite their offer to gift it. The land was cleared, and the construction of the Prophetic Mosque (Al-Masjid an-Nabawi) commenced. The structure was magnificent in its simplicity: walls of unbaked mud bricks, columns formed from dried palm trunks, and a thatched roof of palm branches plastered with clay, oriented initially toward Jerusalem (Bayt al-Maqdis). Muhammad ﷺ worked with his own hands alongside the Muhajirun (Emigrants) and Ansar (Helpers), carrying heavy adobe bricks and chanting lines of spiritual encouragement: "O Allah, there is no true life except the life of the Hereafter, so forgive the Ansar and the Muhajirun!"

The mosque was far more than a sanctuary of ritual prayer; it served as the holistic beating heart of Madinan society. It was the executive headquarters of governance, a parliament for democratic consultation (Shura), a judicial hall for arbitrating civil disputes, a diplomatic embassy receiving foreign delegations, and an academy of higher learning. Attached to the northern rear of the mosque was a roofed terrace known as As-Suffah. Here resided Ahl al-Suffah (The People of the Bench)—a dedicated fraternity of dozens of impoverished, single, or detached companions, including Abu Hurayrah, who devoted their days to Quranic memorization, studying prophetic jurisprudence, and nocturnal worship, subsisting on communal dates and meals provided by the Prophet and affluent companions.

Second, to solve the humanitarian and economic crisis facing the Muhajirun—who had arrived in Madinah penniless after abandoning all worldly possessions in Makkah—the Prophet instituted the institution of Al-Muwakhat (Fraternal Brotherhood). In the courtyard of Anas ibn Malik's residence, the Prophet paired approximately ninety men—forty-five Muhajirun with forty-five Ansar—in a sacred, legally binding pact of brotherhood that initially included mutual inheritance rights until later abrogated by Surah Al-Anfal (8:75). The Ansar demonstrated generosity unmatched in human chronicles. Sa'd ibn ar-Rabi al-Ansari took his assigned brother, Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf, to his home and said: "I am the wealthiest of the Ansar; I divide my wealth equally between us, and I have two orchards, choose whichever you prefer!"

Abdur-Rahman's noble response became a foundational ethic of Islamic enterprise and self-reliance: "May Allah bless you in your family and wealth! I have no need for charity; show me the way to the marketplace (Suq Qaynuqa)!" Abdur-Rahman ventured into the market, traded modest quantities of dried yogurt (aqit) and clarified butter (samn), and within months had accumulated enough legitimate wealth to marry and sponsor independent caravans. The Holy Quran immortalized the Ansar's selfless nobility in Surah Al-Hashr: "And they give [them] preference over themselves, even though they are in poverty" (59:9).

Third, the Prophet drafted Sahifat al-Madinah (The Constitution of Madinah), recognized by modern political historians as the world's first written constitutional framework for a pluralistic society. Comprising approximately fifty-two clauses, the Charter unified the heterogeneous population of Madinah—the Muslim Muhajirun, the Muslim Ansar (Aws and Khazraj), and approximately twelve Jewish tribes (including Banu Qaynuqa, Banu an-Nadir, and Banu Qurayzah)—into a single political federation: "They constitute one political community (Ummatun Wahidah) distinct from all other people."

The Charter established monumental principles: absolute freedom of religion ("To the Jews their religion, and to the Muslims their religion"); mutual defense of Madinah against foreign aggression ("Each party shall assist the other against whoever wages war against the signatories of this document"); collective responsibility for civil peace; prohibition of sheltering criminals or murderers; protection of minority rights; and the recognition of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ as the supreme constitutional arbiter for interstate disputes. Through this brilliant constitutional document, Madinah was transformed from a war-torn tribal wasteland into a rule-of-law commonwealth governed by justice, contract sanctity, and universal civic protection.

CHAPTER 17: Shift of Qiblah & Breakthrough at Badr (2 AH / 624 CE) تحويل القبلة إلى الكعبة المشرفة وغزوة بدر الكبرى يوم الفرقان

The Shift of Qiblah and the Decisive Breakthrough at Badr

⏱️ 6 min read

The second year of the Hijrah (2 AH / 624 CE) was a watershed epoch that redefined the identity and geopolitical presence of the Muslim Ummah. For sixteen to seventeen months following the Hijrah, the Prophet and his companions prayed facing northward toward Bayt al-Maqdis (Jerusalem), sharing the direction of prayer with the People of the Book. Yet the Prophet's heart harbored a deep, unspoken spiritual longing to face the ancient monotheistic Ka'bah built by his forefather Ibrahim. While leading the Dhuhr (or Asr) prayer at the mosque of Banu Salamah in Rajab 2 AH, Allah answered his gaze: "We have certainly seen the turning of your face, [O Muhammad], toward heaven, and We will surely turn you to a qiblah with which you will be pleased. So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram..." (Surah Al-Baqarah 2:144). Mid-prayer, the Prophet rotated 180 degrees south toward Makkah, and the entire congregation followed him in fluid obedience. The mosque was ever after commemorated as Masjid al-Qiblatayn (The Mosque of the Two Qiblahs).

In Ramadan of the same year, intelligence reached Madinah that a massive Qurayshi mercantile caravan returning from Gaza—comprising one thousand camels carrying merchandise valued at fifty thousand gold dinars—was traversing the coastal road. The caravan was led by Abu Sufyan ibn Harb with only thirty to forty guards. Because Quraysh had illegally confiscated and auctioned off all homes, properties, and farms left behind by the emigrants in Makkah, the Prophet called upon a volunteer detachment of believers to intercept the caravan, seeking legitimate restitution and economic leverage to compel Quraysh to cease their hostilities.

Detecting danger, Abu Sufyan dispatched a frantic messenger named Damdam ibn Amr to Makkah. Damdam slashed his camel's ears, ripped his shirt, and screamed in the courtyard of the Ka'bah: "O Quraysh! Your merchandise with Abu Sufyan is intercepted by Muhammad and his companions! Help! Help!" Galvanized by wounded pride, all the aristocratic grandees of Quraysh—including Abu Jahl, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, and Utbah ibn Rabi'ah—marshaled a formidable, fully armored army of one thousand warriors, with one hundred horses, seven hundred camels, singing slave girls, and abundant wine, determined to crush the Muslims forever. Meanwhile, Abu Sufyan skillfully veered his caravan westward along the coastal surf, safely bypassing the Muslim scouts, and dispatched a message to the Makkan army advising them to return. However, Abu Jahl arrogance exploded: "By God, we will not return until we reach the wells of Badr, camp there for three days, slaughter camels, feast on wine, and have women sing to us, so the Arabs will hear of us and fear us forever!"

The Prophet ﷺ found himself facing an entirely unintended existential crisis: instead of intercepting an unarmed caravan, they were on a collision course with the most heavily armed army in Arabia. Because the Ansar's pledge at Al-Aqabah technically applied to defensive warfare inside Madinah, the Prophet convened a council of war (Shura), stating: "Advise me, O people!" First, Al-Miqdad ibn Amr of the Muhajirun rose and declared: "O Messenger of Allah! Proceed to what Allah has commanded you; we will not say to you as the Children of Israel said to Musa, 'Go, you and your Lord, and fight; indeed, we are sitting right here!' Rather, we say: Fight, you and your Lord, and we will fight alongside you!" Then Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, supreme chieftain of the Ansar, rose with tears of passionate loyalty: "Perhaps you fear, O Messenger of Allah, that the Ansar do not feel obligated to fight beyond their borders? By the One Who sent you with the truth, if you were to lead us to the sea and plunge into it, we would plunge in behind you; not a single man among us would stay behind! Lead us forward with the blessing of Allah!"

Fortified by this unbreakable faith, the Muslim force—numbering a mere 313 to 317 men, with only two horses (belonging to Az-Zubayr and Al-Miqdad) and seventy camels that they rode in turns—advanced to Badr. Al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir, an expert in desert warfare, respectfully asked the Prophet: "O Messenger of Allah, is this camping location a divine revelation (Wahy) from Allah that we cannot alter, or is it military opinion, strategy, and war?" The Prophet replied: "It is military opinion, strategy, and war." Al-Hubab advised: "Then this is not the right place; let us advance to the well nearest the enemy, fill in all other wells, and build a reservoir so we will have drinking water while they have none." The Prophet praised his tactical wisdom and immediately executed the plan.

On the eve of Friday, 17th of Ramadan 2 AH, the Prophet spent the entire night in his command shelter (Al-'Arish) in intense, weeping supplication, his arms raised so high toward the heavens that his cloak repeatedly fell from his shoulders. Abu Bakr tenderly placed the cloak back, holding him and saying: "Enough, O Messenger of Allah, your Lord will surely fulfill what He promised you!" The Prophet wept: "O Allah! If this small band of believers is annihilated today, You will never be worshipped on earth!" At dawn, the two armies met. The battle commenced with traditional individual duels: Hamzah ibn Abdul Muttalib, Ali ibn Abi Talib, and Ubaydah ibn al-Harith stepped forward and swiftly eliminated the champions of Quraysh: Utbah, Walid, and Shaybah.

The general battle commenced under the battle cry: "Ahad! Ahad!" Allah fulfilled His promise, descending one thousand angels rank upon rank under Jibreel to assist the believers (Surah Al-Anfal 8:9). The Prophet emerged from his shelter reciting: "Their assembly will be defeated, and they will turn their backs!" (Surah Al-Qamar 54:45). Taking a handful of pebbles, he cast it toward the Qurayshi army, saying: "Shahat al-wujuh!" (May these faces be disfigured!), and the dust entered the eyes of every enemy warrior. A total rout ensued: seventy Makkan aristocrats were killed—including the archenemy Abu Jahl, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, and An-Nadr ibn al-Harith—and seventy prominent chieftains were taken captive, while only fourteen Muslims attained martyrdom.

The Day of Badr was named by the Quran Yawm al-Furqan (The Day of the Criterion between Truth and Falsehood). The Prophet set a revolutionary, unprecedented standard for the ethical treatment of prisoners of war: he commanded the companions to share their bread with the captives while eating only dates themselves. For literate captives who could not afford monetary ransom, the Prophet decreed that teaching ten Muslim boys of Madinah to read and write would serve as their full ransom. Thus, out of the crucible of military victory, the Prophet planted the seeds of universal literacy and human compassion.

CHAPTER 18: Crucible of Uhud (Shawwal 3 AH / 625 CE) غزوة أحد الكبرى وامتحان الرماة واستشهاد حمزة رضي الله عنه

The Crucible of Uhud: Lessons of Obedience, Sacrifice, and Resilience

⏱️ 6 min read

The crushing humiliation at Badr ignited an obsessive thirst for vengeance among the Qurayshi aristocracy. Under the supreme command of Abu Sufyan ibn Harb, all revenue from the saved Syrian trade caravan was pooled into a massive military fund. In Shawwal 3 AH (March 625 CE), Quraysh marched toward Madinah with an overwhelming army of three thousand fully equipped warriors, including two hundred elite cavalry commanded by Khalid ibn al-Walid and Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl, accompanied by aristocratic women beating tambourines under Hind bint Utbah (whose father, uncle, and brother were slain at Badr) singing war poetry to incite the troops to merciless slaughter.

When the Makkan host camped at the foot of Mount Uhud, three miles north of Madinah, the Prophet ﷺ convened a Shura council. The Prophet's own military intuition, supported by senior companions and the hypocrite Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul, was to remain within the fortified perimeter of Madinah, turning the city's alleyways and stone houses into lethal kill zones. However, a large majority of eager young companions, particularly those who had missed the glory of Badr, passionately insisted on meeting the enemy in the open plains: "O Messenger of Allah! Let us go out to our enemies lest they think we are cowards!" Respecting democratic consensus (Shura), the Prophet entered his house, donned two coats of armor and his iron helmet, and slung his sword. When some companions, realizing they had pressured the Prophet, expressed remorse, the Prophet declared an immortal principle of decisive leadership: "It does not befit a Prophet who has put on his armor to lay it down until Allah decides between him and his enemies."

The Prophet marched with one thousand men. At Ash-Shawt, the chief of the hypocrites (Munafiqun), Abdullah ibn Ubayy, executed a calculated treason: he pulled away one-third of the army (three hundred men), declaring: "He obeyed the young boys and disobeyed me; why should we kill ourselves here?" This treasonous withdrawal left only seven hundred steadfast believers to confront three thousand seasoned invaders. Unperturbed, the Prophet deployed his forces at the base of Mount Uhud, utilizing the massive rocky mountain to protect the Muslim rear and right flank.

Recognizing that the only vulnerability in his tactical position was a low rocky hillock on the left flank—known as Jabal ar-Rumat (The Mount of the Archers)—the Prophet stationed fifty elite archers under the command of Abdullah ibn Jubayr. The Prophet issued strict, unequivocal, and emphatic orders: "Guard our backs! Do not abandon your position under any circumstances. Even if you see us snatching the enemy's camp, do not join us; and even if you see birds snatching our corpses from the sky, do not abandon your post until I send for you!"

The battle opened with intense combat. Zubayr ibn al-Awwam, Ali ibn Abi Talib, Abu Dujanah (wielding the Prophet's sword with his red headband of death), and Hamzah ibn Abdul Muttalib tore through the Qurayshi center like a hurricane. Hamzah fought with two swords, cutting down pagan standard-bearers one after another. Seeing their standard fall seven times, Quraysh broke ranks and fled in wild panic, leaving behind their tents, gold, and baggage. The battle appeared conclusively won.

At this decisive juncture, catastrophic human error altered the course of history. Looking down from Jabal ar-Rumat, forty of the fifty archers saw the Muslims gathering the abandoned spoils of war. Blinding temptation seized them, and they argued: "The enemy is routed! Why do we remain here?" Abdullah ibn Jubayr pleaded with them, shouting: "Have you forgotten the command of the Messenger of Allah?" Yet forty archers deserted their post, descending into the plain to collect booty, leaving only Ibn Jubayr and fewer than ten archers on the ridge.

Observing the deserted ridge with predatory military genius, cavalry commander Khalid ibn al-Walid wheeled his two hundred horsemen around the back of the mountain, annihilated the brave remnant under Ibn Jubayr, and crashed into the rear of the Muslim army. Simultaneously, Qurayshi infantry rallied, encircling the Muslims in a terrifying vortex of chaos, dust, and confusion. Believers were separated from their commanders; friends struck friends in the blinding dust. During the mayhem, Mus'ab ibn Umayr, who resembled the Prophet, was martyred, and a voice screamed across the battlefield: "Muhammad has been killed!" This rumor shattered the morale of many, who cast down their weapons, while others like Anas ibn an-Nadr cried: "What will you do with life after him? Rise and die for that which he died for!" and charged into the enemy until he fell with over eighty wounds.

The Prophet ﷺ was not dead, but surrounded by a ferocious circle of pagan champions seeking his head. A tiny phalanx of fewer than thirty companions formed a human fortress around him: Talhah ibn Ubaydullah shielded the Prophet from descending swords, catching arrows with his bare hand until his fingers were permanently paralyzed; Abu Dujanah leaned over the Prophet, absorbing dozens of arrows in his back; Nusaybah bint Ka'b (Umm Umarah) fought fiercely with sword and bow, slaying pagan cavalry. Utbah ibn Abi Waqqas threw a stone that cut the Prophet's lower lip and broke his tooth; Ibn Qami'ah struck his helmet, driving two iron rings of the visor into his cheek, crying: "Take that, I am Ibn Qami'ah!" The Prophet wiped blood from his face, saying with profound grief: "How can a people prosper who drench their Prophet's face with blood while he summons them to their Lord?" Yet he immediately supplicated: "O Allah! Forgive my people, for they know not!"

The Prophet's beloved uncle Hamzah was martyred during the battle: Wahshi ibn Harb, an Ethiopian slave promised freedom by Hind bint Utbah, stalked Hamzah from behind a rock and hurled his spear with deadly precision into Hamzah's abdomen. After the battle, Hind and pagan women mutilated the Muslim corpses, Hind slicing open Hamzah's chest and attempting to chew his liver in savage vengeance. Standing over the mutilated body of his uncle, the Prophet wept bitterly, pronouncing Hamzah Sayyid ash-Shuhada (The Master of Martyrs).

Seventy noble companions were martyred at Uhud. Yet, despite physical retreat, the Prophet demonstrated magnificent psychological resilience: the very next morning, despite severe wounds and exhaustion, he mobilized the surviving wounded army to pursue Quraysh to Hamra al-Asad (eight miles from Madinah), signaling to Arabia that the believers were unbroken. Surah Ali 'Imran extensively analyzed Uhud: not as a defeat, but as a profound divine crucible (Tamhis) teaching that spiritual discipline, unconditional obedience to leadership, and purification from worldly greed are the non-negotiable prerequisites for divine victory.

CHAPTER 19: Battle of the Trench (Shawwal 5 AH / 627 CE) غزوة الخندق والأحزاب وحفر الخندق ورياح النصر الإلهي

The Battle of the Trench (Khandaq) and Siege Warfare

⏱️ 6 min read

In Shawwal of the fifth year of the Hijrah (February–March 627 CE), the Muslim community in Madinah confronted the most terrifying existential siege in its history: Ghazwat al-Ahzab (The Battle of the Confederates, or the Trench / Khandaq). The crisis was orchestrated by the exiled leaders of the Jewish tribe Banu an-Nadir—chief among them Huyayy ibn Akhtab and Sallam ibn Abi al-Huqayq—who traveled across Arabia bribing and forging a mega-coalition. They united the Quraysh of Makkah, the formidable desert confederacy of Ghatafan, the tribes of Banu Sulaym, Banu Asad, and Fazarah. Together, they amassed an unprecedented armada of ten thousand warriors—an army larger than the entire population of Madinah, including women and children.

Facing imminent annihilation, the Prophet ﷺ assembled his companions for Shura. At this critical hour, Salman al-Farsi (RA), a Persian companion who possessed extensive knowledge of international siege tactics, stepped forward with an ingenious proposal completely foreign to Arabian warfare: "O Messenger of Allah! In Persia, when we were besieged by overwhelming cavalry, we would dig a trench (khandaq) around ourselves." Madinah was naturally fortified on three sides: jagged volcanic lava tracts (Harrat Waqim and Harrat Wabarah) guarded the east and west, while thick date-palm groves and private fortresses protected the south. Only the flat, open northern approach was vulnerable to cavalry invasion.

The Prophet immediately embraced Salman's plan. He divided the northern perimeter into forty-cubit sections, assigning groups of ten companions to each section. For twenty grueling days during a season of bitter winter cold and acute famine, three thousand Muslims excavated a trench approximately five thousand meters long, five to seven meters wide, and deep enough that a horse could not leap across it. The Prophet worked with his own hands, digging with picks, breaking rocks, and carrying baskets of dirt on his back until dirt covered his chest, singing poetry to elevate spirits: "O Allah! There is no good except the good of the Hereafter, so bless the Ansar and the Muhajirun!"

During this agonizing excavation, the companions struck an immense, impenetrable white boulder that shattered their iron picks. They summoned the Prophet, who had tied two stones against his stomach to alleviate the gnawing pangs of starvation. The Prophet struck the rock with his pickaxe; on the first blow, a brilliant flash of light burst forth, illuminating the palaces of Yemen. The Prophet declared: "Allahu Akbar! I have been granted the keys of Yemen!" On the second strike, a second flash illuminated the white palaces of Al-Mada'in (Ctesiphon, capital of the Persian Sasanian Empire), and the Prophet announced: "Allahu Akbar! I have been granted the keys of Persia!" On the third strike, the boulder crumbled into sand, and a flash lit up the palaces of Byzantine Syria, and the Prophet foretold: "Allahu Akbar! I have been given the keys of Rome!" The hypocrites scoffed: "Muhammad promises us the treasures of Caesar and Chosroes while none of us feels safe even to relieve himself!" Yet the believers saw this as the promise of divine destiny.

When the Confederate horde arrived, expecting a traditional open-field clash, they halted in utter bewilderment at the trench. Abu Sufyan exclaimed: "By God, this is a stratagem that the Arabs have never used!" For a month, the ten thousand besiegers surrounded Madinah, shooting arrows and attempting to leap narrow gaps. Then, a catastrophic treason unfolded inside Madinah: Huyayy ibn Akhtab snuck into the fortress of Banu Qurayzah, the last remaining Jewish tribe in Madinah bound by the Charter of Madinah. After intense pressure, Huyayy persuaded Ka'b ibn Asad, chieftain of Qurayzah, to tear up the treaty and join the Confederates, agreeing to attack the defenseless Muslim families and children from the southern rear.

The psychological terror inside Madinah was suffocating. The Holy Quran captures this existential horror in Surah Al-Ahzab: "When they came at you from above you and from below you, and when eyes grew wild and hearts reached to the throats, and you assumed about Allah various thoughts. There the believers were tested and shaken with a severe shaking" (33:10–11). The Prophet stationed small defensive detachments to guard the women and children inside the fortresses of Madinah, turning to fervent supplication: "O Allah, Revealer of the Book, Swift in calling to account, defeat the Confederates! O Allah, defeat them and shake them!"

Divine assistance manifested through psychological warfare and cosmic forces. Nu'aym ibn Mas'ud, an influential chieftain of Ghatafan, secretly embraced Islam and came to the Prophet, saying: "My people do not know of my Islam; command me as you will." The Prophet wisely instructed: "You are only one man among us, so sow discord among them if you can, for war is deceit (Al-Harbu Khud'ah)." Nu'aym went to Banu Qurayzah and convinced them that Quraysh might abandon them, advising them not to fight until they took hostages of Qurayshi noblemen. He then went to Abu Sufyan and warned him that Banu Qurayzah regretted breaking their pact and planned to demand Makkan hostages to hand over to Muhammad. Mutual suspicion and paralysis instantly poisoned the coalition.

Then Allah unleashed His cosmic host: a freezing, torrential arctic gale descended upon the Confederate camps in the dead of night. The howling wind tore down tents, extinguished cooking fires, blew cooking pots away, blinded horses, and filled enemy eyes with stinging sand. In absolute terror and misery, Abu Sufyan stood up and shouted: "O Quraysh! We are not in a lasting abode; our horses are perishing, Banu Qurayzah have betrayed us, and the wind has destroyed our camps! Depart, for I am departing!" Abu Sufyan mounted his tethered camel, barely untying it before galloping away. By dawn, the vast confederacy of ten thousand warriors had vanished into the desert. The Prophet looked out over the empty plain and uttered his historic prophecy: "Now we will raid them, and they will never raid us; we will march upon them!" (Sahih al-Bukhari). The siege of Madinah was broken forever.

CHAPTER 20: Treaty of Al-Hudaybiyyah (Dhu al-Qi'dah 6 AH / 628 CE) صلح الحديبية التاريخي وبيعة الرضوان والفتح المبين

The Treaty of Al-Hudaybiyyah and the Bay'at al-Ridwan

⏱️ 6 min read

In Dhu al-Qi'dah of the sixth year of the Hijrah (March 628 CE), the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ experienced a profound, true vision in his sleep: he saw himself entering the Sacred Sanctuary of Makkah in peace and security alongside his companions, shaving their heads, cutting their hair, and circumambulating the Ka'bah without fear. Eager to fulfill this divine vision and demonstrate peaceful religious pilgrimage, the Prophet summoned his companions. Fourteen hundred believers donned the sacred white pilgrim garments (Ihram), driving seventy garlanded sacrificial camels, and took up no weapons other than travelers' sheathed swords, embarking on the two-hundred-and-fifty-mile journey to perform Umrah (the Lesser Pilgrimage).

When the peaceful procession reached Al-Hudaybiyyah—a plain at the western boundary of the Makkan sacred precinct—the Prophet's she-camel, Al-Qaswa, knelt down and refused to advance. The companions remarked: "Al-Qaswa has grown stubborn!" The Prophet corrected them: "She has not grown stubborn, for that is not in her nature; rather, she has been halted by the One Who halted the Elephant (Abraha's army)." He then declared an extraordinary standard of diplomatic reconciliation: "By the One in Whose hand is my soul, whatever terms Quraysh ask of me today that honor the sacred sanctuaries of Allah, I will grant them!"

Quraysh mobilized their cavalry under Khalid ibn al-Walid, swearing that the Muslims would never enter Makkah by force. The Prophet sent Uthman ibn Affan (RA) as an ambassador to Makkah to reassure Quraysh that the Muslims had come purely as peaceful pilgrims, not as conquerors. Quraysh detained Uthman for prolonged negotiations. When days passed without his return, a terrifying rumor swept the camp: Uthman had been assassinated inside Makkah.

The murder of an official diplomatic ambassador was an unforgivable act of war under international law. Standing beneath an acacia tree (Samurah), the Prophet summoned the fourteen hundred companions to take an absolute oath: they clasped the Prophet's hand and swore the historic Bay'at al-Ridwan (The Pledge of Divine Pleasure)—pledging to fight by the Prophet's side to the death and never to flee. The Prophet placed his own right hand upon his left hand, declaring: "This is for Uthman." Allah immortalized this sacred covenant in Surah Al-Fath: "Indeed, Allah was pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you under the tree..." (48:18).

Fortunately, Uthman returned alive, accompanied by Suhayl ibn Amr, an astute diplomat dispatched by Quraysh to draft a formal peace treaty. Prolonged, tense negotiations ensued. When Ali ibn Abi Talib, acting as scribe, wrote: "Bi-ismi Allahi ar-Rahmani ar-Rahim" (In the Name of Allah, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful), Suhayl objected: "As for Ar-Rahman, we do not know what it is; write rather: 'Bi-ismika Allahumma' (In Your Name, O Allah)." The Prophet agreed. Ali then wrote: "This is what Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah, has agreed upon." Suhayl objected again: "If we accepted that you were the Messenger of Allah, we would not have barred you from the House nor fought you! Write rather: 'Muhammad ibn Abdullah'." When Ali hesitated out of profound reverence to erase the title, the Prophet asked Ali to show him the word, and erased it with his own hand, replacing it with Muhammad ibn Abdullah.

The terms of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah appeared severely one-sided and humiliating to the companions: First, a ten-year non-aggression truce (Hudnah) between both parties; Second, the Muslims must return to Madinah that year without performing Umrah, returning the following year for only three days unarmed; Third, any person who fled from Makkah to Madinah to embrace Islam without his guardian's permission must be extradited back to Makkah, whereas any Muslim who deserted Madinah to Makkah would not be returned; Fourth, Arabian tribes were free to enter into treaty alliances with either party (whereupon Banu Khuza'ah allied with the Prophet, and Banu Bakr allied with Quraysh).

The terms stunned the companions; Umar ibn al-Khattab rushed to Abu Bakr and the Prophet in anguish, asking: "Are we not upon the truth and they upon falsehood? Why should we give concessions in our religion?" The Prophet calmly answered: "I am the Messenger of Allah; I do not disobey Him, and He will never forsake me." When the treaty was signed, the Prophet commanded the companions: "Arise, sacrifice your animals, and shave your heads!" Paralyzed by grief, not a single companion moved, even after the Prophet repeated the command three times.

Distressed, the Prophet entered his tent to his wife Sayyidah Umm Salamah (RA). Umm Salamah, demonstrating peerless feminine wisdom and political acumen, offered brilliant advice: "O Messenger of Allah, do not blame them, for they are carrying immense grief. Go out, speak not a word to anyone, slaughter your own camel publicly, and call your barber to shave your head." The Prophet followed her counsel. When the companions saw the Prophet sacrificing his camel and shaving his head, the reality set in; they sprang to their feet, slaughtered their animals, and shaved one another's heads in tears of relief.

On the journey back to Madinah, the divine verdict was revealed: Surah Al-Fath descended: "Indeed, We have granted you a clear triumph (Fathan Mubina)!" (48:1). Umar asked: "Is this truly a triumph, O Messenger of Allah?" The Prophet replied: "Yes, by the One in Whose hand is my soul, it is indeed a triumph!" Historical analysis proved this divine verdict: the peace of Hudaybiyyah dissolved the psychological and military barriers between Muslims and pagans. For the first time, Arabs met freely, discussed Quranic ideas, observed the moral character of believers, and during the two years of peace that followed, more people entered Islam—including military giants like Khalid ibn al-Walid and Amr ibn al-'As—than in the entire previous eighteen years of the prophetic mission combined.

CHAPTER 21: Royal Letters & Khaybar (Muharram 7 AH / 628 CE) رسائل النبي ﷺ إلى ملوك العالم وفتح حصون خيبر المنيعة

Letters to World Emperors and the Conquest of Khaybar

⏱️ 6 min read

With the signing of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, the southern military frontier with Quraysh was neutralized for ten years. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ immediately utilized this period of strategic calm to universalize the message of Islam far beyond the borders of the Arabian Peninsula. In Muharram 7 AH (late spring 628 CE), the Prophet prepared an international diplomatic campaign directed at the reigning superpower emperors, kings, and regional potentates of the known world. When informed that foreign imperial chanceries did not accept unsealed diplomatic letters, the Prophet had an official silver signet ring engraved with three lines: "Muhammad / Rasul / Allah" (Muhammad the Messenger of Allah), reading upward in reverent humility with Allah at the peak.

The Prophet dispatched senior, articulate companions as royal envoys carrying official letters of invitation to monotheism: Dihyah ibn Khalifah al-Kalbi to Heraclius, Emperor of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire; Abdullah ibn Hudhafah as-Sahmi to Chosroes II (Khusraw Parvez), Shahanshah of the Sasanian Persian Empire; Hatib ibn Abi Balta'ah to Al-Muqawqis (Cyrus), Roman Prefect and Patriarch of Alexandria in Egypt; Amr ibn Umayyah ad-Damri to Ashamah an-Najashi (Negus), Christian King of Aksum in Abyssinia; and envoys to the rulers of Damascus, Bahrain, and Oman.

The reactions of the monarchs varied dramatically, mirroring their spiritual discernment. In Jerusalem, Emperor Heraclius received the letter while celebrating his military victory over Persia. Heraclius summoned Arabian merchants currently in Gaza, led by Abu Sufyan (who was still an active enemy of Islam), and subjected Abu Sufyan to a brilliant, penetrating cross-examination regarding Muhammad's lineage, social status, truthfulness, followers, and core teachings. When Abu Sufyan confessed that Muhammad's followers were growing, that none apostatized out of displeasure with the faith, that he commanded prayer, charity, chastity, and truthfulness, and that he had never broken a promise, Heraclius astutely concluded: "If what you say is true, he will soon conquer the very ground beneath my two feet; had I known I could reach him safely, I would have washed his feet!" Heraclius honored Dihyah with gifts, though political fear of his Roman patricians prevented him from publicly embracing Islam.

In sharp contrast, the arrogant Sasanian Emperor Chosroes II was incensed that the Prophet placed his own name before the Emperor's. Chosroes tore the letter to pieces and commanded his Yemeni governor Badhan to arrest the Prophet. When the Prophet heard of the defilement of the letter, he uttered a prophetic supplication: "May Allah tear apart his empire as he tore my letter!" Within months, Chosroes was overthrown and murdered by his own son Sheroe (Kavad II), and within two decades, the Sasanian Empire dissolved completely. In Alexandria, Al-Muqawqis treated Hatib with honor, sending rich gifts including two noble Egyptian Coptic sisters, Mariyah (who bore the Prophet his son Ibrahim) and Sirin, along with a fine white mule named Duldul. The Negus of Abyssinia placed the letter against his eyes, descended from his throne, embraced Islam at the hands of Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, and sent two ships carrying the remaining Muslim emigrants home to Madinah.

Concurrently, the Prophet addressed the remaining existential security threat in the north: the heavily fortified oasis of Khaybar, located one hundred miles north of Madinah. Khaybar was the military and financial capital of Arabian Jewish tribes, especially the exiled Banu an-Nadir, who had financed the Confederate coalition during Khandaq and continued to conspire with the Bedouin Ghatafan tribe to invade Madinah. In Muharram 7 AH, the Prophet marched to Khaybar with only the fourteen hundred veterans of Hudaybiyyah. Khaybar comprised eight formidable stone citadels (including Na'im, As-Sa'b ibn Mu'adh, Zubayr, and Al-Qamus) manned by ten thousand seasoned fighters protected by high volcanic rock walls.

For several days, Muslim detachments assaulted the fortress of Al-Qamus without success. One evening, the Prophet made a momentous announcement recorded in both Sahihayn: "Tomorrow, I will surely give the standard to a man who loves Allah and His Messenger, and whom Allah and His Messenger love; through his hands Allah will grant victory!" The companions spent the night in eager anticipation, each hoping to be chosen. The next morning, the Prophet called for Ali ibn Abi Talib. Ali was suffering from severe ophthalmia (eye infection) that rendered him nearly blind. The Prophet applied his blessed saliva to Ali's eyes and supplicated; Ali was instantly healed as though he had never felt pain. Bestowing the white banner upon Ali, the Prophet commanded: "Advance steadily until you enter their courtyard, then invite them to Islam; for by Allah, that Allah should guide a single person through you is better for you than possessing a herd of red camels!"

Ali charged the fortress. The champion of Khaybar, Marhab—a giant warrior clad in double armor wielding a three-pronged spear—emerged reciting proud verses of combat: "Khaybar knows well that I am Marhab, fully armed, a seasoned hero of war!" Ali replied with his immortal verse: "I am the one whose mother named him Haydarah (The Fierce Lion), like a forest lion of terrifying visage!" In a lightning duel, Ali parried Marhab's blow and struck Marhab with his sword Dhul-Fiqar, cleaving Marhab's iron helmet and skull down to his teeth. When Ali's shield was knocked from his hand, he picked up a heavy iron gate from the fortress and used it as a shield throughout the assault. Fortress after fortress fell to the believers.

Recognizing defeat, the Jewish leaders sued for peace. The Prophet demonstrated exceptional agricultural and civic pragmatism: instead of expelling the inhabitants, he permitted them to remain on their lands, cultivating their date palms in exchange for half of their annual agricultural yield (an arrangement known in Islamic jurisprudence as Musaqah). While at Khaybar, the Prophet married Sayyidah Safiyyah bint Huyayy (RA), the daughter of the deceased chieftain Huyayy ibn Akhtab, after freeing her, thereby elevating her dignity and reconciling regional animosities. Furthermore, the remaining Abyssinian emigrants—led by Ja'far ibn Abi Talib—and the Yemeni tribe of Al-Ash'ariyyun led by Abu Musa al-Ash'ari arrived in Khaybar. The Prophet embraced Ja'far, kissed him between the eyes, and rejoiced with tears: "I do not know what makes me happier: the victory at Khaybar or the arrival of Ja'far!"

The Bloodless Conquest of Makkah and Universal Amnesty

⏱️ 6 min read

In late 8 AH (late 629 CE), the fragile peace established by the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah was catastrophically shattered by Quraysh and their tribal allies. The treaty explicitly allowed Arabian tribes to enter alliances with either camp: the tribe of Banu Khuza'ah allied with the Prophet, while Banu Bakr allied with Quraysh. An ancient vendetta erupted when Banu Bakr launched a treacherous midnight raid against Khuza'ah at the well of Al-Watir. Khuza'ah fled into the sacred sanctuary (Haram) of Makkah, crying: "We are inside the Sanctuary!" Yet Banu Bakr slaughtered them inside the holy precinct, actively aided and supplied with weapons by prominent Qurayshi leaders including Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl and Safwan ibn Umayyah under the cover of night.

A forty-man delegation of Khuza'ah, led by Amr ibn Salim al-Khuza'i, rushed to Madinah. Standing before the Prophet in the mosque, Amr recited poignant poetry pleading for justice and reminding the Prophet of their sacred alliance. The Prophet's eyes filled with righteous indignation, and he declared: "You are aided, O Amr ibn Salim! May I not be helped if I do not help Banu Khuza'ah!" The Prophet dispatched an ultimatum to Quraysh presenting three options: pay blood money for the slain Khuza'ah victims, dissolve their military alliance with Banu Bakr, or declare the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah null and void. Quraysh foolishly selected the third option.

Realizing their catastrophic blunder, Quraysh dispatched their supreme leader, Abu Sufyan ibn Harb, to Madinah to plead for renewal and extension of the truce. However, Abu Sufyan found all doors closed: the Prophet gave him no answer; Abu Bakr and Umar refused to intercede; and when he sought the help of his daughter Umm Habibah (the Prophet's wife), she rolled up the Prophet's mattress so Abu Sufyan could not sit upon it, telling him: "This is the clean bed of the Messenger of Allah, and you are an impure polytheist!" Abu Sufyan returned to Makkah empty-handed in utter humiliation.

In utmost operational secrecy, the Prophet mobilized the largest army ever assembled in the history of the Hijaz: ten thousand fully equipped warriors comprising Muhajirun, Ansar, and allied tribes (Sulaym, Muzaynah, Ghatafan, and Aslam). The Prophet prayed: "O Allah! Blind the eyes and ears of Quraysh so we may take them by surprise in their land!" On the 10th of Ramadan 8 AH (January 630 CE), the army marched south. At Marr az-Zahran, just four miles outside Makkah, the Prophet ordered every soldier to kindle a separate campfire in the darkness. Ten thousand blazing fires lit up the desert night, casting an overwhelming aura of psychological awe over the mountains.

Abu Sufyan slipped out of Makkah to scout the horizon and was intercepted by the Prophet's uncle, Al-Abbas ibn Abdul Muttalib (who had secretly emigrated and joined the Prophet). Al-Abbas escorted Abu Sufyan into the Prophet's camp. Seeing the vast, disciplined host, Abu Sufyan's arrogance dissolved. The Prophet asked him: "Woe to you, O Abu Sufyan! Has the time not come for you to know that there is no god but Allah?" Abu Sufyan replied: "How gentle, noble, and maintaining of kinship you are! By God, I know that if there were another god with Allah, he would have helped me by now." The Prophet asked: "Has the time not come for you to know that I am the Messenger of Allah?" After a brief hesitation, Abu Sufyan embraced Islam. Recognizing Abu Sufyan's aristocratic pride, the Prophet granted him an extraordinary diplomatic honor: "Whoever enters the house of Abu Sufyan is safe, whoever locks his own front door is safe, and whoever enters the Sacred Mosque is safe!"

On the morning of the 20th of Ramadan 8 AH, the ten thousand soldiers entered Makkah in a disciplined, four-pronged peaceful envelopment: Az-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam commanded the left wing entering from the north; Sa'd ibn Ubadah commanded the Ansar from the west; Abu Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah led the center; and Khalid ibn al-Walid commanded the right wing entering from the southern lower valley (where a minor skirmish erupted when pagan diehards under Ikrimah attacked, resulting in twelve pagans killed before fleeing). The Prophet entered the holy city riding his camel Al-Qaswa, wearing a black turban, his head bowed so low in humble gratitude to Allah that the hair of his beard grazed the wooden pommel of his saddle, continually reciting Surah Al-Fath. There was no arrogance, no triumphalist parade, no sacking of homes, and no slaughter.

The Prophet proceeded directly to the Ka'bah, performed seven circuits of Tawaf on his mount, touching the Black Stone with his staff. Three hundred and sixty idols encircled the House of Ibrahim, fastened with lead. The Prophet held his staff, pointing to each idol one by one while reciting the divine proclamation: "Truth has come, and falsehood has departed. Indeed, falsehood is bound to depart!" (Surah Al-Isra 17:81) and "Truth has come, and falsehood can neither originate anything nor repeat it!" (Surah Saba 34:49). As he pointed his staff, each stone effigy collapsed face down in the dust. He summoned Uthman ibn Talhah, keeper of the Ka'bah key, entered the interior, erased all pagan wall frescoes, and prayed two units of prayer.

Standing in the doorway of the Ka'bah, holding the doorposts, the Prophet looked down upon the entire population of Makkah assembled in the courtyard below—the very oligarchs who had mocked him for forty years, tortured his companions, boycotted his family, slaughtered his beloved uncle Hamzah, and waged three massive wars of annihilation against him. A profound, breathless silence fell over the sanctuary. The Prophet broke the silence, asking: "O assembly of Quraysh! What do you think I am about to do with you?" Remembering his forty years of unblemished virtue, they answered with trembling hope: "Akhun karim, wa-ibnu akhi karim" (A noble brother, and the son of a noble brother!).

At this pinnacle of absolute worldly military triumph, when any ancient or medieval conqueror would have unleashed merciless plunder and mass executions, Prophet Muhammad ﷺ delivered the most magnificent act of universal amnesty and restorative justice in human history: "I say to you as my brother Yusuf (Joseph) said to his brothers: 'No blame will there be upon you today. Allah will forgive you; and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful' (Surah Yusuf 12:92). Go your way, for you are free!" (Idhhabu fa-antum at-tulaqa!).

The Prophet returned the eternal key of the Ka'bah to Uthman ibn Talhah, stating: "Take it, O Banu Talhah, eternally and unbroken; none shall strip it from you except an oppressor." He then commanded Bilal ibn Rabah (RA)—the former Ethiopian slave whom the Qurayshi aristocracy had tortured on their burning sands—to climb atop the roof of the Ka'bah and chant the Adhan. As Bilal's thunderous voice proclaimed: "Allahu Akbar! Allahu Akbar! Ashhadu an la ilaha illa Allah!", the spiritual transformation of Arabia was complete. Within days, thousands of Makkan men and women came forward in tears to pledge allegiance, permanently establishing Makkah as the monotheistic heart of Islamic civilization.

CHAPTER 23: Hunayn, Ta'if & Tabuk (8–9 AH / 630–631 CE) غزوة حنين وثبات النبي ﷺ وحصار الطائف وغزوة تبوك العسرة

Hunayn, the Siege of Ta'if, and the Hardship Expedition to Tabuk

⏱️ 6 min read

The bloodless conquest of Makkah stunned the Arabian Peninsula; however, the proud, warlike confederacy of Hawazin and Thaqif—who inhabited the mountainous plateau surrounding Ta'if—refused to submit to Islam. Led by their ambitious thirty-year-old commander Malik ibn Awf an-Nasri, Hawazin mobilized twenty thousand warriors. In an eccentric tactical maneuver, Malik commanded his soldiers to bring all their women, children, sheep, and camels directly behind the battle lines, arguing that no soldier would flee while his family and possessions stood behind him.

In Shawwal 8 AH (scarcely four weeks after entering Makkah), the Prophet marched toward the valley of Hunayn with twelve thousand warriors—ten thousand veteran companions joined by two thousand newly converted Makkan warriors (Tulaqa). Seeing their vast numbers, some young soldiers proudly remarked: "Lan nughlaba al-yawma min qillah!" (We will never be defeated today on account of small numbers!). This subtle reliance on numerical superiority rather than divine assistance was immediately corrected by divine decree.

Malik ibn Awf had secretly deployed his elite archers inside the narrow, twisting defiles and rocky gorges of the valley of Hunayn before dawn. As the Muslim vanguard advanced unsuspectingly into the gorge in the morning twilight, a blinding blizzard of arrows rained down upon them from every mountain crevice. Panicked by the sudden ambush, horses reared, camel lines collapsed, and the Muslim vanguard broke ranks in a frantic retreat, sweeping backward and throwing the entire twelve-thousand-man army into chaotic flight.

At this catastrophic juncture of near-total rout, Prophet Muhammad ﷺ displayed superhuman valor and majestic composure. While the entire army retreated around him, the Prophet stood completely exposed on his white mule (Duldul), intentionally spurring it forward into the shower of arrows toward the enemy lines! His uncle Al-Abbas and Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith desperately held the mule's reins to restrain him. The Prophet raised his voice like a roaring lion across the canyon: "Ana an-Nabiyyu la kadhib! Ana ibnu Abdil-Muttalib!" (I am the Prophet, without a lie! I am the son of Abdul Muttalib!).

The Prophet commanded his uncle Al-Abbas, who possessed a voice of thunderous volume: "Call out: O companions of the Samurah tree (Bay'at al-Ridwan)! O companions of Surah Al-Baqarah!" Al-Abbas shouted across the valley. The call electrified the hearts of the true believers: companions who could not turn their panicked camels leaped off, drew their swords, and ran on foot toward the Prophet's voice, shouting: "Labbayk! Labbayk!" (Here we are at your service!). Within minutes, a phalanx of one hundred elite companions gathered around the Prophet, engaging Hawazin in ferocious hand-to-hand combat. The Prophet picked up a handful of dirt, cast it into the faces of Hawazin, saying: "Shahat al-wujuh!", and an overwhelming rout of Hawazin ensued. Malik ibn Awf fled, abandoning twenty-four thousand camels, forty thousand sheep, four thousand ounces of silver, and six thousand women and children as war spoils.

The Prophet pursued the remnants of Thaqif to Ta'if, besieging their fortified city for several weeks, utilizing catapults and siege engines. When the siege caused hardship without strategic necessity, the Prophet demonstrated deep pastoral wisdom by lifting the siege and praying for Thaqif: "O Allah, guide the people of Thaqif and bring them [to Islam]!" Within a year, Thaqif voluntarily accepted Islam without bloodshed. Returning to Al-Ji'ranah, Hawazin sent a repentant delegation embracing Islam; the Prophet immediately emancipated all six thousand captives and returned their families. In distributing the material spoils, the Prophet gave generous portions to the newly converted Makkan chieftains (Al-Mu'allafatu Qulubuhum) to reconcile their hearts.

When some young Ansar whispered among themselves in disappointment that they had received no camels, the Prophet convened an exclusive assembly of the Ansar and delivered an emotional sermon that brought them to sobbing tears: "O assembly of the Ansar! Did I not find you astray and Allah guided you through me? Divided and Allah united you through me? In poverty and Allah enriched you through me?... Are you not pleased, O Ansar, that the people return home with sheep and camels, while you return home to your dwellings with the Messenger of Allah in your midst? By the One in Whose hand is my soul, if all people took one path through the valley and the Ansar took another, I would walk the path of the Ansar! May Allah have mercy on the Ansar, the sons of the Ansar, and the grandsons of the Ansar!" The Ansar wept until their beards were soaked, crying: "We are well pleased with the Messenger of Allah as our portion and reward!"

In Rajab 9 AH (October 630 CE), the most demanding military expedition in prophetic history was launched: Ghazwat Tabuk (The Hardship Expedition / Jaysh al-'Usrah). Intelligence arrived that the Byzantine Emperor Heraclius had massed a massive imperial army and Arab Christian confederates (Ghassanids) on the northern Syrian frontier to crush Madinah. The expedition coincided with intense summer heat, extreme water scarcity, and the ripening of date harvests in Madinah. The hypocrites discouraged mobilization, scoffing: "Do not march forth in the heat!" to which Allah responded: "Say: The fire of Hell is more intense in heat, if they only understood" (Surah At-Tawbah 9:81).

The Prophet called for universal sacrificial spending: Abu Bakr donated one hundred percent of his worldly possessions, saying: "I have left for my family Allah and His Messenger"; Umar donated fifty percent of his wealth; and Uthman ibn Affan equipped one-third of the entire army from his personal pocket, providing three hundred fully outfitted camels and one thousand gold dinars, causing the Prophet to declare: "Nothing Uthman does after today will ever harm him!" Thirty thousand believers marched five hundred miles across the scorching northern desert to Tabuk. Stunned by the audacious mobilization of thirty thousand Muslims, the Byzantine imperial legions dissolved into their garrisons without fighting. The Prophet signed peaceful security and non-aggression treaties with Christian and Jewish border principalities (including John ibn Ru'bah, prince of Ayla, and the rulers of Jarba and Adhruh), solidifying the northern sovereign frontier of the Islamic state.

CHAPTER 24: Delegations & Farewell Hajj (9–10 AH / 630–632 CE) عام الوفود وحجة الوداع وخطبة عرفات وميثاق حقوق الإنسان الخالد

The Year of Delegations and the Farewell Pilgrimage (Hajjat al-Wada')

⏱️ 6 min read

Following the triumphant return from Tabuk and the voluntary submission of Thaqif, the prestige of the Islamic state reached its zenith. The ninth and tenth years of the Hijrah (630–632 CE) are designated across historical chronicles as 'Am al-Wufud (The Year of Delegations). Over seventy official tribal embassies—representing clans from every corner of the Arabian Peninsula, including Najd, Yemen, Hadramawt, Yamamah, Oman, and Bahrain—streamed into Madinah to meet the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, declare their allegiance, and embrace the faith of Islam.

Among the prominent delegations were Banu Tamim, Banu Hanifah (whose deceitful member Musaylimah would later claim false prophethood), the Christian embassy of Najran (with whom the Prophet concluded a historic treaty guaranteeing total freedom of worship and ecclesiastical property protection), and the delegation of Tayy led by Adi ibn Hatim at-Ta'i, son of the legendarily generous pre-Islamic chieftain Hatim at-Ta'i. During this period, Allah revealed Surah Al-Hujurat, establishing refined spiritual manners, prohibiting mocking other peoples, condemning racial chauvinism, and forbidding spying, backbiting, and suspicion: "O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" (49:13).

In Dhu al-Qi'dah 10 AH (February 632 CE), the Prophet announced his intention to perform his sole complete pilgrimage in Islam: Hajjat al-Wada' (The Farewell Pilgrimage). The announcement reverberated across Arabia; over one hundred and twenty-four thousand believers gathered in Madinah and along the pilgrimage route to perform Hajj with their beloved Messenger. On the 25th of Dhu al-Qi'dah, the Prophet donned his two-piece white unstitched Ihram at Dhul-Hulayfah, commencing the immortal chant of monotheism that echoed through the desert valleys: "Labbayka Allahumma Labbayk! Labbayka la sharika laka Labbayk! Inna al-hamda wan-ni'mata laka wal-mulk, la sharika lak!" (Here I am at Your service, O Allah, here I am! You have no partner, here I am! Truly all praise, favor, and sovereignty belong to You, You have no partner!).

The Prophet meticulously performed every rite of Hajj, saying to the multitude: "Khudhu 'anni manasikakum" (Learn your pilgrimage rites from me, for I know not whether I will perform Hajj after this year of mine). On Friday, the 9th of Dhu al-Hijjah 10 AH (March 6, 632 CE)—the Day of Arafah—the Prophet rode his camel Al-Qaswa to the valley of Uranah on the plains of Mount Arafat. Before a vast congregation of 124,000 pilgrims standing under the open sky, the Prophet delivered his epochal Farewell Sermon (Khutbat al-Wada'), recognized universally by legal scholars as the Magna Carta of Islamic civilization and the premier universal charter of human rights.

In his sermon, repeated sentence by sentence across the plains by Rabiah ibn Umayyah who had a booming voice, the Prophet proclaimed: "O people! Listen to my words, for I do not know whether I will ever meet you again in this place after this year. O people! Indeed your lives, your honor, and your property are sacred and inviolable to one another until you meet your Lord, just as this day of yours, this month of yours, and this city of yours is sacred! Behold! Every custom and practice of Jahiliyyah is trampled under my feet! The blood revenges of the Age of Ignorance are abolished, and the first blood revenge I abolish is that of Ibn Rabi'ah ibn al-Harith. All usury (riba) of Jahiliyyah is canceled, and the first usury I cancel is that of my uncle, Al-Abbas ibn Abdul Muttalib; it is all waived entirely!"

He continued with a monumental charter for women's rights and marital dignity: "Fear Allah concerning women! For you have taken them as a trust from Allah, and their intimacy has been made lawful to you by the word of Allah. They have rights over you: that you provide for them and clothe them with goodness." He demolished racial superiority, tribal nationalism, and ethnic supremacy forever: "O mankind! Your Lord is One, and your father is one. All of you descend from Adam, and Adam was created from dust. Behold! An Arab possesses no superiority over a non-Arab, nor does a non-Arab possess superiority over an Arab; nor does a white person have superiority over a black person, nor a black person over a white person, except by piety and righteous action (Taqwa). Have I delivered the message?"

The 124,000 pilgrims roared with one unified voice that shook the valley: "Yes! We bear witness that you have conveyed the message, fulfilled the trust, and counseled the Ummah!" The Prophet raised his index finger toward the heavens, then lowered it toward the people, saying three times: "Allahumma ish-had! Allahumma ish-had! Allahumma ish-had!" (O Allah, bear witness! O Allah, bear witness! O Allah, bear witness!). Shortly after this sermon, on that very afternoon at Arafat, Allah descended the crowning theological seal upon the Holy Quran: "Today I have perfected for you your religion and completed My favor upon you and have approved for you Islam as your religion" (Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:3). Abu Bakr wept upon hearing the verse, realizing that when a mission is perfected, the messenger's departure is imminent.

CHAPTER 25: Passing & Eternal Legacy (Rabi al-Awwal 11 AH / 632 CE) الرفيق الأعلى ووفاة النبي ﷺ ووداع الأمة والخلود الرسالي

The Passing to the Highest Companion and the Eternal Legacy

⏱️ 6 min read

Shortly after returning to Madinah from the Farewell Pilgrimage in early 11 AH, signs of the Prophet's impending departure began to unfold. In the month of Safar 11 AH, he visited the martyrs of Uhud, praying over them as one bidding farewell to the living and the dead. In the dead of night, accompanied by his freedman Abu Muwayhibah, he visited the cemetery of Al-Baqi', supplicating for the deceased and stating: "Peace be upon you, O dwellers of the graves! Rejoice in what you are in, compared to what the people are experiencing... Indeed, we will soon join you." Shortly thereafter, on his return, an intense, searing fever seized him, accompanied by severe head pain.

As the illness grew severe, the Prophet requested permission from all his wives to spend his nursing period in the room of Sayyidah Aisha bint Abi Bakr (RA). Leaning upon his cousin Al-Fadl ibn al-Abbas and Ali ibn Abi Talib, with his feet dragging along the ground from weakness, he entered Aisha's chamber. Despite debilitating fever, he continued to lead the congregational prayers in the mosque until three days before his passing. When he became too weak to stand, he commanded: "Order Abu Bakr to lead the people in prayer." Aisha attempted to suggest Umar, fearing the emotional burden upon her father, but the Prophet insisted with firm resolve: "Order Abu Bakr to lead the people in prayer!" Abu Bakr led seventeen congregational prayers during the Prophet's illness, establishing his religious precedence before the Ummah.

Five days before his demise, the Prophet had seven leather skins of cool water poured over him to alleviate the fever, then emerged into the mosque with a cloth bound around his head. Seated upon the minbar for the last time, he addressed the weeping believers: "Behold! A servant was offered by Allah the choice between the splendor of this world and that which is with Him, and he chose that which is with Allah!" Hearing this, Abu Bakr wept copiously, shouting: "Nay, we would ransom you with our fathers and mothers!" The Prophet comforted him: "Do not weep, Abu Bakr. Indeed, the one who has shown the greatest generosity to me in his companionship and wealth is Abu Bakr. If I were to take an intimate friend (Khalil) from among the inhabitants of earth, I would take Abu Bakr as a Khalil; but Islamic brotherhood and love suffice. Let no private door opening into the mosque remain, except the door of Abu Bakr."

He also warned against spiritual excess: "Beware of those before you who took the graves of their prophets and righteous men as places of worship! Do not take my grave as an idol to be worshipped; O Allah, do not let my grave be worshipped!" His final advisory words whispered to the community centered upon two supreme trusts: "As-Salah! As-Salah! Wa-ma malakat aymanukum!" (Guard the prayer! Guard the prayer! And treat with kindness those whom your right hands possess / the vulnerable and servants!).

On Monday morning, 12th of Rabi al-Awwal 11 AH (June 8, 632 CE), as the believers were assembled in prayer behind Abu Bakr, the curtain of Aisha's chamber was gently lifted. The Prophet looked out upon his companions standing in straight, harmonious ranks in worship. His blessed face was radiant, as luminous as a page of the Quran (ka-annahu waraqatu mushaf). A serene, joyful smile spread across his face, rejoicing that his life's mission had established a community devoted entirely to Allah. Abu Bakr stepped backward to let the Prophet lead, but the Prophet gently gestured for them to continue, then let the curtain fall. That was the last time the companions beheld his radiant face in this earthly life.

Later that morning, the Prophet rested his head upon the chest of Sayyidah Aisha. Her brother Abdur-Rahman entered carrying a fresh Siwak (tooth-stick). Aisha noticed the Prophet gazing at it with desire; she took it, chewed and softened it with her own saliva, and handed it to the Prophet, who brushed his teeth with vigorous purity. Next to him stood a leather vessel of water; he dipped his blessed hands in it, wiped his face, and murmured: "La ilaha illa Allah, inna lil-mawti la-sakarāt" (There is no god but Allah; truly death has its agonies).

Then he raised his right hand toward the ceiling, his gaze fixed upon the celestial heights, and whispered his final sublime words in human speech: "Ma'a alladhina an'amta 'alayhim min an-nabiyyina was-siddiqina wash-shuhada'i was-salihin... Allahumma ar-Rafiq al-A'la! Allahumma ar-Rafiq al-A'la! Allahumma ar-Rafiq al-A'la!" (With those upon whom You have bestowed favor among the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous... O Allah, with the Highest Companion! O Allah, with the Highest Companion! O Allah, with the Highest Companion!). His hand gently relaxed, his eyes rested, and his pure soul departed to the presence of the Lord of the Worlds. He was sixty-three years of age.

A wave of profound shock and paralyzing grief engulfed Madinah. Men wept openly in the streets; Uthman was struck dumb; Ali was overcome with sorrow; and Umar ibn al-Khattab, brandishing his unsheathed sword, shouted in denial: "Some hypocrites claim that the Messenger of Allah has died! By God, he has not died, but has gone to his Lord as Musa went, and he will return and cut off the hands and feet of those who said he died!"

At this perilous moment of collective breakdown, Abu Bakr as-Siddiq arrived from his home in As-Sunh. He entered Aisha's chamber, unveiled the Prophet's face, kissed his forehead, and wept: "May my father and mother be ransomed for you! You were pure in life and pure in death. The death that Allah decreed for you has come." Abu Bakr walked out into the courtyard of the mosque, where Umar was still shouting to the crowd, and said: "Sit down, O Umar!" When Umar refused, the people turned toward Abu Bakr.

Abu Bakr praised Allah and delivered the definitive theological anchor of the Islamic faith: "Amma ba'd: Whoever among you worshipped Muhammad, know that Muhammad has died! But whoever among you worships Allah, know that Allah is Ever-Living and never dies!" He then recited the revealed Quranic verse: "Muhammad is not but a messenger. Other messengers have passed on before him. So if he was to die or be killed, would you turn back on your heels [to disbelief]? And he who turns back on his heels will never harm Allah at all; but Allah will reward the grateful" (Surah Ali 'Imran 3:144). It was as though the people had never heard this verse until Abu Bakr recited it; Umar fell to his knees, his legs unable to carry him, realizing that the Messenger of Allah had truly departed.

The Prophet was washed by his family (Ali, Al-Abbas, Al-Fadl, Qutham, Usamah ibn Zayd, and Shuqran) while remaining clad in his garment, and shrouded in three white cotton Yemeni sheets. Abu Bakr informed them: "I heard the Messenger of Allah say: 'No prophet dies except that he is buried where he passes away.'" Abu Talhah al-Ansari dug a Lahd grave beneath Aisha's bed. The companions entered the room in small groups—men, then women, then children—praying Janazah over him without a single Imam, in reverence to his peerless status. He was laid to rest on Wednesday night.

Prophet Muhammad ﷺ departed this world leaving behind neither gold dinars nor silver dirhams, neither palaces nor landed estates—leaving only his white mule, his weapons, and a plot of land dedicated as perpetual charity for travelers. Yet he left behind an eternal, indestructible legacy: the Holy Quran preserved letter by letter, the living Sunnah of prophetic character, a transformed continent liberated from polytheism and tribal barbarism, and a universal ethical civilization founded upon absolute monotheism, justice, and mercy to all creation. As the Quran eternally testifies: "And We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds" (21:107). May the peace, blessings, and salutations of Allah be upon him, his noble family, his righteous companions, and all who follow his luminous path until the Day of Judgment.
